Kids & Money: Teaching, Saving and Protecting Them

How to teach money at each age, which account fits which goal (529 plans, the new Trump accounts, custodial Roth IRAs and custodial accounts), how much to save for college, and the paperwork that protects your kids if something happens to you. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

The most valuable thing you can give a child is habits: an allowance or pay to manage, a bank account by the preteen years, a first paycheck in high school. For money you set aside, match the account to the goal. A 529 plan is best for education (tax-free growth; up to $20,000 a year for K–12 tuition from 2026). A custodial Roth IRA is the best long-term account once a teen has earned income. The new Trump account is worth opening if your child was born 2025–2028 and qualifies for the free $1,000 deposit, or if your employer contributes; beyond that it's usually a second choice, because earnings are taxed on the way out and the money is locked until 18. A custodial (UTMA/UGMA) account is flexible but becomes the child's outright at 18–21. College averages $31,000 a year all-in at an in-state public school (2025–26), so most families aim to cover part from savings, not all. And if anyone depends on your income: write a will that names a guardian, buy term life insurance, and don't leave money directly to a minor.

What kids cost, and the tax help

Raising a child to 18 costs about $303,000 on average in the U.S., according to LendingTree's 2026 estimate, and child care is usually the largest single line in the early years. Your own finances come first: the Roadmap's order (emergency fund, employer match, high-interest debt, your own retirement) doesn't change when kids arrive. Your child can borrow for college; you can't borrow for retirement. The tax code offers some help:

Habits beat balances

Money lessons by age

Kids learn money the way they learn anything else: by handling it, making small mistakes while the stakes are low, and watching what you do. Thirty states now require a standalone personal finance course to graduate from high school, but home is still where the habits form.

Allowance, no strings

A budget to practice on

A set amount each week, separate from chores. The point is practice making choices; don't rescue them when it's gone.

Paid for extra work

Earning it

Everyday chores are part of family life; extra jobs (washing the car, yard work) earn money. Connects effort and income.

A mix

What many families settle on

A small base allowance plus paid extra jobs. Whatever you choose, be consistent, and let the money be theirs to spend or save.

New in 2026

Trump accounts, in plain language

Created by the 2025 tax law, Trump accounts are a kind of IRA for children under 18. Families could start adding money on July 4, 2026, and more than 4 million children had been signed up by September. The details:

$1,000

The free deposit

The Treasury deposits a one-time $1,000 for U.S. citizen children born January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2028 who have a Social Security number. It isn't automatic: a parent or guardian elects it on IRS Form 4547 (it can go in with your tax return), online at trumpaccounts.gov, or through Social Security's birth registration at the hospital. Separately, the Michael & Susan Dell Foundation pledged up to $250 each for about 25 million children age 10 and under in lower-income ZIP codes.

ADDING

Contributions

Parents, relatives and others can add up to $5,000 a year per child. Employers can contribute up to $2,500 a year per employee, tax-free to you, counted inside the $5,000. Contributions from governments and charities don't count toward the limit. Parent contributions aren't tax-deductible.

INVESTED

Only low-cost U.S. stock index funds

Until the year the child turns 18, the money must be in mutual funds or ETFs that track a U.S. stock index such as the S&P 500, with fees of 0.10% a year or less. That's close to the honest baseline: cheap and diversified, but 100% stocks, with no option to shift to bonds or cash as a goal gets close.

AT 18

Getting the money out

Generally no withdrawals before 18. After that, the account follows traditional-IRA rules: the parent's after-tax contributions come back tax-free, but the $1,000 deposit, employer money and all the growth are taxed as ordinary income, with a 10% penalty before 59½ unless an exception applies (such as college costs or up to $10,000 toward a first home). Left alone, it's a retirement account with a 40-year head start.

Clearly worth it

Claiming the $1,000 and employer money

Free money in a low-cost index fund. File Form 4547 or sign up online if your child was born 2025–2028.

It depends

Adding your own money

Better than a custodial account for long-term money: it grows tax-deferred with no kiddie tax, and at 18 it doesn't simply become spending money, since withdrawals are taxed and usually penalized. For college, a 529 is better; for a teen with a job, a Roth IRA is better.

Not a fit

Money you'll need before 18

It's locked until then, and it's all in stocks. Use a 529 or savings for anything with an earlier date.

Time is the gift

The head start: why early dollars matter

A dollar invested at birth has 65 years to compound before a traditional retirement age. That's the real power of a Trump account or a custodial Roth: not the tax rules, but the time. Try your own numbers; results are in today's dollars, after inflation.

The big one

Saving for college with a 529

College prices vary more than almost anything else families buy. In 2025–26, an average year at a public four-year school cost $25,850 for in-state tuition, housing and food (about $31,000 with books, transportation and other costs), against about $65,500 all-in at a private nonprofit college. But most students don't pay the sticker price: after grants, average net tuition for in-state public students was about $2,300.

Most 529 plans offer age-based portfolios that shift toward bonds and cash as college approaches, which usually means lower returns in the last few years.

A common rule of thumb is to plan on covering college about one-third each from savings, from income during the college years, and from aid and modest loans. At the defaults (age 3, half of an in-state public budget), $250 a month plus $5,000 already saved covers about 83% of the goal, and about $310 a month would cover all of it. Time does much of the work: covering the same half of an in-state budget from zero takes about $290 a month starting at birth, but about $670 a month starting at age 10.

STATE

Start with your own state's plan

Over 30 states give a tax deduction or credit, usually only for their own plan, and some give it for any plan. If yours doesn't, you can pick any state's plan; compare fees. Direct-sold plans (bought from the plan, not through an advisor) are usually cheapest.

NEW USES

More than college now

From 2026, up to $20,000 a year for K–12 tuition, plus K–12 books, tutoring and test fees; since July 2025, trade and professional credentials (welding, plumbing, CPA exams). Some states haven't adopted these uses for state tax purposes, so check before withdrawing.

LEFTOVERS

If your child doesn't need it all

Change the beneficiary to a sibling, cousin or yourself; withdraw up to the amount of any scholarship without the 10% penalty (tax on earnings still applies); or roll up to $35,000 over the child's lifetime into their Roth IRA, if the 529 has been open 15 years, within the yearly IRA limit.

GRANDPARENTS

Grandparents can help, invisibly

Since the FAFSA simplification, a grandparent-owned 529 isn't reported and its withdrawals no longer count as student income. Grandparents can give $19,000 a year per grandchild ($38,000 for a couple) without gift-tax paperwork, or front-load five years at once. The 2027–28 FAFSA opened September 23, 2026.

Teens

A teen's first paycheck and a Roth IRA

A teenager with a summer job is in the best tax position they'll ever have: in 2026 they can earn up to the standard deduction of $16,100 and owe no federal income tax. Money they put in a Roth IRA now is taxed at 0% going in and 0% coming out in retirement.

RULES

How a custodial Roth IRA works

The contribution can't exceed the teen's earned income for the year (wages, or real self-employment like babysitting or lawn mowing with records), up to $7,500. A parent or grandparent can fund it as a gift, so the teen can keep and spend the paycheck while you "match" it into the Roth. Most major brokerages offer custodial Roths with no minimums; the teen takes control at 18–21 depending on the state.

EXAMPLE

One summer's worth

A 16-year-old earns $4,000 lifeguarding. You put $4,000 in their Roth IRA. At a 5% return after inflation, that single deposit grows to about $62,000 in today's dollars by age 72, tax-free. Do it for three summers and it's about $175,000.

FLEXIBLE

Not as locked up as it sounds

Roth contributions (not the growth) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, which makes a Roth a reasonable backstop for a first car or a college semester. Better to leave it alone, though.

If something happens to you

Protecting them: guardians, insurance, beneficiaries

The most important financial documents for parents aren't investment accounts. They're the ones that decide who raises your children and who manages their money if you can't.

WILL

Name a guardian in a will

Without one, a court decides who raises your children. Name a guardian and a backup, talk to them first, and consider naming a different person to manage the money if that suits your family. Update it after each birth or big change. Both parents need their own will.

INSURANCE

Term life and disability insurance

If anyone depends on your income, term life insurance is usually the cheapest way to replace it until the kids are grown: often 10–12 times income, for a term that lasts until the youngest is independent. A stay-at-home parent needs coverage too, since replacing child care is expensive. Disability insurance protects the paycheck while you're alive.

BENEFICIARIES

Don't name a minor directly

Life insurance companies and retirement plans can't pay a child under 18 directly; a court would appoint someone to manage the money, and the child would get it all at 18. Instead, name a trust for the children, or a custodian under your state's UTMA law. Beneficiary forms override your will, so check them after every life change.

SOCIAL SECURITY

A benefit people forget

If a working parent dies, children under 18 (19 if still in high school) can receive Social Security survivor benefits, often a sizable monthly amount. It doesn't replace life insurance, but it's part of the picture (see Social Security & Medicare).

Do this

A family money checklist

Get your child a Social Security number

Usually requested at the hospital. It's needed for the child tax credit, a Trump account, a 529 and any bank account.

Claim the Trump account $1,000 if eligible

Children born 2025–2028: file Form 4547 or sign up online. Ask HR whether your employer contributes.

Write wills that name a guardian

Both parents, with a backup guardian. Update after each new child.

Buy term life insurance and fix beneficiaries

Enough to cover the years until the youngest is independent. Name a trust or UTMA custodian, not a minor.

Use the dependent care FSA at open enrollment

Up to $7,500 of child care with pre-tax pay in 2026.

Open a 529 and automate a monthly amount

Check your state's deduction first. Share the gifting link with grandparents for birthdays.

Give them money to manage

An allowance or paid jobs, save/spend/give jars, then a bank account and debit card as they grow.

Match a teen's first paycheck into a Roth IRA

Up to what they earned, and up to $7,500 in 2026.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Should I save for my kids' college or my own retirement first?

Your retirement, at least up to the employer match and ideally your full plan. Students can borrow, work and get grants for college; nobody lends you money to retire. A secure retirement is also a gift to your kids, who won't have to support you.

What if my child doesn't go to college?

A 529 still has options: trade school and credentials qualify, you can switch it to a sibling or yourself, or roll up to $35,000 into the child's Roth IRA. As a last resort, withdrawing pays income tax plus 10% on the earnings only, not on what you put in.

Is a Trump account better than a 529?

For education, no: 529 withdrawals for school are tax-free, while Trump account earnings are taxed as income. A Trump account's strength is the free $1,000, employer contributions, and a long-term stock account that can't be spent before 18. Many families will use both.

Can grandparents put money in a Trump account or 529?

Yes, both. For a 529, grandparent-owned accounts are now invisible to the FAFSA. For a Trump account, anyone can contribute within the $5,000 yearly limit.

When should a child get a debit or credit card?

A parent-controlled debit card or app works from about age 10 to 12. Adding a responsible teen as an authorized user on your credit card starts their credit history; make sure the card company reports authorized users and that you pay in full.

Should I tell my kids how much we earn or have?

Share in a way that fits their age. Young kids need to know that money is limited and that choices matter. Teens benefit from seeing a real budget, what college will actually cost the family, and what you expect them to contribute.

Glossary

529 plan
A state-sponsored education savings account with tax-free growth for qualified education costs.
Trump account
A type of IRA for children under 18, created in 2025, invested in U.S. stock index funds.
Pilot contribution
The one-time $1,000 Treasury deposit for eligible children born 2025–2028.
Custodial Roth IRA
A Roth IRA for a minor with earned income, managed by an adult until 18–21.
UTMA / UGMA
State laws for custodial accounts that hold an irrevocable gift for a child until 18–21.
Custodian
The adult who manages a custodial account for the child's benefit.
Kiddie tax
Taxing a child's investment income above $2,700 (2026) at the parents' rate.
Earned income
Pay from work, as opposed to interest, dividends or gifts.
FAFSA
The Free Application for Federal Student Aid.
Student Aid Index (SAI)
The FAFSA's measure of what a family can pay, used to award aid.
Net price
What college costs after grants and scholarships, as opposed to the sticker price.
Superfunding
Putting five years of gift-tax exclusions into a 529 at once.
Gift-tax exclusion
What one person can give another each year without filing a gift-tax return: $19,000 in 2026.
Guardian
The person named to raise your minor children if you can't.
Dependent care FSA
An employer account for paying child care with pre-tax pay.
Child tax credit
A tax credit of up to $2,200 per child under 17 in 2026.
