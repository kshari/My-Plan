Credit & Debt: Scores, Payoff Plans and Student Loans

How credit scores and reports actually work, how to get out of debt faster (avalanche or snowball, and when balance transfers or counseling help), what changed for student loans in 2026, and how to decide between paying off debt and investing. Two calculators use your own balances.

TL;DR

Debt isn't good or bad by label; it's expensive or cheap, and it either builds something or doesn't. Credit cards are the expensive kind: banks charged about 22% on balances carrying interest in May 2026, and Americans owe $1.26 trillion on them. Paying only the minimums can turn a few thousand dollars into a decade of payments. Pick one payoff order and stick to it: avalanche (highest rate first) costs the least; snowball (smallest balance first) gives quicker wins and usually costs only a little more. Your credit score mostly comes down to paying on time and keeping card balances low relative to limits, and your reports are free every week at AnnualCreditReport.com. For student loans, July 2026 brought a new income-based plan (RAP), new borrowing caps and higher rates, and borrowers who sign up for autopay by September 30, 2026 get a 1-point rate cut through mid-2028. Whether to pay down debt or invest comes down to the rate: above about 8%, pay it off; below about 5%, invest (after the employer match); in between, splitting is reasonable.

What debt really costs

The useful way to sort debt isn't "good" versus "bad." It's the interest rate, and whether the debt bought something that lasts or grows (a home, an education, a reliable car to get to work) or something that's already gone. A 22% credit card balance for last year's vacation is the most expensive money most people ever borrow.

The number lenders see

Your credit score: what moves it

A credit score is a three-digit guess at how likely you are to pay late, calculated from your credit reports. It sets the rate on your mortgage, car loan and cards, and can affect insurance premiums and apartment applications. Most scores run from 300 to 850.

Factor (FICO's weights)ShareWhat helps
Payment history35%Pay every bill on time. One payment 30+ days late can drop a good score sharply and stays on your report for seven years. Set up autopay for at least the minimum.
Amounts owed (utilization)30%Keep card balances low relative to limits: under 30% of each card's limit and overall, lower is better. Paying before the statement date lowers the balance that's reported.
Length of history15%Keep your oldest cards open (use them occasionally so they aren't closed).
New credit10%Apply only when you need to. Several mortgage or auto inquiries within a short shopping window count as one.
Credit mix10%Having both cards and installment loans helps a little. Never borrow just to improve your mix.

Two scoring families exist: FICO and VantageScore. As of September 2026, lenders selling mortgages to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac can use either classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0. Some newer models also count rent payments and "buy now, pay later" loans. Your score doesn't include your income, savings or net worth, so a millionaire with no credit history can have a thin file, and a high earner who pays late will have a poor score.

The raw data behind the score

Your credit report: check, freeze, fix

CHECK

Free reports every week

Equifax, Experian and TransUnion each keep a report on you. You can get all three free, every week, at AnnualCreditReport.com, the only official free source. Check for accounts you don't recognize, wrong balances, and late payments you didn't make.

FIX

Dispute errors in writing

Contact both the credit bureau and the company that reported the item. They must investigate, usually within 30 days. Most negative items (late payments, collections) fall off after seven years; a Chapter 7 bankruptcy after ten. Paid medical collections and medical debts under $500 aren't reported by the three bureaus (see Health & Disability).

FREEZE

Freeze your credit; it's free

A freeze stops anyone, including you, from opening new credit in your name until you lift it, online, in minutes. It's free at all three bureaus by federal law and is the single best protection against identity theft. Lift it temporarily when you apply for a loan.

Getting out

A payoff plan: avalanche vs. snowball

Both methods work the same way: pay the minimum on every debt, put every extra dollar on one target, and when it's gone, roll its payment into the next. They differ only in which debt goes first. Avalanche targets the highest interest rate, which saves the most money. Snowball targets the smallest balance, which clears whole accounts sooner and keeps some people motivated. The best method is the one you'll stick with.

Payments freed up by a paid-off debt roll into the next target. If a minimum doesn't cover the interest, that debt never shrinks on its own; the calculator stops at 50 years.

With the default debts, avalanche and snowball both finish in about 46 months; avalanche saves about $130 in interest. The gap grows when high-rate balances are large, but for many households it's small enough that motivation matters more. The bigger win by far is having a plan and an extra amount at all: about $8,700 less interest and six years sooner than minimums only.

A lot changed in 2026

Student loans after the 2026 changes

The 2025 tax and spending law rewrote federal student lending, with most changes starting July 1, 2026. Federal loans still come with protections private loans don't, so the order of options matters.

RAP

A new income-based plan: the Repayment Assistance Plan

For new borrowers, RAP is the only income-driven option: payments of 1%–10% of adjusted gross income, a $10 minimum, $50 less per dependent child, unpaid interest waived, and forgiveness after 30 years. The SAVE plan is ending; existing borrowers in it must choose a new plan, and the older PAYE and ICR plans close by mid-2028. Check your servicer's notices so you aren't moved into a plan by default.

CAPS

New limits and higher rates

Grad PLUS loans are gone for new borrowers. Graduate students can borrow up to $20,500 a year ($100,000 total), professional students $50,000 a year ($200,000 total), and Parent PLUS is capped at $20,000 a year and $65,000 per child. Rates for 2026–27 are 6.52% for undergraduates, 8.07% for graduate loans and 9.07% for PLUS loans.

AUTOPAY

A limited-time 1-point rate cut

Borrowers with Direct Loans made after July 1, 2012 who are enrolled in autopay by September 30, 2026 get a 1 percentage-point rate reduction (up from the usual 0.25) through June 30, 2028. Enroll with your servicer if you haven't.

PSLF

Public Service Loan Forgiveness still exists

Teachers, nurses, government and nonprofit workers can have remaining federal balances forgiven after 120 qualifying payments. Certify your employment regularly. Note that new Parent PLUS loans no longer lead to PSLF.

PRIVATE

Refinancing federal loans into private ones is a one-way door

A lower private rate can make sense for high earners with secure jobs, but you permanently give up income-based plans, forgiveness programs and federal hardship options. Refinance private loans freely; think hard before refinancing federal ones.

The eternal question

Pay off debt or invest?

Paying down a debt earns a guaranteed return equal to its interest rate. Investing earns an expected return that could be higher or lower. So the answer depends mostly on the rate, and the Roadmap's order still applies: starter emergency fund and the full employer match come first.

Above ~8%

Pay it off first

Credit cards, most personal loans, many private student loans. No investment reliably beats a guaranteed 20%.

~5% to 8%

A judgment call; splitting is fine

Newer federal student loans, many car loans, today's new mortgages. Roughly a coin flip against expected stock returns, and paying down debt is risk-free.

Below ~5%

Usually invest

Older low-rate mortgages and loans. Pay the minimum, fill tax-advantaged accounts, and let time do the work.

Extra money each month: to the debt first, or invested from day one?
Net worth from these dollars at the end: investments minus any debt still owed. "Debt first" sends minimum + extra to the debt, then invests the whole amount once it's paid off. "Invest first" pays only the minimum and invests the extra from the start. Investment returns are assumed steady; real returns vary, and debt payoff is guaranteed. Ignores taxes (student loan interest can be partly deductible; investing inside a 401(k) or IRA adds tax benefits).

At the defaults, a 6.5% student loan against a 7% expected return is nearly a tie after ten years, which is exactly why this middle zone is a judgment call. Change the rate to a 23% credit card and debt-first wins clearly; drop the expected return to 5% and it wins too.

A debt-free checklist

List every debt

Balance, rate, minimum and due date, in one place. Most people are surprised by at least one number.

Set autopay for every minimum

A missed payment costs more in credit-score damage and fees than almost anything else.

Build a small cash buffer first

$1,000–$2,000, so a surprise bill doesn't go straight back on a card.

Choose avalanche or snowball, and an extra amount

Run the calculator, pick one, and automate the extra payment to your target.

Call your card issuer and ask for a lower rate

It works more often than people expect, especially with a good payment history.

Pull your three credit reports and freeze them

Free every week at AnnualCreditReport.com; dispute errors; freeze at each bureau.

Student loans: check your plan and autopay

Confirm your repayment plan after the 2026 changes, and enroll in autopay by September 30, 2026 for the 1-point rate cut.

When a debt is gone, redirect the payment

Roll it to the next debt, then to savings and investing, before it disappears into spending.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Should I close a credit card after paying it off?

Usually not, especially an old one with no annual fee: closing it shortens your history and raises your utilization. Put a small recurring charge on it with autopay. Close it if the annual fee isn't worth it or if having it tempts you to spend.

Does checking my own score hurt it?

No. Checking your own score or reports is a "soft" inquiry with no effect. Only applications for new credit ("hard" inquiries) count, and only a little.

Is it OK to carry a small balance to build credit?

No. That's a myth. Paying the full statement balance every month builds credit just as well and costs nothing in interest.

A debt collector called me. What are my rights?

Ask for written validation of the debt, don't pay anything until you've confirmed it's yours and the amount is right, and check whether it's past your state's statute of limitations. Collectors can't harass or threaten you. Scammers pose as collectors, so never pay by gift card or wire.

Is "buy now, pay later" a problem?

Used occasionally for planned purchases, it's fine. The risk is stacking several plans at once and losing track; late fees apply, and some newer credit scores now count these loans.

Should I use savings to pay off my credit card?

Yes, if you'd still keep a small emergency cushion. Money in savings earns maybe 4%; the card costs about 22%. Just don't run the card back up.

Glossary

APR
Annual percentage rate: the yearly cost of borrowing, including some fees.
Minimum payment
The smallest amount due each month to keep an account current.
Credit utilization
Card balances as a share of credit limits.
Credit report
A record of your borrowing and payment history kept by each credit bureau.
Credit score
A number (usually 300–850) predicting how likely you are to pay late.
Hard inquiry
A lender's check when you apply for credit; lowers your score slightly for a while.
Credit freeze
A free block on new credit being opened in your name.
Debt avalanche
Paying extra on the highest-rate debt first.
Debt snowball
Paying extra on the smallest balance first.
Balance transfer
Moving a balance to a different card, often at 0% for a promotional period.
Debt management plan
A nonprofit counselor-arranged repayment plan with reduced card rates.
Debt settlement
Negotiating to pay less than owed, usually after stopping payments. Risky.
RAP
Repayment Assistance Plan: the new federal income-based student loan plan from July 2026.
PSLF
Public Service Loan Forgiveness: forgiveness after 120 qualifying payments while working in public service.
Collections
A debt passed to a collection agency after serious delinquency.
Statute of limitations
The period after which a creditor can no longer sue to collect a debt; varies by state.
