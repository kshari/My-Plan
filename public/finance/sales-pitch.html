Spotting the Sales Pitch & Working with Advisors

How financial advice is paid for, what "fiduciary" and "best interest" actually promise, the seven pitches you'll hear most, and how to find, check and pay an advisor you can trust. This episode ties the whole library together: every product gets the same test.

TL;DR

Most bad financial decisions aren't scams. They're ordinary products sold on a conflicted incentive. So start every conversation with one question: how is this person paid, in dollars, if I say yes? Advice comes in four price tags: commissions, a percentage of your assets (commonly about 1% a year), a flat fee, or hourly. Only some advisors owe you a fiduciary duty; a broker owes a narrower "best interest" standard at the moment of a sale, and an insurance agent a state standard that permits commissions. After a 2026 court ruling, one-time advice to roll a 401(k) into a product often has no federal fiduciary duty at all. The pitches that cost people most repeat the same patterns: upside with no downside, "tax-free," a day-one bonus, guaranteed income "rates," steady distributions, a star manager, and "only until Friday." Each can be answered with the baseline from Investing Basics. Commissions aren't fraud and advice can be worth paying for. Just know the price, verify the person on BrokerCheck, keep your money at an independent custodian, and get a fee-only second opinion before anything you can't easily undo.

Follow the money: four ways advice gets paid

Nobody gives financial advice for free. The question is never whether you pay, but how, and what that payment nudges the advisor to recommend. Almost every arrangement is one of four models, or a mix.

Commission

Paid per product sold

The company behind the product pays the seller when you buy: a share of your premium or investment, sometimes plus ongoing "trails." Common for insurance, annuities and some mutual funds.

Nudges towardselling something, and the higher-paying product.

Assets under management

A % of your balance, every year

Commonly about 1% a year up to $1 million, lower above that, plus the cost of the funds used. Charged whether markets are up or down.

Nudges towardkeeping money with them (and against paying off your mortgage or buying an annuity elsewhere).

Flat fee / retainer

A set price for a plan or a year

A one-time plan (Kitces Research found a median of about $2,400) or an annual retainer. The price doesn't change with your balance or what you buy.

Nudges towardfewer conflicts, though ongoing retainers still want to be renewed.

Hourly

Pay for time, like a lawyer

Useful for a one-off question or a yearly check-up. Kitces Research found a median of about $220 an hour for fee-only planners (the rate has likely risen since).

Nudges towardnothing in particular, but you manage the follow-through yourself.

"Fee-only" isn't the same as "fee-based." Fee-only means the advisor is paid only by you, never by commissions. Fee-based usually means fees and commissions. The words sound alike on purpose. Ask directly: "Will you or your firm receive any payment from anyone other than me if I follow your advice?"

The legal promise

Fiduciary, best interest, suitability: what the words promise

The person across the table may owe you a very different legal duty depending on their license, and some can switch between roles from one recommendation to the next. Here's what each standard actually requires.

WhoStandard they owe youWhat it means in practice
Registered investment adviser (RIA)Fiduciary duty under the Investment Advisers ActA duty of care and loyalty for the whole relationship, not just one sale. Conflicts must be disclosed, and the advice must put you first. Their Form ADV Part 2 brochure explains fees and conflicts.
Broker-dealer representativeRegulation Best Interest (since 2020)A recommendation must be in your best interest when it's made. Conflicts must be disclosed and managed, but commissions and in-house products are allowed, and there's generally no ongoing duty to monitor your account.
Insurance agent selling annuitiesState best-interest rule based on the NAIC model (adopted by 48 states as of 2025)Must act with reasonable care and not put their own financial interest ahead of yours; must disclose the sources and types of their pay, and more detail on request. Commissions are fully allowed.
Insurance agent selling life insuranceVaries by state; often a suitability standardIn most states, the annuity best-interest rule doesn't cover life insurance (New York is a notable exception). "Suitable" is a lower bar than "best."
CFP® professionalCFP Board fiduciary duty when giving financial adviceA certification standard enforced by the CFP Board, not a law, but real. Check the certification and any discipline at cfp.net.
Anyone advising on a 401(k) rolloverOften no federal fiduciary duty, since March 2026A court vacated the Labor Department's 2024 rule that would have made one-time rollover and annuity recommendations fiduciary advice. The older 1975 test applies again, and it rarely covers a single conversation. The Labor Department has said it plans a new rule.

Two practical points. Many advisors are "dually registered": a fiduciary when managing your portfolio, a salesperson when selling you an annuity in the same meeting. Ask which hat they're wearing for each recommendation. And the moment you're most exposed is often a rollover: leaving a job or retiring with a large 401(k) balance is exactly when product pitches arrive and when the protections are thinnest.

Every broker and adviser must hand you a short Form CRS (relationship summary). It includes the SEC's "conversation starter" questions, which are worth asking out loud. The most useful: "If I give you $10,000 to invest, how much will go to fees and costs?" and "How might your conflicts of interest affect me, and how will you address them?"

The price tag, compounded

What advice costs over a lifetime

A percentage fee feels small because it's quoted as a percentage. Measured in dollars, it grows every year your savings grow, even though the work involved usually doesn't. Compare the same saver under three arrangements.

Same savings, three ways to get advice
Do it yourself · index funds Flat-fee planner + index funds % of assets advisor
Yearly compounding; fees taken at the end of each year; the DIY and flat-fee paths hold index funds at 0.05%. The default 1.6% combines a 1% advisory fee with about 0.6% of underlying fund and platform costs, close to the median all-in cost for accounts up to $1 million in Bob Veres' survey (via Kitces). Flat fee held constant in dollars.

At the defaults, the percentage-based advisor's fee starts around $4,300 in year one and reaches about $24,000 a year by year 25, for largely the same service. The portfolio ends near $1.50 million, versus $1.94 million with a flat-fee planner and $2.09 million doing it yourself. None of that means a 1% advisor is never worth it. A good one who keeps you from selling in a crash, fixes your taxes and plans your retirement income can earn the fee. It means the fee should buy that much, and it's reasonable to ask for a flat price or a lower rate as balances grow.

What you'll hear

Seven pitches, decoded

The same few ideas carry most high-commission sales. None of them is a lie on its face; each one leaves out the price. Here's the claim, what's really going on, and the question that brings the price into view.

"You get the market's upside with none of the downside."
What's left out

The floor is paid for by giving up upside: caps, participation rates and spreads, and usually no dividends. In strong years you get a fraction of the market's gain. Annuities Explained shows how.

AskWhat would this have credited in 2021, 2023 and 2024, and what did the S&P 500 return with dividends in those years?

"It's tax-free, like a Roth with no limits." · "Be your own bank."
What's left out

Usually permanent life insurance. The "tax-free" money is policy loans against cash value that's built after years of insurance charges and commissions, and the policy can lapse. A 401(k), Roth IRA and HSA give tax breaks for a fraction of the cost. Life Insurance Explained covers it.

AskHave I already filled my 401(k), IRA and HSA? What is the cash value in year 5 if I stop paying?

"You get a 10% bonus on day one."
What's left out

Bonuses are paid back through lower caps, higher fees, vesting schedules and longer surrender periods. No product hands out free money.

AskWhat are the cap rates and surrender schedule on the same product without the bonus?

"Your income base grows at a guaranteed 7% a year."
What's left out

An income base (or "benefit base") isn't money you can withdraw. It's a number used to calculate lifetime income payments, and the rider that grows it has a yearly fee. The "7%" isn't a return. Annuities Explained works through it.

AskWhat is my actual cash surrender value in year 10, and what does the rider cost each year?

"Steady 6% income, with none of the stock market's swings."
What's left out

Often a non-traded REIT or private fund. The SEC warns upfront fees can reach 15%, redemptions can be limited or suspended, distributions may be paid from investors' own money, and the smooth price comes from infrequent appraisals, not a market.

AskHow much of the distribution is return of my own capital? How and when can I sell, and at what price?

"Our manager beat the market last year."
What's left out

Some managers beat the index every year; very few keep doing it, and over 20 years about 93% of large-company funds trailed the S&P 500. See Investing Basics.

AskHow has it done over 10 and 15 years, after all fees, against a total-market index fund?

"This rate is only available until Friday."
What's left out

Urgency exists to stop you from getting a second opinion. Good long-term decisions survive a week of thinking. Insurance products also come with a state-mandated free-look period (commonly 10 to 30 days) to cancel for a refund.

AskCan I take the contract, not the brochure, home for a week and show it to someone else?

Getting good help

Finding and checking an advisor

Plenty of people benefit from advice, especially around big transitions: a windfall, a divorce, a business sale, retirement income, or simply wanting someone to keep you from panicking in a crash. The goal isn't to avoid advisors. It's to pay a fair price to someone whose interests line up with yours.

FIND

Where fee-only advisors list themselves

NAPFA (fee-only advisors), the XY Planning Network (flat-fee and subscription planners, often for younger clients), the Garrett Planning Network (hourly), the Advice-Only Financial network, and the CFP Board's "Let's Make a Plan" search.

CHECK

Verify before you meet

Look up the person and firm on FINRA BrokerCheck and the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site: licenses, employment history, customer complaints and discipline. Check a CFP® mark at cfp.net and an insurance license with your state insurance department. Read the firm's Form CRS and Form ADV Part 2.

CUSTODY

Your money should sit with an independent custodian

Your account should be at a large, separate firm (a major brokerage or bank), in your name, with statements coming to you directly from it. Never write a check to the advisor personally. Keeping the advisor away from the money itself is what stops the rare outright fraud.

ASK

Five interview questions

Are you a fiduciary for everything you recommend to me, and will you put that in writing? How exactly are you paid, and by whom? What will I pay in total, in dollars, in a typical year? What services do I get for that? What happens if I want to leave?

Often worth paying for

Planning, taxes, behavior

A full plan across taxes, insurance, estate and retirement income; help through a big transition; or a steady voice that keeps you invested when markets fall. A flat fee or hourly engagement often buys this well.

Rarely worth a premium

Picking funds for you

Choosing and rebalancing a portfolio is the part the baseline already does for 0.05%–0.3% a year, through a target-date fund or a robo-advisor. Paying 1% for that alone is expensive.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Is a commission always a bad sign?

No. Buying term life insurance through an agent is commission-based and usually perfectly sensible, and a commission can be cheaper than years of percentage fees for a one-time purchase. The issue is when the commission is large, hidden, or tied to a product you don't need. Know the amount and compare it to the alternatives.

My advisor charges 1%. Should I leave?

Not automatically. List what you actually get (planning, tax work, estate coordination, coaching) and price it in dollars using the calculator above. If it's mostly fund picking, a flat-fee planner plus index funds may serve you better. If you stay, ask about lower rates as your balance grows.

I already bought one of these products. What now?

If you're still inside the free-look period, you can cancel for a refund. After that, don't rush to "fix" it: surrender charges and a new contract's fresh surrender period can make switching worse. Get a fee-only review of the actual contract and compare keeping it, taking penalty-free withdrawals, and surrendering.

Are robo-advisors a good middle ground?

For portfolio management alone, often yes: they build and rebalance an index portfolio automatically for a small fee on top of fund costs. Many now offer access to human planners for a flat fee. Compare the all-in cost with a single target-date fund, which does much of the same thing.

The advisor is a friend or family member. Do I still need to check?

Yes, with the same questions and the same lookups. Affinity is how many bad sales, and most frauds, travel. A good advisor won't mind.

Where can I complain if something went wrong?

For brokers and securities: FINRA and the SEC. For investment advisers: the SEC or your state securities regulator. For insurance and annuities: your state insurance department. Arbitration through FINRA is the usual path for recovering money from a broker.

Reference

Glossary

Fiduciary
Someone legally required to act in your best interest, putting your interests ahead of their own.
Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI)
The SEC standard for brokers: recommendations must be in your best interest when made.
Suitability
A lower standard: the product must be appropriate for you, not necessarily the best option.
Registered investment adviser (RIA)
A firm registered with the SEC or a state to give investment advice for a fee; owes a fiduciary duty.
Broker-dealer
A firm that buys and sells securities for customers, typically paid by commissions.
Dual registrant
A firm or person acting as both adviser and broker, depending on the service.
Fee-only
Paid only by the client; no commissions from anyone.
Fee-based
Usually a mix of client fees and product commissions.
AUM fee
A yearly fee charged as a percentage of the assets an advisor manages.
Trail commission
An ongoing payment to a seller for as long as you hold a product.
Surrender charge
A penalty for leaving an annuity or insurance policy early, often funding the seller's commission.
Free-look period
A state-required window after buying insurance or an annuity to cancel for a refund.
Form CRS
A short relationship summary every broker and adviser must give you, covering services, fees and conflicts.
Form ADV Part 2
An adviser's plain-language brochure about services, fees, conflicts and discipline.
BrokerCheck
FINRA's free lookup of brokers' and advisers' licenses, history and complaints.
Custodian
The separate institution that actually holds your investments and sends your statements.
Non-traded REIT
A real estate investment trust not listed on an exchange: hard to sell and often high-fee.
Income base
A notional value used to calculate annuity income payments; not money you can withdraw.
