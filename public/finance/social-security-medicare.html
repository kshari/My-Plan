Social Security & Medicare in Depth

How your Social Security benefit is figured, when to claim it, how spousal and survivor benefits work, whether the program will be there, and how Medicare's parts, deadlines and income surcharges fit together. Three calculators use 2026 rules.

TL;DR

Social Security is an inflation-adjusted pension for life, and the age you claim sets its size for good. For anyone born in 1960 or later, claiming at 62 pays 70% of your full benefit, waiting to 67 pays 100%, and waiting to 70 pays 124%. Claiming at 70 instead of 62 breaks even around age 80, so waiting is a bet on living longer, and it's the cheapest longevity insurance you can buy. For couples, the higher earner's claiming age matters most, because their benefit becomes the survivor benefit. The 2026 Trustees Report projects the combined trust funds run short in 2034, after which incoming taxes would still pay about 83% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts. Medicare starts at 65 regardless of when you claim Social Security, with a seven-month enrollment window and lifelong penalties for missing it. In 2026, Part B costs $202.90 a month, rising in steps (IRMAA) if your income from two years earlier was above $109,000 single or $218,000 joint.

How your Social Security benefit is figured

Social Security isn't a savings account with your name on it. It's social insurance: today's workers' payroll taxes pay today's retirees, and your benefit is set by a formula based on your own earnings record. Three steps turn a working life into a monthly check.

STEP 1

Qualify

40 credits, about 10 years of work. In 2026 you earn one credit per $1,890 of earnings, up to four a year.

STEP 2

Average your best 35 years

Each year's earnings are adjusted for wage growth, and the top 35 are averaged. Fewer than 35 years? Zeros fill the gap.

STEP 3

Apply the formula

A progressive formula replaces a bigger share of lower earnings. The result is your full benefit at full retirement age (your "PIA").

STEP 4

Adjust for claiming age

Claim early and it's permanently reduced; wait past full retirement age and it grows, up to 70.

= your check, raised yearly for inflation

In 2026 the average retired worker receives about $2,071 a month after a 2.8% cost-of-living increase, and the maximum at full retirement age is $4,152. Your own estimate, based on your actual record, is on your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount. Check the earnings history there for missing years while you can still fix them.

Two more things worth knowing. Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable, depending on your other income (Taxes 101 has a calculator). And the Social Security Fairness Act, signed in January 2025, repealed the Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset, which had reduced benefits for many teachers, police, firefighters and other public workers with pensions from jobs outside Social Security.

The biggest decision

When to claim

You can claim any month from 62 to 70. Every month you wait raises your check permanently. Before full retirement age, each month early cuts it by about 0.56% (then 0.42% beyond three years early). After it, each month adds 0.67%, or 8% a year, until 70.

The lines cross at the break-even ages: about 79 for 62 versus 67, about 80 for 62 versus 70, and about 82 for 67 versus 70. A 65-year-old today has a good chance of living past those ages, especially a married couple, where one spouse will often reach their 90s. The real value of waiting isn't winning the bet on average; it's the insurance. Running out of savings at 90 is the expensive outcome, and a larger inflation-adjusted check for life is the cheapest protection against it. No annuity sold today offers inflation protection at that price (see Annuities Explained).

Waiting usually makes sense if —

you're healthy, or married and the higher earner

You have savings or work income to live on in the meantime, your family tends to live long, or your benefit will become your spouse's survivor benefit. Spending from savings in your 60s to delay Social Security is often a good trade, and it can open a low-tax window for Roth conversions.

Claiming earlier can make sense if —

health, cash or the couple math says so

Serious health problems or a short family life expectancy; no other way to pay the bills without high-interest debt; or you're the lower earner in a couple and the higher earner is delaying. Claiming early isn't a mistake when it's a choice made with these facts.

Two records, one household

Spouses, survivors and ex-spouses

Marriage adds two benefits that often matter more than the claiming age of the lower earner: a spousal benefit while both are alive, and a survivor benefit after one dies.

SPOUSE

Spousal benefit: up to 50% of your spouse's full benefit

If half of your spouse's full-retirement-age benefit is more than your own, you get a top-up to that level once your spouse has filed. It's reduced if you claim before your own full retirement age (to 32.5% at 62) and doesn't grow past full retirement age, so a lower earner gains nothing by waiting past 67 for it. When you file, Social Security automatically counts you as applying for both your own and spousal benefits.

SURVIVOR

Survivor benefit: the bigger check continues

When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the larger of the two benefits and the smaller one stops. If the higher earner waited until 70, the survivor inherits that larger, inflation-adjusted check. A survivor can claim as early as 60 at a reduced rate (71.5%), or full at their own full retirement age.

EX

Divorced? You may still qualify

If the marriage lasted at least 10 years and you're unmarried, you can claim spousal and survivor benefits on an ex-spouse's record, without reducing theirs or their new spouse's. They don't need to know.

Paychecks and benefits together

Working while collecting

If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, the earnings test temporarily withholds benefits. It isn't a tax. Withheld months are credited back once you reach full retirement age, raising your check afterward.

Only wages and self-employment income count, not investment income, pensions or IRA withdrawals. If you plan to keep working full time, claiming early rarely makes sense. And any year you earn more than one of your top-35 years raises your benefit automatically.

The question everyone asks

Will Social Security be there?

Yes, but possibly not in full without changes. Social Security is mostly paid by current payroll taxes, with trust fund reserves covering the gap between taxes and benefits. Those reserves are shrinking.

"Running out" doesn't mean zero: payroll taxes would still come in. Congress faced a similar shortfall in 1983 and closed it with a mix of higher taxes and a later retirement age, phased in slowly. Some combination of higher taxes, benefit changes for future retirees, or general revenue is likely again, but nobody knows the mix. For planning: people near retirement can reasonably count on most of their scheduled benefit; younger workers may want a cushion, for example planning on 75%–80% of their statement estimate. Either way, the claiming-age math above still holds.

Health coverage from 65

Medicare's parts and what they cost

Medicare is federal health insurance from 65 (or earlier for some people with disabilities). It comes in parts, and the main choice is between Original Medicare with add-ons and a private Medicare Advantage plan.

Medicare Advantage

Lower premiums, more rules

Often extras like dental and vision, but networks, referrals and prior authorization, and copays that add up in a bad year. Check that your doctors and hospitals are in network, and look up the plan's out-of-pocket maximum. Heavily marketed; see Spotting the Sales Pitch.

Missing these costs money for life

Medicare deadlines and penalties

65

Your initial window: seven months around your 65th birthday

Three months before your birthday month, the month itself, and three months after. If you're already collecting Social Security you're enrolled automatically; if not, you must sign up. Delaying Social Security does not delay Medicare. This is the most common and costly trap.

WORK

Still working at 65?

If you (or your spouse) have health coverage from current employment, you can usually delay Part B without penalty and sign up within eight months after that job or coverage ends. COBRA and retiree coverage don't count as current employment. Small employers (under 20 workers) may require Medicare to go first.

LATE

Late penalties last for life

Part B: 10% added to your premium for each full 12 months you could have had it but didn't. Part D: 1% of the national base premium for each month without drug coverage at least as good as Medicare's. Miss your window and you may also wait for the general enrollment period (January to March).

GAP

The Medigap window

For six months starting when you're 65 and enrolled in Part B, insurers must sell you any Medigap policy at standard rates, regardless of health. After that, in most states they can decline you or charge more.

HSA

Stop HSA contributions in time

You can't contribute to an HSA once you're on Medicare. Part A enrollment can be backdated up to six months when you sign up after 65, so stop contributions six months before you enroll or claim Social Security. The money already in your HSA is still yours to spend.

The income surcharge

IRMAA: when income raises your premiums

Higher-income retirees pay more for Parts B and D. The surcharge, called IRMAA, is based on your tax return from two years earlier: 2026 premiums use 2024 income. The brackets are cliffs, so one dollar over a line triggers the whole surcharge.

If your income dropped because of a life-changing event (retirement, a spouse's death, divorce, loss of a pension), ask Social Security to use your newer income with Form SSA-44.

A checklist from 60 to 70

Run the claiming and couple calculators

Plan how you'll pay the bills while waiting

Savings, part-time work, or a bond or cash "bridge" sized to the years between retiring and claiming.

Put your Medicare window on the calendar

Three months before your 65th birthday month. Decide Original + Medigap + Part D or Medicare Advantage before then.

Still working at 65? Confirm with HR

Is your coverage from current employment, and does the employer have 20+ workers? Get it in writing before skipping Part B.

Stop HSA contributions six months ahead

Before enrolling in Medicare Part A or claiming Social Security after 65.

Watch income two years ahead of Medicare

Your income at 63 sets your premiums at 65. Time Roth conversions and big sales with the IRMAA brackets in mind.

Review Part D or Advantage coverage every fall

Open enrollment runs October 15 to December 7. Plans change their prices and drug lists every year.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Shouldn't I claim early in case Social Security runs out?

Probably not. If benefits are cut, the cut would most likely apply to everyone's check, early claimers included, and early claimers would still have the permanently smaller one. Claiming early to "get yours" locks in a lower lifetime benefit to hedge a risk it doesn't really hedge.

Can I change my mind after claiming?

Within 12 months you can withdraw your application once, by repaying everything you and your family received. After full retirement age you can also voluntarily suspend benefits to earn delayed credits until 70.

What if I didn't work 10 years?

You may still qualify through a spouse or ex-spouse. Otherwise, a few more years of work to reach 40 credits can be very valuable, and so can any year that replaces a zero in your top 35.

Do I have to take Medicare Part B if I have retiree coverage?

Usually yes. Most retiree plans assume Medicare pays first and cover little without it. Retiree and COBRA coverage don't protect you from the late penalty.

Medicare Advantage ads say I'll get benefits for $0. What's the catch?

The plan is paid by Medicare, so the extra premium can be low. The trade-offs are networks, prior authorization and copays, and switching back to Original Medicare with Medigap later may require health questions. Compare total costs in a bad year, not just the premium.

How do I sign up?

Online at ssa.gov (for both Social Security and Medicare), by phone, or at a local office. Apply for Social Security up to four months before you want benefits to start.

Glossary

Full retirement age (FRA)
The age you get 100% of your benefit: 67 if born in 1960 or later.
Primary insurance amount (PIA)
Your monthly benefit if you claim exactly at full retirement age.
Delayed retirement credits
8% a year added for waiting past full retirement age, up to 70.
COLA
Cost-of-living adjustment: the yearly inflation raise (2.8% for 2026).
Work credits
Earned from wages; 40 are needed to qualify for retirement benefits.
Spousal benefit
Up to 50% of a spouse's full benefit, for a lower-earning spouse.
Survivor benefit
The deceased spouse's benefit, paid to the surviving spouse if it's larger than their own.
Earnings test
Temporary withholding of benefits for people under full retirement age with wages above a limit.
Trust funds
Social Security's reserves, which cover the gap between payroll taxes and benefits.
Original Medicare
Parts A and B, run by the federal government.
Medigap
Private supplemental insurance that covers Original Medicare's deductibles and coinsurance.
Medicare Advantage (Part C)
A private plan that replaces Original Medicare, usually with drug coverage and networks.
Part D
Prescription drug coverage through private plans.
Initial enrollment period
The seven-month window around your 65th birthday to sign up for Medicare.
IRMAA
Income-related monthly adjustment amount: higher Part B and D premiums for higher incomes.
Form SSA-44
The form to ask for lower IRMAA after a life-changing event reduces your income.
