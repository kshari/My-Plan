Estate Planning Essentials: Wills, Trusts, Beneficiaries and the Binder

The handful of documents everyone needs, how your money actually passes when you die (often not through your will), when a trust is worth it, planning for the day you can't make decisions yourself, estate taxes in 2026, and an "in case of emergency" binder you can print and fill in. One calculator maps your own accounts.

TL;DR

Estate planning isn't just for the wealthy; it's for anyone with a family, a home, a retirement account or opinions about their medical care. Only about 1 in 4 U.S. adults has a will. The essentials are a will (which names a guardian for minor children), up-to-date beneficiary designations, a durable financial power of attorney and a health care proxy with a living will. Most of your money probably passes by beneficiary form, joint ownership or transfer-on-death registration, which override your will, so checking those forms is the highest-value hour you can spend. A revocable living trust helps avoid probate and manage money if you become incapacitated, and is most useful with property in several states, a blended family, young or vulnerable heirs, or in costly-probate states. The federal estate tax only applies above $15 million per person ($30 million per couple) in 2026, but 12 states and D.C. have their own estate tax. Finally, write down where everything is: the binder below saves your family months.

Why bother, and what happens without a plan

Everyone has an estate plan. If you don't write one, your state's law supplies a default one. In Caring.com's 2025 survey, just 24% of Americans had a will, down from 33% in 2022, and the most common reason was simply not getting around to it. Here's what the default plan looks like.

QuestionWith a planWithout one
Who raises your minor children?The guardian you namedA judge decides, sometimes after family disputes
Who inherits what?The people and charities you choseState "intestacy" law: usually a spouse and children in fixed shares; unmarried partners and stepchildren often get nothing
Who handles the paperwork?The executor or trustee you pickedA court-appointed administrator, possibly requiring a bond
Who manages money if you're incapacitated?Your agent under a power of attorneyA court guardianship or conservatorship: slow, public and costly

The short list

The core documents

Four documents cover most families; a trust and a letter of instruction round it out. In a 2026 survey of 909 law firms, the median flat fee was about $1,000 for a will-based package and $2,700 for a trust-based package (about $1,500 and $3,000 for couples). Online services cost less and work for simple situations.

WILL

Last will and testament

Names an executor to settle your affairs, a guardian for minor children, and who gets anything that doesn't pass another way. It must be signed and witnessed under your state's rules. Median attorney fee: about $625.

BENEFICIARIES

Beneficiary designations and TOD/POD registrations

The forms on your 401(k), IRA, life insurance, annuities, HSA and bank and brokerage accounts. They decide who gets that money, regardless of what your will says. Free, and usually online.

POA

Durable financial power of attorney

Lets someone you trust pay your bills, manage accounts and file taxes if you can't. "Durable" means it keeps working after you lose capacity. Median fee about $300.

HEALTH

Health care proxy, living will and HIPAA release

The proxy (health care power of attorney) names who makes medical decisions for you; the living will records your wishes about life support and end-of-life care; the HIPAA release lets doctors talk to the people you name. Often combined into one "advance directive." Many states offer free forms.

TRUST

Revocable living trust (optional)

Holds your assets while you're alive (you stay in control) and passes them to heirs without probate, managed by a successor trustee if you're incapacitated or die. Median fee about $2,475. Only works for assets you actually move into it. Do you need one?

LETTER

Letter of instruction (not legally binding)

Plain-language notes for your family: where things are, who to call, your funeral wishes, what you'd like done with personal items. The binder below is a template.

The part people miss

How your money actually passes

Your will only controls what's left after four other routes have done their work. For most families, the largest assets (retirement accounts, life insurance, a jointly owned home) never go near the will. Anything that falls through to the will goes through probate, a court process that's public, often takes many months, and can cost several percent of the estate in fees. Many states have faster, simpler procedures for small estates.

ROUTE 1

Beneficiary form

Retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, HSAs. Paid directly to whoever is named.

ROUTE 2

Joint, TOD or POD

Joint owners with survivorship rights; payable-on-death bank accounts; transfer-on-death brokerage accounts, and TOD deeds for homes in 30+ states.

ROUTE 3

Trust

Anything titled in a living trust passes to the heirs the trust names, handled by your successor trustee.

ROUTE 4

Will and probate

Everything else in your name alone. No will? State law decides who gets it.

The big choice

Will or living trust?

A living trust isn't a tax shelter and isn't only for the rich. It's a way to skip probate and keep things private, and to have someone step in smoothly if you can't manage your money. A will plus good beneficiary and TOD designations gets many families most of the same benefit for less.

A will is often enough

Simple estates

A home owned jointly or with a TOD deed, retirement accounts and insurance with named beneficiaries, TOD on brokerage and bank accounts, and adult heirs you'd give money to outright.

A trust earns its cost

More moving parts

Real estate in more than one state, a blended family, minor or vulnerable heirs, a desire for privacy, a business, or a state where probate is slow and expensive.

The forms that beat your will

Getting beneficiary designations right

BOTH

Name a primary and a contingent beneficiary

If your only beneficiary dies before you and there's no backup, the account can fall into probate. Consider "per stirpes," so a child's share passes to their children if they die first.

NOT A MINOR

Don't name a minor child directly

Companies can't pay a minor, so a court would appoint someone to manage the money, and the child gets it all at 18. Name a trust for the children or a custodian under your state's UTMA law (see Kids & Money).

NOT THE ESTATE

Avoid naming "my estate"

It pushes the money into probate and, for retirement accounts, can force heirs to empty the account faster and pay more tax.

EX-SPOUSE

Update after divorce

For employer plans like 401(k)s, federal law generally pays whoever is on the form, even an ex-spouse, whatever your will or state law says. In a 401(k), your spouse is the default beneficiary unless they sign a waiver.

HEIRS' TAXES

What heirs owe

Most non-spouse heirs must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years, paying income tax on traditional accounts (see Turning Savings into a Paycheck). Roth accounts pass tax-free, and a spouse can roll the account into their own. Stocks, funds and a home in a taxable account or held outright get a "step-up" in cost basis at death, so the gain during your lifetime is never taxed; that's a reason not to give away highly appreciated assets shortly before death.

Plan for living, too

If you can't decide for yourself

A stroke, dementia or an accident is more likely to disrupt a family's finances than death, and a will does nothing until you die. Without a power of attorney and health care proxy, your family may need to go to court for a guardianship or conservatorship just to pay your bills.

Money

Durable power of attorney

Choose someone organized and trustworthy, plus a backup. Banks sometimes balk at old or unfamiliar forms, so use your state's statutory form and give copies to your financial institutions.

Medical

Health care proxy and living will

Talk through your wishes with the person you name. Give copies to your doctors and keep one where family can find it.

Young adults

At 18, parents lose access

Once a child turns 18, parents can't see medical records or manage accounts without permission. College-bound kids should sign a health care proxy, HIPAA release and POA.

Mostly not a worry

Estate and gift taxes in 2026

The 2025 tax law set the federal estate and gift tax exemption at $15 million per person ($30 million for a married couple) for 2026, adjusted for inflation each year, with no expiration date. Above that, the top rate is 40%. Fewer than 1 in 1,000 estates owe it. State taxes are a different story.

GIFTS

Giving while you're alive

You can give up to $19,000 per person per year (2026) without filing anything. Larger gifts just require a gift tax return and count against your $15 million lifetime exemption; tax is due only after you've used it all. Paying tuition or medical bills directly to the school or provider doesn't count at all.

PORTABILITY

Married? File at the first death

A surviving spouse can use whatever part of the first spouse's exemption went unused, but only if the executor files an estate tax return (Form 706) after the first death, even when no tax is owed. It matters most for couples who could someday cross a state or federal threshold.

The gift your family will thank you for

The "in case of emergency" binder

When someone dies or is hospitalized, the hardest part for the family is often just finding things: which bank, which insurer, where the will is, what the phone passcode is. A binder (paper, or a secure digital folder) fixes that. Print the template, fill it in by hand, keep it somewhere safe, and tell two people where it is.

1. People to call

  • Executor, trustee and agents under POA and health proxy
  • Attorney, financial advisor, accountant, insurance agent
  • Employer or HR (for life insurance and final pay)
  • Guardian for children; care for pets

2. Where the documents are

  • Will, trust, powers of attorney, advance directive
  • Birth and marriage certificates, divorce decree, military discharge (DD-214)
  • Deeds, car titles, recent tax returns
  • Safe deposit box location and key

3. Money accounts

  • Bank, brokerage, retirement and HSA: institution, type, last 4 digits, beneficiary
  • Pensions and Social Security
  • Crypto: exchange or wallet, and where recovery info is kept

4. Insurance

  • Life, disability, long-term care: company, policy number, beneficiary
  • Health, Medicare, home, auto, umbrella
  • Employer-provided coverage

5. Bills and debts

  • Mortgage, loans, credit cards
  • Utilities and subscriptions; which are on autopay, from which account
  • Property taxes and estimated tax payments

6. Digital life

  • Password manager and its emergency-access setup
  • Phone and computer passcodes (or where they're kept)
  • Email and two-factor recovery; Apple and Google "legacy contact" settings
  • Social media wishes

7. Home and property

  • Home: deed, mortgage, insurer, spare keys, alarm codes
  • Vehicles: titles, loans, insurance
  • Valuables and who should get them

8. Final wishes

  • Burial or cremation; prepaid plans
  • Service preferences; obituary notes
  • Organ donation
  • Personal messages

Don't write full account numbers, passwords or Social Security numbers in a paper binder. Write where to find them (a password manager with an emergency-access contact is the safest option). Almost every state now has a law letting executors access digital accounts, but providers' own tools, like Apple's Legacy Contact and Google's Inactive Account Manager, are faster.

Not one and done

When to update

Review everything every three to five years, and right away after any of these: marriage, divorce, a birth or adoption, a death in the family, a move to another state, buying a home or a big change in wealth, a child turning 18, or a change of heart about the people you've named. Check beneficiary forms at the same time; they're the part that goes stale most often.

Do this

An estate planning checklist

Sign a will that names an executor and a guardian

Online services work for simple situations; an attorney is worth it for blended families, a business or a special-needs heir.

Sign a durable power of attorney

Use your state's statutory form and give copies to your bank and brokerage.

Sign a health care proxy, living will and HIPAA release

And talk your wishes through with the person you name.

Log in and check every beneficiary form

401(k), IRA, HSA, life insurance, annuities. Primary and contingent; no minors, no ex-spouses, not "my estate."

Add TOD or POD to bank and brokerage accounts

And consider a transfer-on-death deed for your home if your state allows it.

Decide whether you need a trust

If you create one, fund it: retitle the home and accounts and confirm with each institution.

Fill in the binder and tell two people where it is

Set up a password manager with emergency access and your phone's legacy-contact settings.

Put a review on the calendar

Every three to five years, and after any major life change.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Is an online will legally valid?

Yes, if it's signed and witnessed (and notarized, where required) under your state's rules; the service will explain how. They work well for straightforward situations. A handwritten will is valid in about half the states but is easy to get wrong.

Does a trust protect my assets from nursing home costs or creditors?

A revocable trust doesn't: you still own and control the assets, so they count for Medicaid and creditors. Irrevocable trusts can, but they mean giving up control and have strict timing rules (Medicaid looks back five years). That's specialist territory.

Should I add my child to my bank account or house deed to avoid probate?

Usually not. A joint owner legally co-owns it now: their creditors or divorce can reach it, you may need their signature to sell, it can create a gift-tax filing, and your other children may be cut out. A POD/TOD designation or a trust avoids probate without those problems.

Who should I pick as executor or trustee?

Someone organized, trustworthy and willing, ideally not far away, with a backup. It's a real job that can take a year or more. A professional or corporate trustee can make sense for large or complicated estates, for a fee.

Where should I keep the original will?

Somewhere safe that your executor can get to: a fireproof home safe, your attorney's office, or your county's will registry if it has one. A safe deposit box can be hard for others to open after you die.

Do I need to worry about estate taxes?

Federally, only if you're above $15 million ($30 million as a couple) in 2026. Check the state table above if you live in one of those states, own property there, or could inherit from someone who does; some state exemptions start at $1 million to $2 million, which a home plus retirement accounts can reach.

Glossary

Estate
Everything you own at death, minus what you owe.
Executor (personal representative)
The person who settles your estate under your will.
Probate
The court process that validates a will and oversees paying debts and distributing assets.
Intestate
Dying without a valid will; state law decides who inherits.
Guardian
The person named to raise minor children; also a court-appointed decision-maker for an incapacitated adult.
Revocable living trust
A trust you control and can change while alive, which passes assets outside probate.
Irrevocable trust
A trust that generally can't be changed, used for tax, Medicaid or asset-protection planning.
Trustee / successor trustee
The person managing a trust; the successor takes over when you can't.
Pour-over will
A will that sends anything left outside a trust into the trust.
Funding a trust
Retitling assets into the trust's name so it controls them.
Beneficiary designation
The form naming who receives an account or policy at death.
Per stirpes
If a beneficiary dies first, their share goes to their descendants.
TOD / POD
Transfer-on-death / payable-on-death: naming who inherits an account or property directly.
Durable power of attorney
Authority for an agent to handle your finances, lasting through incapacity.
Health care proxy
The person authorized to make medical decisions if you can't.
Living will
Written wishes about end-of-life medical treatment.
Step-up in basis
Resetting an inherited asset's cost basis to its value at death, erasing the taxable gain.
Portability
A surviving spouse using the unused part of the first spouse's estate tax exemption.
