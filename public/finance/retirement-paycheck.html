Turning Savings into a Paycheck: Withdrawals, Roth Conversions and RMDs

How much you can safely spend from savings, why the order of good and bad years matters, how to build a floor of guaranteed income, which accounts to draw from first, and how Roth conversions in the "gap years" can shrink required withdrawals and lifetime taxes. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

Retirement flips the job of your savings from growing to paying you. Start with the gap between what you spend and what Social Security and any pension pay; that gap is what your portfolio has to cover. For a 30-year retirement, Morningstar's research puts a safe fixed starting withdrawal at about 3.9% of savings a year (adjusted for inflation after that), and closer to 6% if you're willing to cut spending in bad markets. The biggest danger is a bad market early in retirement, so keep one to two years of spending in cash and bonds, and be ready to trim. Build a floor of guaranteed income by delaying Social Security where you can. For taxes, don't just drain one account at a time: use the low-tax years between retiring and age 73 or 75 to withdraw or convert to Roth up to the top of a low bracket, which shrinks the required minimum distributions that would otherwise push you into higher brackets, raise Medicare premiums and tax your Social Security later.

From saving to spending: the income gap

For 30 or 40 years, the plan was simple: save, invest, don't touch it. Now you need a monthly paycheck from a pile of accounts, each taxed differently, for a lifespan you don't know, through markets you can't predict. The first step is simple arithmetic.

STEP 1

What you'll spend

Your yearly spending in retirement, including taxes, health care and Medicare premiums. Many people spend less as they age, apart from health care.

STEP 2

Minus guaranteed income

Social Security, pensions, annuity payments. These last for life, and Social Security rises with inflation.

STEP 3

= The gap

What your savings must supply each year. This is the number that drives everything else.

= withdrawals
STEP 4

Gap ÷ savings

Your withdrawal rate. Around 4% or less is generally sustainable for 30 years; much above 5% needs flexibility or a shorter horizon.

Example: a couple spends $90,000 a year, and Social Security pays them $48,000. The gap is $42,000. With $1,000,000 saved, that's a 4.2% withdrawal rate: workable, but with little room for a bad start. Delaying one Social Security claim, working a year or two longer, or trimming $5,000 of spending each move the number meaningfully.

The 4% question

How much can you spend?

The "4% rule" comes from a 1994 study: withdraw 4% of your savings in the first year, raise that dollar amount with inflation every year, and historically the money lasted at least 30 years. Morningstar's latest research, for people retiring in 2026, puts the highest safe fixed starting rate at 3.9% (a 90% chance of lasting 30 years, with 30%–50% in stocks), up from 3.7% a year earlier. Retirees who are willing to spend less after bad years can start closer to 6%.

Withdrawals are taken at the start of each year. "Flexible" spends 10% less in any year the portfolio is below 80% of its starting value. Real markets are far less tidy; this isolates the effect of order.

At the defaults, a 4.5% withdrawal survives 30 years if returns are steady or the bad years come late, but runs out in year 28 if they come first, even though the average return is identical. Spending 10% less while the portfolio is down keeps it going, with about $221,000 left. That's the case for a cash cushion, a guaranteed-income floor, and a willingness to trim in bad years.

Fixed rule

About 3.9% of savings

Same inflation-adjusted amount every year, no matter what. Simple and predictable, but it leaves money unspent in most historical periods.

Guardrails

Start higher, adjust

Start around 4.5%–5%. Cut about 10% if your withdrawal rate drifts well above where it started; give yourself a raise if it falls well below.

Percentage of balance

Never runs out

Spend a fixed percentage of whatever the balance is each year. It can't hit zero, but your income swings with the market. Works best on top of a solid income floor.

Sleep-at-night money

Building a floor of guaranteed income

The more of your essential spending that's covered by income you can't outlive, the less a bad market can hurt you and the more freely you can spend the rest. There are three main ways to build that floor.

DELAY SS

Delay Social Security (the cheapest annuity there is)

Each year you wait past full retirement age adds 8% to your check, for life, with inflation adjustments and a survivor benefit for your spouse. A common plan is to spend savings from retirement until 70 as a "bridge," then let the larger check cover more of the rest. See the claiming calculator.

ANNUITY

A simple income annuity

A single-premium immediate annuity (SPIA) turns a lump sum into a lifelong monthly check, like buying a private pension. It's the plain version, with no market-linked bells and whistles and usually no commissions buried in surrender charges. It makes the most sense for part of your savings, to cover essentials Social Security doesn't. Annuities Explained covers the types and what to avoid.

TIPS

A TIPS ladder

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities that mature one per year give a known, inflation-adjusted amount each year for a set period, such as the years before Social Security starts. With 5-year real yields around 2.5% in September 2026, a ladder locks in a solid return above inflation. Unlike an annuity, you keep control, but it doesn't protect against living longer than the ladder.

A picture that helps

Buckets: cash for the bad years

A bucket plan splits the portfolio by when you'll need the money. It doesn't change the math much, but it makes it much easier to stay calm and avoid selling stocks after a crash.

BucketHoldsCoversWhere
1. Cash1–2 years of withdrawalsThis year's and next year's gapHigh-yield savings, money market funds, T-bills (see Where to Keep Your Cash)
2. BondsAbout 3–10 years of withdrawalsRefills bucket 1 when stocks are downHigh-quality bond funds, a Treasury or TIPS ladder
3. StocksEverything elseGrowth for years 10 and beyond, and inflation protectionBroad, low-cost index funds (the baseline)

The refill rule is what makes it work: in good years, sell some stocks to top up the cash bucket; in bad years, spend the cash and bonds and leave stocks alone to recover. Check it once a year, not every time the market moves.

Taxes decide the order

Which account to draw from first

Most retirees have three kinds of money: taxable (a brokerage account, where only gains are taxed), tax-deferred (traditional 401(k) and IRA, where every dollar out is income) and tax-free (Roth). The old rule of thumb spends them in that order. A better approach blends them to keep your taxable income smooth and low every year.

The rule of thumb

Taxable → tax-deferred → Roth

Lets the tax-advantaged accounts grow longest. But it often means years of almost no taxable income early on, followed by large required withdrawals later that land in higher brackets, raise Medicare premiums and make more of your Social Security taxable.

Usually better

Fill the low brackets every year

Each year, take (or convert) enough from traditional accounts to fill the 10% and 12% brackets, or 22% for larger balances, and cover the rest from taxable savings, where long-term gains may be taxed at 0% up to $98,900 of taxable income for couples in 2026. Save the Roth for last, for big one-off expenses, and for heirs.

ALWAYS

Take any required minimum distribution first

Once they start, RMDs come out regardless; count them toward your spending before taking anything else.

WATCH

The three hidden tax cliffs

Extra income can do more than raise your bracket. It can make up to 85% of your Social Security taxable (the "tax torpedo" in Taxes 101), trigger higher Medicare premiums (IRMAA, starting above $218,000 of income for couples, based on your return from two years earlier), and before 65 cost you marketplace health subsidies (see below).

The gap years

Roth conversions in the gap years

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) to a Roth IRA. You pay income tax on the amount now; after that it grows tax-free and never has required withdrawals. The best time is often the stretch between retiring and starting Social Security and RMDs, when your income, and so your tax bracket, is unusually low. Converting "up to the top of a bracket" each year uses that cheap space before it disappears.

At the defaults, a 66-year-old couple delaying Social Security, withdrawing $70,000 to live on, has about $73,000 of room left in the 12% bracket and could convert it for about $8,800 of federal tax. If they could do that every year until 75, the projected first RMD would drop from about $44,000 to about $11,000; in practice the room shrinks once Social Security starts at 70, so the real effect is smaller but still large. Conversions aren't free money: they're paying tax now at a rate you expect to be lower than later. They make the most sense when your future income (Social Security plus RMDs) would put you in a higher bracket, when you want to leave tax-free money to heirs, or when a surviving spouse would later file as single with narrower brackets.

RULES

The fine print

You can convert any amount, any year, at any age; conversions can't be undone. Each conversion has its own five-year clock before the converted amount can come out penalty-free if you're under 59½. Conversions raise your income for IRMAA (two years later) and, before 65, for marketplace subsidies. At 73 or 75, you must take the year's RMD before converting.

The IRS wants its share

Required minimum distributions

Traditional IRAs, 401(k)s and similar accounts must start paying out at a set age, whether you need the money or not. Each year's required amount is the prior December 31 balance divided by an IRS life-expectancy factor, so the percentage rises as you age.

WHEN

73 or 75, depending on birth year

Born 1951–1959: RMDs start at 73. Born 1960 or later: 75. The first one can wait until April 1 of the following year, but then you take two in one year. Still working? You can usually delay RMDs from your current employer's 401(k) (not IRAs) until you retire, unless you own more than 5% of the company.

ROTH

Roth accounts are exempt

Roth IRAs never have RMDs for the original owner, and since 2024 neither do Roth 401(k)s. That's a big reason conversions help.

QCD

Give to charity straight from the IRA

From age 70½, a qualified charitable distribution sends up to $111,000 (2026) a year directly from your IRA to charity. It counts toward your RMD but isn't taxable income, which beats taking the money and deducting the gift, especially if you take the standard deduction. Donor-advised funds don't qualify.

PENALTY

Don't miss one

A missed RMD costs 25% of the shortfall, cut to 10% if corrected within two years. Many custodians will calculate and send it automatically; set that up.

HEIRS

What your kids inherit

Most non-spouse heirs must empty an inherited traditional IRA within 10 years, often during their own peak earning years, and if you had already started RMDs, they must take some each year too. An inherited Roth also has the 10-year rule, but the withdrawals are tax-free.

Retiring before 59½ or 65

Retiring early adds two puzzles: getting to retirement money before 59½ without the 10% penalty, and paying for health insurance before Medicare at 65.

The health insurance cliff. Before 65, most early retirees buy coverage on the ACA marketplace, where subsidies depend on income. The extra subsidies of 2021–2025 expired, so for 2026 coverage, subsidies stop entirely above 400% of the poverty level: $62,600 for a single person and $84,600 for a couple. For 2027 coverage (open enrollment starts November 1, 2026), the limits rise to about $63,840 and $86,560. One dollar over can cost thousands in lost help, so plan Roth conversions, capital gains and IRA withdrawals to stay under it, or convert heavily in years you're already over. Do this

A retirement-income checklist

Write down your spending and your gap

Essential and flexible spending, minus Social Security and pensions. That gap, divided by your savings, is your withdrawal rate.

Decide your Social Security claiming ages

Especially the higher earner's, since it becomes the survivor's benefit.

Set up a cash bucket

One to two years of withdrawals in savings, money market funds or T-bills, with a once-a-year refill rule.

Pick a spending rule and your guardrails

Know in advance how much you'll trim after a bad year, and when you'll give yourself a raise.

Map your tax brackets year by year

Especially the gap years before Social Security and RMDs; plan withdrawals and Roth conversions to fill low brackets.

Check the cliffs before year-end

The ACA 400% line before 65, IRMAA brackets from 63 on, and how much Social Security is becoming taxable.

Automate RMDs and consider QCDs

Have the custodian send RMDs automatically; give to charity directly from the IRA from 70½.

Update beneficiaries on every account

Retirement accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by your will.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Is the 4% rule still valid?

As a starting point, yes. Morningstar's 2026 figure is 3.9% for a fixed, inflation-adjusted withdrawal over 30 years. It's lower for longer horizons or all-cash-and-bonds portfolios, and higher if you'll adjust spending, have a large income floor, or have a shorter horizon. Treat it as a check, not a promise.

Should I move everything to bonds when I retire?

No. A 30-year retirement still needs growth to keep up with inflation. Many retirees hold roughly 30%–60% in stocks; what matters most is having enough in cash and bonds to cover several years of withdrawals without selling stocks in a downturn.

Should I pay off my mortgage before retiring?

It lowers the spending you need to cover, which can help with the ACA and tax cliffs, but pulling a large sum from an IRA to do it can cost a lot in tax. Compare the mortgage rate with what your savings earn, and consider paying it down gradually from taxable money. See Credit & Debt.

How do I actually get a monthly paycheck?

Most brokerages let you set up an automatic monthly transfer from an IRA or brokerage account to checking, with federal (and state) tax withheld. Some retirees take one lump sum from the IRA each December instead, with tax withheld, which also counts as paid evenly through the year.

Do I need to pay estimated taxes in retirement?

If there's no withholding on your income, yes, quarterly. The easier route is to have tax withheld from IRA withdrawals and Social Security (Form W-4V) so you don't owe a penalty.

What about the "annuity with a bonus" my advisor suggested?

Compare it with the simple options first: delaying Social Security, a plain immediate annuity, or a TIPS ladder. Bonus and indexed annuities often carry long surrender periods and complex caps. See Spotting the Sales Pitch and Annuities Explained.

Glossary

Withdrawal rate
Yearly withdrawals as a percentage of savings, usually measured in the first year.
Safe withdrawal rate
The highest starting rate that historically lasted a set period, such as 30 years.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The risk that bad returns early in retirement, while you're withdrawing, do lasting damage.
Guardrails
A spending rule that trims or raises withdrawals when they drift outside set limits.
Income floor
Lifetime income, like Social Security, pensions and annuities, that covers essential spending.
SPIA
Single-premium immediate annuity: a lump sum exchanged for lifelong payments.
TIPS ladder
TIPS bought to mature one per year, providing known inflation-adjusted income.
Bucket strategy
Dividing savings into cash, bonds and stocks by when the money will be needed.
Roth conversion
Moving money from a traditional account to a Roth, paying tax on it now.
Gap years
The years between retiring and starting Social Security and RMDs, when income is often low.
RMD
Required minimum distribution: the yearly amount you must withdraw from traditional accounts from 73 or 75.
QCD
Qualified charitable distribution: a tax-free gift straight from an IRA to charity from age 70½.
IRMAA
Income-related monthly adjustment amount: higher Medicare premiums above certain incomes.
Tax torpedo
When extra income also makes more Social Security taxable, raising the effective rate.
Rule of 55
Penalty-free 401(k) withdrawals after leaving that employer in or after the year you turn 55.
72(t)
Substantially equal periodic payments that avoid the early-withdrawal penalty.
Subsidy cliff
The income (400% of the poverty level) above which ACA marketplace subsidies stop.
