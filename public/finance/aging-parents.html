Caring for Aging Parents: The Talk, the Paperwork and the Money

How to start the conversation, the documents and permissions that let you help, ways to watch over a parent's money without taking it over, where care happens and who pays, what caregiving costs you, the tax breaks for supporting a parent, and how to guard against the scams that target older adults. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

About 63 million Americans now care for a family member or friend, and most start without a plan. Have the conversation early, while your parent can decide for themselves, and get three things in place: a durable power of attorney, a health care proxy and HIPAA release, and a list of their accounts and bills. Help with money in steps, from view-only access and alerts to a "trusted contact" at each bank and brokerage, rather than adding your name to accounts. Care costs are covered in Long-Term Care; this episode is about the family side. Caregiving has real costs for you: lost pay, lost retirement savings and out-of-pocket spending that averages about $7,200 a year. Use FMLA leave or your state's paid family leave (13 states and D.C.), check whether you can claim your parent for tax breaks, and keep funding your own retirement. Older adults reported $7.7 billion in fraud losses to the FBI in 2025; a few simple settings make your parent a much harder target.

Signs it's time to step in

Money trouble is often the first visible sign of cognitive decline, sometimes years before a diagnosis. Watch for patterns, not one-off slips:

The hardest part

Having the conversation

The goal is to learn your parent's wishes and get the permissions to carry them out, not to take control. Start before there's a crisis, ideally while everyone is healthy.

HOW

Lead with their goals, not your worries

"What matters most to you if your health changes?" works better than "We need to talk about your money." Use a trigger that isn't about them: a friend's situation, your own estate planning, a news story. Expect several conversations, not one.

LEARN

What you need to know

Where they bank and invest; what income comes in (Social Security, pensions, IRA withdrawals); what bills go out and how they're paid; insurance policies, including any long-term care policy; where the will, power of attorney and health care proxy are; who their doctors, lawyer and advisor are; and where they'd want to live if they need help. The binder template is a ready-made list.

RESPECT

Their money, their choices

A parent who can still decide has every right to make choices you disagree with. Your role is to make sure they're informed, protected from people who would take advantage, and that someone can act if they can't.

Permission to help

The paperwork and permissions

Without legal authority, even a devoted child can't talk to a bank, a doctor or Medicare on a parent's behalf. If a parent loses capacity before signing these, the family usually has to go to court for a guardianship or conservatorship.

A ladder, not a takeover

Helping with money without taking over

Match the help to the need, and move up a step only when you have to. Every step keeps your parent in charge for as long as possible.

STEP 1

Simplify

Consolidate accounts, set up autopay for regular bills, direct deposit for all income, and a credit freeze.

STEP 2

See

View-only online access or duplicate statements, plus alerts for large withdrawals and low balances.

STEP 3

Share

A trusted contact everywhere; a small "spending" account they control, with the main savings separate.

STEP 4

Act

Use the power of attorney to pay bills and manage accounts, keeping records of every transaction.

Avoid becoming a joint owner. Adding a child to a parent's account seems simple, but it makes the money legally part-yours: it can be reached by your creditors or in your divorce, it passes to you at death regardless of the will (cutting out siblings), and it can complicate Medicaid. A power of attorney, a "convenience signer" where the bank offers it, or a payable-on-death beneficiary does the job without those problems.

The care itself

Where care happens, and who pays

Most care starts at home and moves to more support as needs grow. Long-Term Care covers costs, insurance and Medicaid's rules in depth; here's where families find help.

LOCAL

Start with the Area Agency on Aging

Every county is served by one. They connect families to meals, transportation, in-home help, respite care, caregiver training and benefits counseling, often free or low-cost. Find yours through the federal Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116).

MEDICARE

Medicare's dementia program (GUIDE)

For people with dementia on Original Medicare who live at home, participating providers offer a care navigator, a 24/7 support line, caregiver training and up to $2,500 a year of respite care. It's a pilot that runs through 2032 and isn't available everywhere; ask their doctor.

MEDICAID

Home and community care, and PACE

Medicaid pays for nursing homes and, through waiver programs, for care at home and in some assisted living, often with waiting lists. PACE programs combine medical care and daily support for people 55+ who would otherwise need a nursing home. The asset limits, spouse protections and five-year look-back are in Long-Term Care.

PAYING FAMILY

Getting paid as a family caregiver

Many state Medicaid programs let participants hire a relative as their paid caregiver (rules on spouses vary). A parent can also pay you privately under a written personal care agreement at a fair rate; it's taxable income to you, and paying for care this way, rather than as a gift, matters for a later Medicaid application. The VA's Aid and Attendance pension and caregiver programs help eligible veterans.

The hidden bill

What caregiving costs you

Caregiving is unpaid work, and it often costs the caregiver in reduced hours, a slower career, smaller retirement contributions and money spent directly on a parent's needs. About 6 in 10 family caregivers also hold jobs. The numbers help when deciding how to split care with siblings or whether to pay for help.

Your cost of caregiving vs. paying for help
Lost payLost retirement matchOut of pocketPaid home care
Lost pay = paid hours given up × hourly pay × 52 weeks × years; lost match = that pay × the match rate; out of pocket defaults to AARP's average of about $7,200 a year. Paid help = your caregiving hours × the 2025 national median of $35 an hour for a home caregiver. Ignores raises and promotions forgone, lost Social Security credits, the growth the missing retirement money would have earned, and taxes; the first three make the caregiver's real cost higher.
FMLA

Unpaid, job-protected leave

The federal Family and Medical Leave Act gives up to 12 weeks a year of unpaid, job-protected leave to care for a parent with a serious health condition, at employers with 50 or more workers, once you've worked there a year and 1,250 hours. It can be taken in pieces, a day or a few hours at a time. In-laws aren't covered.

PAID LEAVE

Paid family leave in 13 states and D.C.

California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Maine, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Washington and D.C. run paid family leave programs that can replace part of your pay while caring for a seriously ill parent; New Hampshire has a voluntary program, and Maryland and Virginia start in 2028. Check your state's rules on who counts as family.

AT WORK

Employer benefits people forget

Employee assistance programs (often including elder care consultants), backup care benefits, flexible schedules, and the dependent care FSA, which can pay for adult day care for a parent who lives with you. Ask HR before cutting hours.

YOU

Protect your own future

Keep contributing at least enough for the employer match, keep your own emergency fund, and don't co-sign loans or drain your retirement accounts. A financially secure caregiver can help for longer.

Support the tax code recognizes

Tax breaks for supporting a parent

If you help pay for a parent's living costs, you may be able to claim them as a dependent, deduct their medical bills, file as head of household, or use the dependent care credit. Answer a few questions to see which might apply.

Which breaks could you qualify for? (2026)
Simplified from IRS rules for 2026: a parent is a "qualifying relative" if their gross income is under $5,300 (tax-free Social Security doesn't count) and you provide more than half their support; parents don't have to live with you. The medical deduction can include a parent's bills even if their income is too high, as long as you provide over half their support. Assumes your parent is a U.S. citizen or resident and isn't claimed by anyone else. Confirm with tax software or a preparer.

The people who target them

Protecting a parent from scams

Older adults reported $7.7 billion in losses to the FBI's internet crime center in 2025, about 60% more than a year earlier, and most fraud is never reported. Impostor, tech-support, investment, crypto and romance scams lead the list, and AI-generated voices make "grandchild in trouble" calls more convincing.

Settings

Make them a harder target

Freeze their credit at all three bureaus; add a trusted contact at each financial firm; turn on transaction alerts to your phone too; set up call blocking and a "family password" for emergencies.

Rules

Three rules to repeat

No real company or agency asks to be paid in gift cards, wire transfers or cryptocurrency. Never move money because someone calls or texts. Hang up, then call back on a number you already know.

If it happens

Act fast

Call the bank or card company immediately, then report to ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov, and to Adult Protective Services if someone close is exploiting them. The AARP Fraud Watch Network helpline (877-908-3360) offers free support.

Exploitation by people a parent knows, such as a new caregiver, a neighbor or a relative, is common and harder to spot. Regular, friendly check-ins on statements, done with your parent rather than behind their back, catch most problems early. Spotting the Sales Pitch covers the legal-but-costly products often sold to retirees.

Families are teams, sometimes

Sharing the load with siblings

ROLES

Divide by strengths and distance

The sibling nearby often does hands-on care; one who's organized can handle bills and paperwork remotely; others can contribute money, research, or regular visits that give the main caregiver a break.

OPEN BOOKS

Keep everything transparent

A shared spreadsheet of a parent's accounts and spending, and regular family check-ins, prevent the suspicion that tears families apart later. Whoever holds the power of attorney should keep receipts for everything.

FAIRNESS

Recognize unequal effort

If one child provides most of the care, siblings can share costs, the parent can pay that child under a personal care agreement, or the parent can adjust their will. Agreeing on this openly is far better than surprises after a death.

TAXES

Multiple support agreements

When siblings together pay more than half of a parent's support but no one pays more than half alone, a multiple support agreement (IRS Form 2120) lets one sibling who pays over 10% claim the parent; you can rotate who claims each year.

The bigger picture

Where this fits in the bigger picture

On the Money River, the Family thread runs both ways: in your 30s and 40s you may be supporting children and parents at once (the "sandwich generation"). On the Money Roadmap, that's the peak-earning stage, when your own retirement saving matters most. Helping a parent shouldn't mean giving up your own plan.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

My parent refuses to discuss money. What can I do?

Start smaller: ask them to name a trusted contact at their bank, or to put the location of their documents somewhere you can find it. Involve a doctor, lawyer or advisor they trust. If you see signs of exploitation or that they can't manage safely, Adult Protective Services can assess the situation.

Is it too late for a power of attorney if they have dementia?

Not necessarily. Someone in the early stages may still have the capacity to sign; an elder-law attorney can assess it, sometimes with a doctor's letter. If capacity is gone, a court guardianship or conservatorship is usually the only route.

Should my parent move in with us?

It can save money and keep them close, but consider the physical setup (stairs, bathrooms), the strain on your household, and whether they'd pay rent or share costs. Put money arrangements in writing so siblings understand and Medicaid sees fair dealing.

Am I responsible for my parent's debts?

Generally no. Debts are paid from their estate; children don't inherit them unless they co-signed. Be careful when signing nursing home admission papers not to sign as a "responsible party" who guarantees payment.

Can my parent give me money to protect it from a nursing home?

Gifts within five years before applying for Medicaid can delay eligibility, and they make the money yours (subject to your creditors and divorce). Talk to an elder-law attorney before moving assets.

How do I care for a parent from far away?

Hire a geriatric care manager (an aging life care professional) for a local assessment, build a network of neighbors and friends, use shared online calendars and the Area Agency on Aging, and plan visits around doctor appointments.

Reference

Glossary

Durable power of attorney
A document naming an agent to handle someone's finances, lasting through incapacity.
Health care proxy
The person authorized to make medical decisions if someone can't.
HIPAA release
Permission for doctors to share health information with named people.
Representative payee
A person or organization appointed by Social Security to manage someone's benefits.
Trusted contact
A person a financial firm may contact about suspected exploitation; no account access.
Guardianship / conservatorship
A court appointment to make personal or financial decisions for someone who can't.
Area Agency on Aging
A local agency that connects older adults and caregivers to services.
Respite care
Short-term care that gives a family caregiver a break.
PACE
Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly: combined medical and long-term care for people 55+.
Personal care agreement
A written contract for paying a family member to provide care.
FMLA
Family and Medical Leave Act: up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave.
Qualifying relative
A dependent other than a child, such as a parent you support, for tax purposes.
Multiple support agreement
An IRS form letting one of several supporting siblings claim a parent.
Adult Protective Services
State agencies that investigate abuse, neglect and exploitation of vulnerable adults.
Sandwich generation
Adults caring for both their children and their parents.
