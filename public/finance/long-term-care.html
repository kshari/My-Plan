Long-Term Care: What It Costs, Who Pays, and Whether to Insure It

The odds you'll need help with daily living, what that care costs in 2026, why Medicare mostly doesn't pay and how Medicaid does, and an honest comparison of self-funding, traditional long-term care insurance and the hybrid life-and-LTC policies that dominate today's sales pitches. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

Long-term care means help with everyday tasks (bathing, dressing, eating, getting around) or supervision for dementia. It isn't medical care, so Medicare and health insurance mostly don't pay for it. About half of people turning 65 will need significant care at some point, usually for a couple of years, but about 1 in 7 will need it for more than five years. In 2025 the national median was $129,575 a year for a private nursing-home room, $74,400 for assisted living and about $80,000 for full-time help at home. Families pay about half of all long-term care costs themselves; Medicaid pays once savings are nearly gone. Broadly: people with modest savings will likely rely on Medicaid; people with several million dollars can usually self-fund; and the people in between are the ones for whom insurance is worth a serious look. If you buy, compare a plain traditional policy with inflation protection against a hybrid, understand that premiums on traditional policies can rise, and be wary of any pitch that promises you "can't lose."

What long-term care is, and the odds you'll need it

Long-term care is help with the basics of daily life when illness, injury, frailty or dementia makes them hard to do alone. Insurers and Medicaid measure it by six "activities of daily living" (bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, continence and transferring in and out of a bed or chair) plus severe cognitive impairment. Most of it happens at home, from family.

Those figures come from the federal government's long-term services research (ASPE), and they show both sides of the risk: about 48% of people never need significant care, while a minority need many years of it. Women need care longer, largely because they live longer and are more often widowed. Families end up paying about half of all long-term care costs out of pocket, and about 63 million Americans now provide unpaid care to a family member or friend.

The big misunderstanding

Who pays: Medicare, Medicaid and you

MEDICARE

Medicare pays for recovery, not ongoing care

After a hospital stay of at least three days as an admitted inpatient (time "under observation" doesn't count), Medicare covers skilled nursing or rehab: days 1–20 in full, days 21–100 with a coinsurance of $217 a day in 2026, and nothing after day 100. It also covers part-time skilled home health and hospice. It does not pay for help with bathing, dressing or supervision, which is most long-term care. See Social Security & Medicare.

MEDICAID

Medicaid pays once your money is nearly gone

Medicaid is the largest payer of long-term care, including nursing homes and, in most states, some home and community care. To qualify, a single person usually must spend countable assets down to about $2,000 (varies by state). Your home is usually exempt while you or a spouse lives there, up to an equity limit of $752,000 to $1,130,000 in 2026, depending on the state; a 2025 law caps it at a flat $1 million starting in 2028 for states above that level, with exceptions such as when a spouse lives there.

SPOUSES

Protection for the spouse at home

When one spouse needs nursing-home care, the other can keep the home, a car, and a share of the couple's savings (up to $162,660 in 2026, depending on the state) plus enough of the couple's income to bring them up to a monthly allowance (as much as $4,066.50 in 2026).

LOOK-BACK

The five-year look-back

Medicaid reviews gifts and below-value transfers made in the 60 months before you apply; giving money away to qualify triggers a waiting period. After death, states can also seek repayment from the estate, often from the house. Planning around these rules is legal but technical; use an elder-law attorney, not a salesperson.

OTHER

Veterans and state programs

Wartime veterans and surviving spouses with limited income may qualify for the VA's Aid and Attendance pension. Washington state's WA Cares Fund, the first public long-term care insurance program, began paying benefits on July 1, 2026, up to $36,500 per person, funded by a 0.58% payroll premium; other states are studying similar programs.

The core trade-off

Insure or self-fund?

Insurance is a bet you hope to lose: you pay premiums so that a long, expensive stay doesn't wreck the plan. The fair comparison is the benefit the policy would pay against what the same premiums would grow to if you invested them yourself instead.

A traditional policy vs. investing the premiums
Policy benefit poolPremiums invested instead
Default premium: AALTCI's 2026 average for a single 55-year-old woman, $165,000 pool with 3% compound growth (a man of the same age averaged $2,200). Premiums are assumed level and stop when care begins (most policies waive them during a claim); real policies can raise premiums. The pool is what the policy could pay in total; it pays out over time as you use care. Investing assumes steady returns, before taxes.
Modest savings

Medicaid is the backstop

If your savings (outside the home) are under about $100,000–$200,000, premiums may strain your budget and Medicaid would likely step in anyway. Focus on a care plan and the paperwork.

In the middle

Insurance is worth pricing

Households with a few hundred thousand to a couple of million dollars have the most to protect, especially a spouse who'd be left behind. Consider covering part of the risk, such as two to three years of care.

Substantial wealth

Self-funding usually works

With several million dollars, a long stay is expensive but survivable. Some still buy coverage to protect heirs or for peace of mind; it's a choice, not a necessity.

Reading the fine print

If you buy a policy: what matters

WHEN

Mid-50s to mid-60s is the usual window

Premiums rise with age, and the chance of being declined for health reasons rises faster. Waiting until you need it doesn't work: you can't buy coverage once you're already having trouble with daily activities.

BENEFIT

Daily or monthly benefit, and the total pool

Size it to cover part of local care costs, not necessarily all; income and savings can fill the rest. A pool of two to three years of benefits covers most claims.

INFLATION

Inflation protection matters most

If you buy at 55 and claim at 85, a policy with no growth pays in 2026 dollars for 2056 prices. Compound growth of 3% roughly doubles the benefit over 25 years; it also more than doubles the premium.

WAIT

Elimination period

The waiting period before benefits start, often 90 days. A longer wait lowers the premium; plan to pay for that period yourself (Medicare may cover part of it after a hospital stay).

COUPLES

Shared care

A shared-care rider lets spouses draw on each other's pools, useful because one partner often needs much more care than the other.

PARTNERSHIP

State partnership policies

In most states, a "partnership-qualified" policy lets you keep extra assets, dollar for dollar with what the policy paid, if you later need Medicaid. Ask whether a policy qualifies in your state.

INSURER

The company and its rate history

Choose a financially strong insurer and ask about its history of premium increases on older policies. If your premium does rise, you can usually keep it level by trimming benefits (a shorter benefit period or less inflation growth) rather than dropping the policy.

Heavy selling ahead

Pitches to be careful with

Long-term care is a fear-driven purchase, and hybrid products pay some of the highest commissions in insurance. The Sales Pitch episode's questions apply in full. A few specific ones:

You hearAsk
"With a hybrid, you never lose: if you don't need care, your heirs get the money."Compared with what? What would a traditional policy plus investing the difference leave? How much is the death benefit per dollar of premium, and what's the inflation protection?
"Move your IRA or annuity into this policy."What are the taxes and surrender charges to move it? What's the commission?
"Replace your old LTC policy; this one is better."Older policies were often priced more generously. You'd re-qualify at your current age and health. Get the old insurer's options in writing first.
"Give your assets away now so Medicaid pays."The five-year look-back can leave you ineligible exactly when you need care. Talk to an independent elder-law attorney.
"This is a one-time offer; prices go up next month."Premiums do rise with age, by your birthday, not by a sales deadline. Take the illustration home.

Whatever you decide

A plan, with or without insurance

TALK

Have the conversation early

Where would you want care: at home, near a particular child, in a community? Who would coordinate it? What can family realistically provide? Agreeing on this before a crisis prevents rushed, expensive decisions.

EARMARK

Earmark a care reserve

If self-funding, decide which money is for care: a portion of savings, the home's equity (sold or borrowed against later), or the income a delayed Social Security claim provides. The guaranteed-income floor helps here too.

HOME

Make the home workable

A first-floor bedroom and bathroom, grab bars, no-step entry and good lighting can delay or avoid a move.

PAPERS

Sign the documents

A durable power of attorney and health care proxy let someone act for you without a court guardianship. See Estate Planning Essentials.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Doesn't Medicare cover nursing homes?

Only short stays for skilled care after a qualifying hospital admission, up to 100 days, with a $217-a-day coinsurance from day 21. Long-term custodial care isn't covered. Medicare Advantage plans have the same basic limits.

My parent's LTC premium just went up 40%. Should they drop it?

Usually not outright. Ask the insurer for the options: reducing the daily benefit, shortening the benefit period or lowering the inflation growth can hold the premium steady while keeping meaningful coverage. Dropping it forfeits years of premiums, and a new policy at their age would cost far more if they could qualify at all.

Is a hybrid policy better than traditional?

It's different, not better. Hybrids guarantee premiums and pay something either way, which many people value; traditional policies buy more care per dollar. Compare the same care pool, inflation protection and total cost, and ask what you'd have if you bought traditional and invested the difference.

Can I use home equity for care?

Yes: selling the home, a reverse mortgage while one spouse stays home, or a home-equity line. Keeping the home as the "care reserve" is a common self-funding plan, as long as everyone understands that's its purpose.

Will my family have to pay for my nursing home?

Adult children generally aren't responsible for a parent's care bills, though a few states have "filial responsibility" laws that are rarely enforced. Don't sign admission papers as a "responsible party" for someone else without reading what it commits you to.

I'm 72 with some health issues. Is it too late?

Traditional coverage may be hard to get or expensive. Short-term care policies, annuity/LTC hybrids with simpler underwriting, or a self-funding plan around home equity are the usual alternatives. Get an independent quote before ruling it in or out.

Reference

Glossary

Long-term care (LTSS)
Help with daily activities or supervision over an extended time; "long-term services and supports."
Activities of daily living (ADLs)
Bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, continence and transferring; needing help with two usually triggers benefits.
Custodial care
Non-medical help with daily living; what Medicare doesn't cover.
Skilled care
Care needing a nurse or therapist; Medicare covers it short-term.
Assisted living
Housing with help for daily activities, less medical than a nursing home.
Benefit pool
The total a policy can pay over its life.
Elimination period
The waiting period before a policy starts paying.
Inflation protection
A rider that raises the benefit each year, often 3% compound.
Hybrid (linked-benefit) policy
Life insurance or an annuity whose value can pay for long-term care.
Shared care
A rider letting spouses use each other's benefits.
Partnership policy
A state-approved policy that protects extra assets if you later need Medicaid.
Spend-down
Using up assets until you meet Medicaid's limit.
Look-back period
The five years of transfers Medicaid reviews when you apply.
Estate recovery
A state recouping Medicaid costs from a recipient's estate after death.
CSRA
Community spouse resource allowance: savings the at-home spouse may keep.
Tax-qualified policy
A policy meeting federal rules so premiums can be deductible and benefits tax-free.
