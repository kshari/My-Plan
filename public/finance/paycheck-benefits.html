Paycheck & Benefits: Reading Your Pay Stub and Choosing at Open Enrollment

Where each dollar of your paycheck goes, how to fill out a W-4 so April holds no surprises, the open-enrollment choices worth real money, getting the most from your 401(k), and how stock pay like ESPPs and RSUs actually works. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

Your paycheck is a monthly report card on your finances: gross pay, minus pre-tax savings and benefits, minus taxes, minus after-tax deductions, equals take-home pay. Because pre-tax money skips income tax, saving an extra dollar costs you only about 70–80 cents of take-home pay. Fill out the W-4 carefully (the 2026 form adds lines for the new tip, overtime and senior deductions) and check it with the IRS withholding estimator after any big change. At open enrollment, the choices that matter most are the health plan, the HSA or FSA ($4,400 / $8,750 HSA limits in 2026), disability insurance, and contributing at least enough to get the full 401(k) match ($24,500 limit in 2026). An ESPP with a 15% discount is close to free money if you sell promptly; RSUs are taxed as pay when they vest, and keeping too much in one company's stock is the risk to manage.

Reading a pay stub

Every pay stub follows the same order, from the top line (what you earned) to the bottom line (what reached your bank account). Read yours line by line at least once a year, and every time something changes.

1

Gross pay

Salary for the period, or hours × rate, plus overtime, bonuses and commissions.

2

Pre-tax deductions

Traditional 401(k), health, dental and vision premiums, HSA and FSA contributions, commuter benefits.

3

Taxes

Federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), and state and local income tax where they apply.

4

After-tax deductions

Roth 401(k), ESPP purchases, some insurance, union dues, garnishments.

Follow the money

Where your paycheck goes

Enter your pay and elections to see how one paycheck splits between take-home pay, your future, and taxes, and what saving a little more would really cost you.

One paycheck, split up (2026)
Federal withholding is estimated as your yearly 2026 tax (standard deduction and brackets, no credits or other income) spread evenly across paychecks, which is roughly what a W-4 with no extra entries produces. Health premiums and HSA/FSA contributions are assumed pre-tax for all taxes; a traditional 401(k) reduces income tax but not Social Security and Medicare. State and local taxes are a flat rate on wages after pre-tax deductions; real rules vary (some cities tax 401(k) contributions). Your actual stub will differ a little.

Neither a refund nor a bill

The W-4: getting withholding right

The W-4 tells your employer how much federal tax to withhold. The goal is to come out close to even in April: a big refund means you lent the government money for free, and a big bill can come with a penalty.

STEP 1

Personal information and filing status

Single, married filing jointly, or head of household. With nothing else filled in, withholding assumes one job and the standard deduction.

STEP 2

Two jobs, or two earners

The most common cause of a surprise tax bill. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator, or check the box in Step 2(c) on both jobs' W-4s if pay is similar.

STEP 3

Credits

The child tax credit ($2,200 per child under 17 in 2026) and credit for other dependents ($500) reduce withholding.

STEP 4

Other income, deductions, extra withholding

Add side income or investment income so tax is withheld for it, deductions beyond the standard one, and any extra amount per paycheck. The 2026 form's deductions worksheet adds lines for the temporary deductions for qualified tips (up to $25,000), overtime (up to $12,500, or $25,000 joint), car loan interest (up to $10,000) and the $6,000 senior deduction.

When to redo it: a new job, marriage or divorce, a new child, a second job or a spouse starting work, a big raise or bonus, starting to receive tips or overtime, or a tax-time surprise. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/w4app walks you through it using your latest pay stub. Taxes 101 explains how the tax itself is figured.

Free money first

Your workplace retirement plan

MATCH

Always get the full match

A common formula is 50 cents per dollar on the first 6% of pay. Not contributing enough to get it is turning down part of your salary. It's step 02 of the Roadmap.

LIMITS

2026 limits

$24,500 of your own contributions (traditional and Roth combined), plus $8,000 more at 50 or older, or $11,250 more at ages 60–63 if your plan allows. Starting in 2026, if you earned more than $150,000 in Social Security wages the year before, your catch-up contributions must go in as Roth.

VESTING

Know your vesting schedule

Your own contributions are always yours; employer contributions may vest over several years. Check before you leave a job: a few months can be worth thousands.

AUTO

Turn on auto-increase

Raising your rate 1% a year, ideally timed with raises, gets most people to 15% total savings without feeling it.

CHOICES

Traditional or Roth, and what to invest in

See Which Account? for the tax choice; for investments, a low-cost target-date or index fund is the honest baseline.

Equity compensation

Stock pay: ESPPs and RSUs

Company stock can be a valuable part of pay, and a hidden risk: your job and your savings end up riding on the same company. A common guideline is to keep no more than 10% of your investments in your employer's stock.

ESPP

Employee stock purchase plans

You contribute from each paycheck, after tax, and the plan buys company shares at a discount (up to 15%) at the end of each offering period, often with a "lookback" that uses the lower of the starting or ending price. Purchases are capped at $25,000 of stock (valued at the start) per year. Selling right away locks in the discount; holding longer can qualify for better tax treatment but adds single-stock risk.

RSU

Restricted stock units

Shares granted to you that vest over time. At vesting, their value is ordinary income, taxed like salary, and employers usually withhold federal tax at a flat 22% (37% above $1 million), which is too little for many people in higher brackets. Treat vested RSUs like a cash bonus that happened to arrive as stock: would you buy this much of your employer's stock with that cash?

OPTIONS

Stock options, briefly

The right to buy shares at a set price. Their tax rules (ISOs vs. NSOs, the alternative minimum tax) are complex enough that a session with a tax professional usually pays for itself before exercising.

What an ESPP purchase is worth
Assumes you sell all shares at the purchase-date price. The discount (the gap between what you paid and the market price) is taxed as ordinary income when you sell; any later gain or loss is a capital gain or loss. Your money is invested evenly over the period, so on average it's tied up for about half the period; the annualized figure reflects that. Brokerage fees ignored.

More pay, used well

Raises, bonuses, overtime and tips

RAISES

Save half of every raise

Before it reaches your checking account: raise your 401(k) percentage the same week. You won't miss money you never got used to.

BONUSES

Bonuses look over-taxed, but usually aren't

They're often withheld at a flat 22% federal rate, plus Social Security, Medicare and state tax. That withholding is just a deposit toward your real tax; if it's too much, it comes back as a refund. Decide in advance where a bonus goes: the emergency fund, debt, or savings goals.

OT & TIPS

The new overtime and tip deductions (2025–2028)

Qualified overtime (the extra half in time-and-a-half) is deductible up to $12,500 a year ($25,000 joint), and qualified tips up to $25,000, both phasing out at higher incomes. They reduce income tax, not Social Security and Medicare. Employers now report these amounts separately, and you can reflect them on your W-4.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Is a big tax refund good?

It feels good, but it means too much was withheld all year, an interest-free loan to the government. Adjusting your W-4 puts that money in each paycheck instead, where it can pay down debt or go to savings.

Why did my take-home pay drop in January?

Usually new benefit premiums, a new 401(k) percentage taking effect, or Social Security tax restarting after you hit last year's wage base. Compare December's and January's stubs line by line.

Can I claim "exempt" so no tax is withheld?

Only if you owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year. Otherwise you'll face a tax bill and possibly a penalty.

Should I contribute to the ESPP if money is tight?

If you can afford the paycheck deduction and sell shares promptly, a 15% discount is one of the best deals in benefits. If it would mean carrying a credit card balance, pay the card first.

Do I need the employer's life insurance if I have my own?

The free amount is worth keeping. Extra group coverage is convenient but tied to the job, and often costs more than an individual term policy for healthy people.

I'm a contractor or freelancer. What's different?

No one withholds for you: you pay self-employment tax (15.3% up to the wage base) and quarterly estimated taxes, and you choose your own retirement plan, such as a Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA, and health coverage. See Which Account?.

Reference

Glossary

Gross pay
What you earned before any deductions.
Net pay
What's deposited after taxes and deductions; take-home pay.
Pre-tax deduction
Money taken out before income tax is calculated, such as a traditional 401(k) or health premiums.
FICA
Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) taxes on wages.
Wage base
The earnings cap for Social Security tax: $184,500 in 2026.
W-4
The form telling your employer how much federal tax to withhold.
Withholding
Tax taken from each paycheck as a prepayment of your yearly tax.
Open enrollment
The yearly window to change benefit elections.
Qualifying life event
A change like marriage or a birth that lets you change benefits mid-year.
Employer match
Your employer's contribution to your 401(k), tied to what you contribute.
Vesting
The schedule on which employer contributions or stock become fully yours.
ESPP
Employee stock purchase plan: buying company stock at a discount through payroll.
Lookback
An ESPP feature using the lower of the start or end price.
RSU
Restricted stock unit: company shares that become yours as they vest.
Supplemental wages
Bonuses, commissions and RSU income, often withheld at a flat 22%.
COBRA
The right to continue an employer health plan after leaving, at full cost.
