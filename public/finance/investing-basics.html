Investing Basics: The Honest Baseline

What you actually own when you invest, why time beats timing, why owning the whole market beats picking pieces of it, and what fees really cost — with 98 years of real returns to test every claim against. It ends by defining the simple, low-cost portfolio that every other product in this library has to beat.

TL;DR

Investing is owning businesses (stocks) and lending money (bonds) for long enough that their growth outruns inflation. From 1928 through 2025, U.S. stocks returned about 10% a year (roughly 6.8% after inflation) against 4.5% for 10-year Treasury bonds and 3.4% for T-bills — but stocks fell in 26 of those 98 years, once by 44%. Time is what tames that: no 20-year stretch of the S&P 500 ever lost money, even after inflation. Picking individual stocks is a long shot, because a tiny share of companies produce nearly all the gains, so the honest default is to own all of them through an index fund for a few hundredths of a percent a year. Over 20 years, about 93% of actively managed large-company funds trailed the S&P 500, and costs are the main reason: at 1.64% a year in combined fund and advisor fees, $500 a month for 30 years grows to about $428,000 instead of $579,000. Set the stock/bond mix by when you'll need the money, automate contributions, and rebalance once a year. That simple, cheap, liquid portfolio is the honest baseline: any annuity, insurance policy, or managed product has to beat it after all costs to earn a place in your plan.

What you're actually buying: stocks, bonds and cash

Strip away the jargon and there are only three things most people ever invest in. You can own a slice of a business, you can lend money to a government or a company, or you can park cash somewhere safe and short-term. Nearly every fund, account and product is some mix of those three in a different wrapper.

OWN

Stocks: a share of a business

A share of stock is a small slice of ownership in a company, with a claim on its future profits. When the business grows, the share is worth more, and many companies also pay part of their profits out as dividends. There is no promise attached: if the business struggles the share price falls, and if it fails the share can go to zero. That lack of a promise is exactly why stocks have paid the most over time. Investors demand extra return for carrying the risk.

LEND

Bonds: a loan with a schedule

A bond is a loan. You hand over money, and the borrower (the U.S. Treasury, a city, a company) promises regular interest and your money back on a set date. The promise makes bonds steadier than stocks, but not risk-free: when interest rates rise, older bonds paying lower rates lose value. That is what happened in 2022, when 10-year Treasuries lost 17.8% in the same year stocks fell 18%.

PARK

Cash: short-term and safe, but barely ahead of prices

Savings accounts, money market funds and Treasury bills (very short government loans) almost never lose value, which makes them the right home for an emergency fund or money you'll spend soon. The catch is that over long periods they have only just kept pace with inflation. Cash protects your balance but does little to grow its buying power.

The price of that growth is the ride: stocks fell in more than one year out of four. The rest of this piece is about how to take the growth while surviving the ride.

Risk, measured honestly

Why time matters more than timing

Over one year, the stock market is close to a coin toss with a heavy thumb on the scale. Over twenty years, it has never lost. The chart below takes every possible holding period in the 98-year record and shows the best and worst annual return an investor actually got.

Two lessons fall out of that picture. First, money you need within about five years doesn't belong in stocks. More than one five-year window in ten lost money, and the worst lost 12.7% a year. Second, long-term money that sits out of stocks pays a steep price for comfort. Even measured after inflation, the worst 20-year stretch (1929–1948, through the Depression and a world war) still came out slightly ahead, at +0.6% a year.

That's also why "timing the market" is a trap. The big up years tend to arrive right after the big down years (1933 followed 1931–32; 2009 followed 2008). An investor who sells to wait out the storm has to be right twice: when to get out, and when to get back in. The dependable edge isn't timing. It's staying invested long enough for the range to narrow.

Diversification

One stock is a lottery ticket; the market is the lottery

The S&P 500's 10% a year is the return of owning all of it. Owning a handful of individual companies is a very different bet, because the market's long-run gains come from a surprisingly small number of huge winners.

Of every 100 U.S. stocks listed since 1926…
Accounted for all net wealth createdThe rest, together, about matched T-bills
Hendrik Bessembinder, "Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?" (Journal of Financial Economics, 2018), covering U.S. stocks 1926–2016.

Finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder traced every U.S. stock listed since 1926 and found that about 4% of companies accounted for all of the stock market's net wealth creation. The other 96%, taken together, did no better than one-month Treasury bills. A later global study found the same pattern from 1991 to 2020: 55% of U.S. stocks returned less than T-bills over their lifetimes, and the top 2.4% of companies worldwide produced all $75.7 trillion of net wealth created.

If you own five or ten stocks, the odds are good that you'll miss most of the handful that end up mattering. If you own all of them, you are guaranteed to hold every one of the eventual winners. That's what diversification buys: it doesn't raise the market's average, it makes sure you actually get it. The same logic stretches in three directions:

  • Across companies: thousands, not dozens. A total-market index fund holds essentially every public U.S. company.
  • Across countries: U.S. stocks are close to two-thirds of the world's stock market value (about 64% of the MSCI All Country World Index in September 2026), and leadership between the U.S. and the rest of the world has swung back and forth over the decades. An international fund covers the other part.
  • Across asset types: bonds and cash don't fall with stocks in most bad years (2022 was the painful exception), which is what makes the stock/bond dial further down work.

The vehicle

Index funds: owning the whole haystack

An index fund doesn't try to find the needle. It buys the entire haystack, meaning every stock (or bond) in a published list like the S&P 500, in proportion to its size, and simply holds it. No research team, no forecasts, very little trading. That's why it can charge almost nothing.

Index funds come in two wrappers that hold the same kinds of portfolios. Mutual funds trade once a day at the closing price and are what most 401(k) plans offer. ETFs (exchange-traded funds) trade all day like a stock and tend to be a little more tax-efficient in a regular brokerage account. For a long-term investor the difference is minor. What matters is what the fund holds and what it charges.

Why the "boring" option keeps winning

There's a piece of simple arithmetic behind it, first spelled out by Nobel laureate William Sharpe. Before costs, all the money invested in U.S. stocks is the market, so the average actively managed dollar has to earn the market's return, no better. Active funds then charge more, trade more, and hold some cash, so after costs, the average active dollar has to trail the index. Some managers will beat it in any given year, but picking which ones in advance is the hard part, and winners rarely stay winners.

The cost

What fees really cost

A fee of "only 1%" sounds small because it's quoted against your balance. Measured against what your money earns each year, and compounded over a working life, it's one of the biggest numbers in personal finance. Move the sliders to see it on your own figures.

Same savings, same market, three price tags
Index fund · 0.05% Average active fund · 0.64% Active fund + 1% advisor · 1.64%
Monthly contributions, monthly compounding, a steady return every year, fees deducted continuously. Real markets are bumpy, but fees are charged in good years and bad ones alike, so the gap holds up.

At the default settings ($500 a month for 30 years at 7%), the index fund ends near $579,000, the average active fund near $516,000, and the active fund with a 1% advisor on top near $428,000. That's about $151,000, or a quarter of the index result, handed over in fees, on $180,000 of contributions. Put $100,000 in once and leave it for 30 years, and the 1.64% version finishes about 38% behind.

Where costs hide

The expense ratio is only the most visible layer. When you compare anything against the baseline, add up all of them:

  • Fund expense ratio, taken out of the fund's returns every day. Index stock funds averaged 0.05% in 2025, actively managed stock funds 0.64%, and target-date funds 0.27%.
  • Advisory fee, often charged as a percentage of your balance each year (about 1% is common). It's paid on top of the funds' own fees.
  • Sales charges and commissions: front-end "loads" on some mutual funds, and commissions built into insurance and annuity products.
  • Costs built into the product: caps, participation rates and spreads on indexed annuities and IUL, cost-of-insurance charges, rider fees. They never appear as an "expense ratio" but reduce what you get just the same. Annuities Explained and Life Insurance Explained put numbers on them.
  • Exit costs: surrender charges that lock money in for years.
  • Tax drag: high-turnover funds in a taxable account pass capital gains through to you every year. Taxes 101 (coming next in this tier) covers this.
The one rule to remember: future returns are uncertain, but fees are guaranteed. Every dollar of cost comes straight out of your return, every year, whether the market is up or down.

The main decision you control

The stock/bond dial

Once you own the whole market cheaply, almost everything left comes down to one setting: how much goes in stocks and how much in bonds. More stocks means more growth and deeper drops. More bonds means a smoother ride and less growth. Turn the dial and see what each mix actually did from 1928 to 2025.

A few settings worth trying. At 100% stocks, $10,000 in 1928 grew past $115 million, but you'd have lived through a 44% loss in 1931 and 37% in 2008. At 60/40, it grew to about $25.7 million, the 2008 loss shrank to about 14%, and the worst year was 27%. At 0% stocks, the worst year was only 18%, but the ending balance was 1/150th of the all-stock result.

No mix is "right" in the abstract. The honest way to set the dial is by when you'll need the money and how much of a drop you can sit through without selling. The second one matters as much as the first, because the worst outcome is picking an aggressive mix, panicking in a crash, and locking in the loss.

Putting it together

The honest baseline, built three ways

Everything above comes together in one simple portfolio: own the whole market through index funds, set the stock/bond mix by your timeline, keep costs near zero, and leave it alone. There are three common ways to build it, and they differ in convenience, not in substance.

Simplest

One target-date index fund

One fund holds U.S. stocks, international stocks and bonds, and rebalances and shifts its mix for you over time. Pick the index version: the average target-date fund charged 0.27% in 2025, and low-cost index series charge a fraction of that. Hard to beat inside a 401(k).

More control

A three-fund portfolio

A U.S. total stock market index fund, an international total stock index fund and a U.S. total bond index fund, in the mix you choose. The cheapest and most flexible version, but you do the rebalancing, about once a year.

Hands-off

A balanced fund or robo-advisor

A fixed-mix balanced index fund, or an automated service that builds and rebalances an index portfolio for you. Robo-advisors add a management fee on top of the funds, so check the all-in cost against the other two.

Habits that matter more than the fund you pick

AUTO

Automate contributions

Money that moves on payday, whether through a 401(k) deduction or an automatic transfer, gets invested whether the headlines are good or bad. Investing a fixed amount on a schedule also buys more shares when prices are low, which is called dollar-cost averaging.

LUMP

Got a lump sum? Investing it sooner usually wins

Vanguard compared investing a windfall all at once against spreading it over a year. In the U.S. (1979–2022), investing it right away came out ahead 73.7% of the time, simply because markets rise more often than they fall. Spreading it out is a fine choice if it's what gets you to invest at all. Just set an end date.

ONCE

Rebalance once a year, then look away

Once a year, move money from whatever grew past its target back to what fell behind. It keeps your risk where you chose it, and it makes you sell high and buy low without having to predict anything. Checking the balance daily adds worry and nothing else.

Why this piece comes first

Using the baseline to judge everything else

This is the benchmark the rest of the library uses. Annuities, cash-value life insurance and managed accounts are each sold as an improvement on "just investing." The fair test is whether they beat this simple portfolio after all their costs, for your specific need.

Five questions to ask about any financial product:

  • What does it cost all-in, per year? Include fund, advisory, rider and insurance charges, and any cost built into caps or spreads.
  • What do I give up in a great year, and what am I protected from in a bad one? Then check whether that protection is worth the price, using the "time" chart above.
  • How and when can I get my money out, and what does leaving early cost?
  • How is the person recommending it paid? A commission ties the pay to the sale, whether or not the product fits.
  • What is it being compared to? If the answer is a savings account or "the market with no floor," ask to see it compared with a low-cost index portfolio at your own stock/bond mix.

The bigger picture

Where investing fits in the bigger picture

Investing isn't step one. On the Money Roadmap, money goes to a few safer, higher-return uses first: a starter emergency fund, any employer 401(k) match (an instant 50%–100% return), and paying off high-interest debt, since a credit card charging 20%+ beats any honest investment return. After that, long-term money goes into the baseline portfolio described here.

Do this

A getting-started checklist

Cover the basics first

A starter emergency fund in cash, and no credit card balances. Investing money you may need next month forces you to sell at the wrong time.

Take the full employer match

Contribute at least enough to your 401(k) to get every matching dollar. It's the best return available anywhere.

Choose your stock/bond mix by date

Use the dial above: when will you need this money, and what drop could you sit through without selling?

Pick one of the three baseline builds

Target-date index fund, three-fund portfolio, or a balanced fund or robo-advisor. Look for broad index funds.

Check every expense ratio

Look up each fund's expense ratio. For broad index funds, anything much above 0.2% deserves a question. In a 401(k), the plan's index options are often the cheapest on the menu.

Automate it

Payroll deduction or an automatic monthly transfer. Raise it whenever your pay goes up.

Put a yearly check-up on the calendar

Rebalance to your target, confirm your beneficiaries, and skip the daily balance checks.

Hold every pitch up to the baseline

Before buying any product sold as "better than the market," ask the five questions above.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Is now a bad time to start? The market seems high.

The market is at or near an all-time high a large share of the time, because over long periods it mostly goes up. Waiting for a drop means sitting in cash that has barely beaten inflation, and nobody reliably knows when the drop will come. Vanguard's research found that investing a lump sum right away beat spreading it out about three times in four. If you're nervous, automatic monthly investing is a fine compromise. The worst option is waiting indefinitely.

Can I just pick a few great companies I believe in?

You can with a small "fun money" slice. But the research above shows that most individual stocks trail even T-bills over their lifetimes, and a few rare winners drive the market's gains. Owning a few stocks makes it very likely you'll miss those winners. Owning the index guarantees you'll hold them.

ETF or mutual fund?

For a long-term investor it matters much less than cost and what the fund holds. Inside a 401(k) you'll usually get mutual funds, and that's fine. In a regular brokerage account, ETFs are often a bit more tax-efficient. Either way, a broad index fund with a very low expense ratio is what you're looking for.

Do I need international stocks?

U.S. stocks have led the world for most of the past 15 years, but not in every era, and nobody knows which region will lead next. Owning both spreads your bet across the whole global economy. Target-date funds already include international stocks. In a three-fund portfolio, a common range is 20%–40% of the stock portion.

What about gold, crypto, or real estate?

Gold jumped about 66% in 2025 and has also gone through long stretches with no real gain. Unlike stocks and bonds, it produces no profits or interest. Crypto is even more volatile and has a much shorter history. Neither is part of the baseline. If you want some, treat it as a small side bet you could lose. Your home is real estate exposure already, and broad stock index funds include real estate companies.

What if the market crashes right after I invest?

At some point it will. Stocks fell in 26 of the last 98 years. The table in "Why time matters" is the answer: long holding periods have always recovered, and money you'll need within a few years shouldn't be in stocks in the first place. A crash only turns into a permanent loss if you sell.

Do I need a financial advisor?

Not to run a baseline portfolio. It's designed to be simple. Advice is worth paying for when it covers more than picking funds: a full plan, taxes, insurance, estate questions, or keeping you from panic-selling. If you do hire help, look for a fee-only fiduciary, and consider paying a flat or hourly fee rather than 1% of your balance every year. Spotting the Sales Pitch & Working with Advisors (Tier 1, #4) covers how.

Why does this page use 10-year Treasuries as "bonds"?

They're the longest clean bond series available back to 1928. Most people own a total bond market fund instead, which mixes government and corporate bonds of different maturities. Its returns over any given stretch will differ somewhat, but the same principles apply.

Glossary

Stock (equity)
A share of ownership in a company, with a claim on its future profits.
Bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns your money on a set date.
Treasury bill (T-bill)
A short-term U.S. government loan (one year or less), the standard measure of a "safe" return on cash.
Dividend
Part of a company's profit paid out to shareholders.
Index
A published list of investments, like the S&P 500, used to measure a market.
Index fund
A fund that holds everything in an index, in proportion to its size, instead of picking investments.
Actively managed fund
A fund whose managers pick investments to try to beat an index.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund: a fund that trades all day on an exchange like a stock.
Mutual fund
A pooled fund bought and sold once a day at its closing value.
Expense ratio
A fund's yearly cost as a percentage of your investment, taken out of returns automatically.
Advisory (AUM) fee
A yearly fee charged as a percentage of the assets an advisor manages, on top of fund costs.
Asset allocation
How your money is split between stocks, bonds and cash.
Diversification
Spreading money across many investments so no single failure does much damage.
Rebalancing
Periodically moving money back to your target mix after markets have shifted it.
Target-date fund
A single fund that holds a mix of stocks and bonds and gradually shifts toward bonds as a chosen year approaches.
Three-fund portfolio
U.S. stock, international stock and bond index funds, combined in a chosen mix.
Annualized (compound) return
The steady yearly rate that would turn the starting amount into the ending amount.
Real return
Return after inflation, which measures growth in actual buying power.
Dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule, regardless of price.
Fiduciary
An advisor legally required to put your interests ahead of their own.
