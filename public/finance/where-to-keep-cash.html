Where to Keep Your Cash: Emergency Funds, Savings, T-Bills and CDs

How big your emergency fund should be, where to hold it so it earns something without taking risk, and how high-yield savings, money market funds, Treasury bills, CDs and I bonds compare once taxes and inflation take their share. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

Cash has one job: to be there, at full value, when you need it. Keep a month of bills in checking, then build an emergency fund of about 3–6 months of essential expenses (more if your income is irregular or you're the only earner). The average U.S. savings account pays 0.37%, while top online savings accounts pay about 4%, and 6-month Treasury bills about 4.3% with no state income tax, so where you park cash is worth hundreds of dollars a year on a typical emergency fund. Keep bank deposits under the $250,000 FDIC limit per bank and ownership category, and check that "cash" in a brokerage or app isn't sitting in a sweep paying almost nothing. The Fed raised rates in September 2026 and inflation is 3.4%, so even the best cash roughly treads water after tax; that's fine, because growth is what your investments are for. Money you'll need within about five years (a down payment, a car, tuition) belongs here too, not in stocks.

Start here

The three jobs of cash

Cash isn't an investment that's supposed to grow. It's the part of your money that must not shrink when you need it: when the car breaks, the job ends, or the house closes. Stocks can fall 30% in a bad year (see Investing Basics), which is fine for money you won't touch for a decade and a disaster for next month's rent. Give each dollar of cash one of three jobs.

SPEND

Checking: this month's bills, plus a cushion

Enough to cover a month of spending so a bill landing early doesn't cause an overdraft. Checking usually pays next to nothing, so don't keep much more than that.

RESCUE

Emergency fund: surprises and income loss

Three to six months of essential expenses (housing, food, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, transportation), held somewhere safe that you can reach within a day or two. Start with $1,000–$2,000, then grow it once the employer match and high-interest debt are handled (steps 01 and 04 of the Roadmap).

SOON

Goal money: anything you'll spend within about five years

A down payment, a wedding, a car, next year's tuition, a tax bill. The date is known and the amount can't be cut if markets fall, so it belongs in savings, Treasury bills or CDs matched to when you'll need it.

Most people aren't there yet, and that's the point of starting. In Bankrate's 2026 emergency savings survey, 47% of U.S. adults said they could cover a $1,000 emergency from savings, 46% had three months of expenses saved, and 24% had no emergency savings at all. More than a quarter had more credit card debt than emergency savings.

Your number

How big should your emergency fund be?

"Three to six months" is a range, not a rule. The right number depends on how likely your income is to stop, how long it would take to replace, and how many big bills could hit at once. Start from your essential monthly spending, not your total spending: in a real emergency, restaurants and vacations stop first.

Your emergency fund target, and where each layer goes
Target months start at 3 and add: one steady income +1, bonus or commission pay +2, self-employed +3; dependents +1; home or older car +1; long job search +2; capped at 12. Add your health plan's deductible (or out-of-pocket maximum) if your HSA doesn't already cover it. Layers fill in order: a starter fund of $2,000, the rest of the first three months in instant-access savings, and anything beyond three months in a Treasury bill or CD ladder. A rule of thumb, not a guarantee.

With the defaults (two steady incomes, kids, a house), the target is 5 months, $22,500. At $750 a month on top of the $6,000 already saved, it's fully funded in about 22 months. Two things can shrink the number: a disability policy that replaces income if you can't work, and a Roth IRA, whose contributions can be withdrawn at any time as a last-resort backup. Neither replaces the first three months of cash.

Taxes and inflation take a share

What you actually keep

Interest from savings, money market funds and CDs is taxed as ordinary income, by the IRS and your state. Treasury bill interest is taxed only by the IRS. So a Treasury bill paying a little less on paper can leave more in your pocket, especially in a high-tax state. Plug in your own brackets (see Taxes 101) and today's rates.

After-tax yield = yield × (1 − federal rate − state rate) for savings, money market funds and CDs; yield × (1 − federal rate) for Treasury bills. Ignores the federal deduction for state taxes, local income taxes, and the portion of some Treasury-only money market funds that is state-tax-free. Rates are late-September 2026 examples; savings and money market rates float, while CD and T-bill rates are locked for the term.

At the defaults, the Treasury bill keeps about 3.36% after tax and high-yield savings about 3.03%, while big-bank savings keeps 0.27%. On $25,000 that's roughly $770 a year of difference between the best option and the worst, for the same safety. Against 3.4% inflation, even the best choice about breaks even. That's the honest trade: cash protects your spending power in a crisis; it doesn't grow it. The growth job belongs to your long-term investments.

Floating or fixed

Locking in a rate: CDs, T-bills and ladders

Savings accounts and money market funds float: their rates follow the Fed up and down within weeks. CDs and Treasury bills lock a rate for their term. Neither choice is smarter in general; they're bets on where rates go, and nobody predicts that reliably.

Rates rising

Floating wins

Your savings rate climbs with each Fed increase, and short T-bills roll into higher rates every few months. The Fed raised its target to 3.75%–4% on September 16, 2026, and the 1-year T-bill (4.49%) yields more than the 3-month (4.04%), a sign markets expect rates to stay higher for a while.

Rates flat

About a tie

Locked rates are usually a little higher than floating ones, which pays you for giving up access. For money with a known date, that small extra is worth taking.

Rates falling

Locking wins

A 1- or 2-year CD keeps paying its rate while savings accounts drop. That's what happened to savers who locked in 2023–2024 before the 2024–2025 cuts.

A ladder: don't bet, spread it out

A ladder splits money across several maturities so part of it comes due every few months. You always have some cash about to free up, you're never all-in at one rate, and if rates rise, each rung reinvests higher. Here's $20,000 of emergency money beyond the first three months:

T-BILLS

How to buy Treasury bills

Either at TreasuryDirect.gov (direct from the government, no fees, but clunky and slow to sell) or through any major brokerage (no fee for new-issue bills, sell any day, and most offer "auto-roll" so a maturing bill buys the next one). Bills are sold at a discount and pay full face value at maturity; the difference is your interest.

CDs

Bank CDs, brokered CDs, no-penalty CDs

Bank CDs charge an early-withdrawal penalty, often 3 months of interest for a 1-year CD and 6–12 months for longer ones; read it before you buy. Brokered CDs (bought through a brokerage) have no penalty but must be sold on the market, possibly at a loss if rates have risen. No-penalty CDs let you withdraw early for free and pay a bit less. All are FDIC-insured up to the limit per bank.

Sleep at night

Is my money safe? FDIC, SIPC and apps

"Safe" means two different things: safe from a bank failing, and safe from prices falling. Insured deposits and Treasuries cover both. Here's what protects what.

Spread large balances

Use a second bank, add a joint or trust account (different ownership categories are insured separately), or hold the excess in Treasury bills. The FDIC's free EDIE calculator shows exactly how much of your money is covered.

APPS

Fintech apps aren't banks

Many savings apps hold your money at a partner bank and advertise FDIC coverage "through" it. That coverage only works if the records show which dollars are yours. When the middleman Synapse collapsed in 2024, customers of several apps had their money frozen for months, and some came up short. Keep your emergency fund at an actual bank or credit union, or confirm the app names its partner bank and holds your money in your name.

MMFs

Money market funds: very safe, not insured

Government money market funds hold Treasury bills and repurchase agreements backed by Treasuries, and U.S. money funds held about $8.4 trillion in August 2026. They aim to hold $1.00 a share, and only a handful have ever "broken the buck," most famously one in 2008 that held debt from the failed Lehman Brothers. For an emergency fund, a government fund is a reasonable choice; an insured account is the most conservative one.

Inflation protection

I bonds and TIPS: cash that tracks inflation

Ordinary cash loses spending power whenever inflation beats its yield. Two Treasury products are built to prevent that. Neither is instant-access, so they fit the second layer of an emergency fund or goal money, not the first few months.

I BONDS

Series I savings bonds

The rate combines a fixed rate (kept for the bond's 30-year life) and an inflation rate reset every six months. Bonds bought May–October 2026 earn 4.26% for their first six months (0.90% fixed + 3.36% inflation). The next rate is set November 1, 2026, and forecasters expect a higher fixed rate, so if you're about to buy, waiting until November is likely to lock in more. Rules: buy up to $10,000 per person per year at TreasuryDirect.gov; you can't cash them for 12 months; cashing before 5 years costs the last 3 months of interest; interest is free of state tax and federal tax is deferred until you cash them.

TIPS

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities

TIPS pay a real yield on top of inflation, with no purchase limit, bought at auction or through a brokerage. In September 2026 a 5-year TIPS paid a real yield of about 2.55% above inflation, well above the I bond's fixed rate. The catch: TIPS prices move with interest rates if you sell early, and the inflation adjustment is taxed each year even though you don't receive it until maturity. They're better for known future spending (hold to maturity, ideally in an IRA) than for emergencies.

Money leaks

Where cash quietly earns nothing

The most common cash mistake isn't choosing the wrong product. It's not choosing at all, and leaving money wherever it happened to land.

BANK

The big-bank savings account

The national average savings rate is 0.37%; many big banks pay 0.01%. The top online banks pay about 11 times the average. Moving takes an afternoon, and you can link the new account to your existing checking.

SWEEP

Uninvested cash at a brokerage or in your IRA

When you sell an investment or make a contribution, the cash lands in a "sweep." Some brokerages sweep into a money market fund paying market rates; others default to a bank sweep that paid about 0.40% in mid-2026 while money funds paid about 3.5%. Check where your cash goes, and if it's the low-rate sweep, buy a money market fund or T-bill yourself. Money contributed to an IRA or 401(k) and never invested is the same problem, only for decades.

CHECKING

Overstuffed checking

A month of bills plus a small cushion is plenty. Everything else can sit one click away in high-yield savings and move back in a day or two.

CDs

CDs that renew on autopilot

Many bank CDs renew automatically at the bank's current rate, which can be far below the best available. Set a reminder a week before maturity and shop.

Do this

A cash checklist

Add up your essential monthly expenses

Housing, food, utilities, insurance, transportation and minimum debt payments. Use the calculator above to set a target.

Check what your savings actually pays

Log in and find the APY. If it starts with a zero, open a high-yield account at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union and link it to your checking.

Automate a transfer every payday

Even $50 a paycheck builds the starter fund in a few months. Raise it when a debt is paid off.

Find your brokerage and IRA sweep rate

If uninvested cash earns under 1%, move it into a money market fund or T-bills, or invest it as planned.

Give goal money a date and a matching term

A down payment in 18 months can go in a 6- and 12-month T-bill ladder, not stocks.

Stay under $250,000 per bank and category

Check with the FDIC's EDIE tool if you're close; spread the rest or use Treasuries.

Consider I bonds after November 1

For money you won't need for at least a year, up to $10,000 per person, once the new fixed rate is announced.

Once it's full, stop

Past your target, extra cash is better invested for the long term (see Which Account?). Refill the fund after you use it.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Isn't it a waste to keep so much in cash when stocks earn more?

Over decades, yes, stocks usually earn more, and that's where long-term money belongs. But emergencies tend to arrive with recessions, when stocks are down and jobs are scarce. Cash is insurance that pays out at exactly that moment. The cost of holding it is the gap between cash and stock returns on a few months of expenses, not on your whole net worth.

Should I pay off my credit card or build my emergency fund?

Both, in order: a starter fund of $1,000–$2,000 first so the next surprise doesn't go back on the card, then pay down the card, then build the full fund. See Credit & Debt.

Can I use my 401(k) as an emergency fund?

Only as a last resort. Since 2024, plans can allow one penalty-free emergency withdrawal of up to $1,000 a year (you owe income tax, and can repay it within three years), and some employers offer an emergency savings account linked to the 401(k), capped at $2,600 in 2026. Anything larger means taxes, a 10% penalty if you're under 59½, and money pulled out of the market, often at a low point.

Is a money market fund the same as a money market account?

No. A money market account is a bank deposit, FDIC-insured. A money market fund is a mutual fund that buys short-term debt; it isn't insured but government funds are very safe. Both pay similar rates when you pick a good one.

Should I keep emergency cash at home?

A small amount, a few hundred dollars, is useful for power outages or when cards don't work. More than that earns nothing, isn't insured, and can be lost or stolen.

How do I avoid spending the emergency fund on non-emergencies?

Keep it at a different bank from your checking, name the account "Emergency fund," and don't attach a debit card. Save separately for things you know are coming (car repairs, holidays, annual bills) in their own named savings "buckets." Those are expenses, not emergencies.

Glossary

APY
Annual percentage yield: what a deposit earns in a year, including compounding.
Emergency fund
Cash set aside for job loss and unplanned essential expenses.
High-yield savings account
A savings account, usually at an online bank, paying close to market interest rates.
Money market account
An insured bank deposit account, often with check-writing.
Money market fund
A mutual fund holding very short-term debt; not insured, aims to stay at $1 a share.
7-day yield
A money market fund's annualized yield over the past week, after fees.
Treasury bill (T-bill)
U.S. government debt maturing in one year or less, sold at a discount.
CD
Certificate of deposit: an insured deposit with a fixed rate for a fixed term.
Brokered CD
A bank CD bought through a brokerage; sold on the market instead of paying a penalty.
Ladder
Splitting money across several maturities so part comes due regularly.
I bond
A U.S. savings bond whose rate combines a fixed rate and current inflation.
TIPS
Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities: bonds whose principal rises with inflation.
Real yield
What an investment earns above inflation.
FDIC / NCUA
The federal agencies insuring bank and credit union deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category.
SIPC
Protection if a brokerage firm fails; doesn't cover investment losses.
Sweep
Where a brokerage automatically parks uninvested cash.
Federal funds rate
The Fed's target overnight rate; savings, money market and T-bill rates follow it.
