Taxes 101: What You Actually Pay, and Why

How the federal income tax really works, why your bracket isn't your tax rate, how investments are taxed, which investments belong in which account, and why many retirees pay far less than they expect. Two calculators use 2026 rules so you can test it on your own numbers.

TL;DR

The U.S. taxes income in layers: each bracket's rate applies only to the dollars inside that bracket. That's why a single person earning $85,000 in 2026 is "in the 22% bracket" but pays about 10% of their income in federal income tax, and why a raise can never shrink your take-home pay. Your marginal rate (the rate on your next dollar) drives decisions like Roth vs. traditional; your effective rate is what you actually pay. On top of income tax, most workers pay 7.65% in Social Security and Medicare tax. Investments held more than a year are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, and a couple with up to $98,900 of taxable income pays 0% on long-term gains. Hold bonds in pre-tax accounts, your fastest growers in Roth, and broad stock index funds in taxable accounts, but don't let this outrank saving more. In retirement, withdrawals fill the low brackets first and the standard deduction grows at 65, so many retirees pay single-digit effective rates, with one trap: Social Security taxation can make the next dollar cost much more than your bracket suggests.

How income tax actually works, in five steps

A tax return looks intimidating, but federal income tax follows the same five steps for almost everyone. Knowing them tells you where each decision in this library, like a 401(k) contribution or an HSA, actually reduces your bill.

STEP 1

Total income

Wages, interest, dividends, gains, side income, retirement withdrawals.

STEP 2

Adjusted gross income

Minus pre-tax 401(k) and HSA contributions and a few other adjustments.

= AGI
STEP 3

Taxable income

Minus the standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 joint in 2026) or itemized deductions, whichever is larger.

STEP 4

Tax, by layer

Ordinary income runs through the brackets; long-term gains sit on top at their own rates.

STEP 5

Minus credits

Credits (like the child tax credit) come straight off the tax itself.

= what you owe

What you've already paid through paycheck withholding is then compared with that final number. The difference is your refund or balance due. A refund isn't a bonus; it's your own money, returned without interest.

The most misunderstood idea in personal finance

Brackets: marginal vs. effective rate

Being "in the 22% bracket" doesn't mean you pay 22% on everything. Taxable income fills the brackets like water filling a set of stairs: the first dollars are taxed at 10%, the next layer at 12%, and only the dollars that reach the 22% step are taxed at 22%.

At the defaults (single, $85,000 salary, $6,000 into a pre-tax 401(k)), the top dollars are taxed at 22%, but the tax works out to about $8,660, roughly 10% of income. Both numbers matter, for different questions:

Marginal rate

For decisions about the next dollar

How much a pre-tax contribution saves, what a raise or side gig nets you, whether Roth or traditional wins (see Which Account?). A $1,000 traditional 401(k) contribution at 22% cuts your tax by $220.

Effective rate

For understanding what you really pay

Total tax divided by total income. Useful for budgeting and for comparing years. It's always lower than your marginal rate, often by half or more.

Before income tax

The other taxes on your paycheck

For many workers, the biggest federal tax isn't income tax at all. It's FICA, the payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare, and it starts with your first dollar of pay, with no standard deduction.

TaxYour share (2026)Notes
Social Security6.2% of wages up to $184,500Your employer pays another 6.2%. Self-employed people pay both halves (12.4%).
Medicare1.45% of all wagesPlus an extra 0.9% on wages over $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (joint).
State and local income tax0% to over 10%Nine states have no tax on wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and Wyoming. Some cities add their own.

In the default example above, FICA comes to about $6,500, close to the $8,660 of income tax. One practical consequence: a pre-tax 401(k) contribution lowers income tax but not FICA, while an HSA contribution made through payroll lowers both. That's one more reason the HSA ranks so high in Which Account?.

Two ways to lower the bill

Deductions vs. credits, and what's new

A deduction lowers the income you're taxed on, so it saves you your marginal rate: a $1,000 deduction at 22% saves $220. A credit comes straight off the tax: a $1,000 credit saves $1,000. A credit is worth several times more than a deduction of the same size.

Standard deduction or itemize?

You take whichever is larger: the standard deduction, or the total of itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts, large medical bills). Since the standard deduction nearly doubled in 2018, roughly nine in ten filers take it. The 2025 law raised the cap on state and local tax (SALT) deductions to $40,000 (rising 1% a year through 2029, and reduced for incomes over $500,000), so more homeowners in high-tax states may itemize again.

Credits worth knowing

  • Child tax credit: $2,200 per child under 17 in 2026, up to $1,700 of it refundable even if you owe no tax. Reduced by $50 per $1,000 of income over $200,000 (single) or $400,000 (joint).
  • Saver's credit: a credit of 10%–50% of up to $2,000 of retirement contributions ($4,000 joint) for lower incomes. In 2026 the 50% rate applies up to $24,250 of AGI (single) or $48,500 (joint), and any credit phases out above $40,250 / $80,500. Often missed by young workers and part-timers.
  • Education, energy and dependent-care credits also exist, each with its own rules. Tax software will ask about them.

Your portfolio

How investments are taxed

In a 401(k), IRA or HSA, none of this applies until you withdraw. In a regular brokerage account, what you own and how long you own it decide the rate.

Qualified dividends (most U.S. and many foreign stocks)At the lower long-term rates: 0%, 15% or 20%.

STACK

Gains sit on top of your other income

Long-term gains are taxed as if they came last, after wages and interest. A married couple with $80,000 of wages and $40,000 of long-term gains has $47,800 of taxable ordinary income, so the full $40,000 of gains fits under the $98,900 line and is taxed at 0%. Try it in the calculator above.

LOSS

Losses aren't wasted

Selling an investment for less than you paid creates a capital loss that cancels out gains, and up to $3,000 a year of losses beyond that can reduce ordinary income. Unused losses carry forward to future years. "Tax-loss harvesting" means selling a loser on purpose and buying something similar (but not substantially identical within 30 days before or after, or the wash-sale rule disallows the loss).

0%

The 0% bracket is an opportunity

In a low-income year (between jobs, early retirement, a sabbatical), you can sell winners and pay no federal tax on the gain, then buy right back. This resets your cost basis higher, and the wash-sale rule applies only to losses.

BASIS

Track what you paid

Your gain is the sale price minus your cost basis, which includes reinvested dividends. Brokers track this for most stocks and funds bought since 2011–2012. For investments held until death, heirs generally get a "step-up" to the value on the date of death, so decades of gains are never taxed.

Asset location

Which investments go in which account

Asset allocation is your overall stock/bond mix. Asset location is deciding which account holds which part of that mix. Keeping the same overall mix but placing each piece where its tax bill is smallest can add a little to your after-tax return for free.

AccountGood fitsWhy
Traditional 401(k) / IRABonds and bond funds, REITs, high-yield funds, actively traded fundsTheir income would be taxed at ordinary rates anyway. Slower growth here also keeps future required withdrawals smaller.
Roth IRA / Roth 401(k) · HSAYour highest-growth holdings: stock funds, especially small-company or more aggressive onesGrowth is never taxed, so it's worth the most on whatever grows the most.

International funds here can claim the foreign tax credit.
Keep it in proportion. Location is fine-tuning. Saving more, keeping fees low and holding a sensible mix matter far more. A target-date fund in every account is a perfectly good answer, and if you only have a 401(k), there's nothing to locate.

Taxes in retirement: why the rate is often lower

Three things work in retirees' favor: withdrawals fill the low brackets first, the standard deduction grows at 65 (plus the temporary $6,000 senior deduction), and payroll taxes stop when paychecks do. One thing works against them: Social Security benefits become taxable in layers, which can make the next dollar surprisingly expensive.

A retiree's 2026 federal income tax
Uses the IRS provisional-income formula for Social Security (up to 50% or 85% taxable), the 2026 standard deduction with the 65+ add-on, and the 2025–2028 senior deduction ($6,000 per person 65+, phased out at 6% of income above $75,000 single / $150,000 joint). Ignores state tax and long-term gains. Roth withdrawals don't count as income, so they'd add nothing here.

At the defaults, a retired couple with $90,000 of total income owes only about $1,920 in federal income tax, a 2% effective rate. But the next $1,000 of IRA withdrawal adds about $185 in tax, an 18.5% effective marginal rate while they're in the 10% bracket, because each extra dollar also makes more of their Social Security taxable. Planners call this the "tax torpedo." It's one reason the order of withdrawals and the timing of Roth conversions matter.

GAP

The gap years are a tax window

Between retiring and starting Social Security and required withdrawals, many people have unusually low income. Converting some traditional IRA money to Roth in those years, filling up the 10%–12% brackets on purpose, can shrink later required withdrawals and the torpedo effect.

RMD

Required withdrawals start at 73 or 75

Pre-tax accounts must start paying out at 73 (75 if born in 1960 or later), whether you need the money or not. Large pre-tax balances can mean large forced withdrawals and higher brackets later in life.

IRMAA

Medicare premiums rise with income too

Higher income in retirement can also raise Medicare Part B and D premiums, based on your tax return from two years earlier. Social Security & Medicare in Depth (Tier 1, #6) covers it, and Turning Savings into a Paycheck (Tier 2, #11) puts all of this into a withdrawal plan.

Clearing things up

Six tax myths

MYTH 1

"A raise could bump me into a higher bracket and I'd take home less."

Only the dollars above the bracket line are taxed at the higher rate. More gross pay always means more take-home pay from the income tax itself. (A few benefits with hard income cutoffs are the rare exception.)

MYTH 2

"A big refund means I did my taxes well."

It means you overpaid all year and lent the government money at 0%. Adjusting your W-4 puts that money in each paycheck instead.

MYTH 3

"It's deductible, so it's basically free."

A deduction saves your marginal rate, not the whole cost. Spending $1,000 to save $220 still costs $780.

MYTH 4

"Selling at a loss is always a mistake."

Harvested losses offset gains and up to $3,000 a year of ordinary income, and carry forward indefinitely. Buy a similar (not identical) fund to stay invested.

MYTH 5

"Social Security isn't taxed."

Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable, depending on your other income. Many retirees owe some tax on it.

MYTH 6

"I can never sell my winners because of the tax."

Long-term gains are often taxed at 0% or 15%, and waiting forever lets taxes run your portfolio. A big gain is a good problem; plan the sale, don't avoid it.

Do this

A yearly tax checklist

Know your two rates

From last year's return: total tax ÷ total income is your effective rate; find your marginal rate in the bracket table. Write both down.

Check your withholding

A big refund or a big bill? Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator and update your W-4.

Use the pre-tax breaks you have

401(k), HSA through payroll, FSAs. Each saves at your marginal rate, and the HSA saves FICA too.

Before December 31: review taxable accounts

Harvest losses, or harvest gains at 0% in a low-income year. Watch the 30-day wash-sale window.

Check for credits you may be missing

Saver's credit, child tax credit, education credits, and the new 65+ deduction.

Keep the paperwork

Cost-basis records, HSA receipts, charitable receipts and last year's return in one place.

In a low-income year, think Roth conversion

Retirement, a sabbatical or a job gap can be the cheapest time to move pre-tax money to Roth. Run the numbers or ask a tax pro first.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Do I owe tax when I sell my home?

Usually not. If you owned and lived in it for at least two of the last five years, up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for a married couple) is excluded. Gains above that are taxed as long-term capital gains.

Is it worth moving to a state with no income tax?

It can be, but compare the whole bill. States without an income tax often lean harder on property and sales taxes, and cost of living matters more than any single tax. Nine states don't tax wages; Washington does tax large capital gains.

When do I need to pay estimated taxes?

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more beyond what's withheld, typically from self-employment, a side business or large investment income. You generally avoid penalties by paying at least as much as last year's total tax (110% if your income was over $150,000) through withholding and quarterly payments.

Do rollovers between retirement accounts count as income?

Not if done correctly. A direct rollover (trustee to trustee) from a 401(k) to an IRA is tax-free. A Roth conversion is different: the converted amount is taxable income that year.

Are "tax-free" insurance and annuity products a good way to cut taxes?

Usually they're an expensive way to get a tax break you could get more cheaply from a 401(k), IRA, HSA or a plain index fund held long-term. Compare against the baseline in Investing Basics, and read Life Insurance Explained and Annuities Explained first.

Should I use tax software or a professional?

With a W-2, a few accounts and the standard deduction, software (or IRS Free File if eligible) handles it well. A self-employed business, rental property, stock compensation, a large Roth conversion, an inheritance or a move between states are good reasons to pay a CPA or enrolled agent.

Reference

Glossary

Gross income
All your income before any adjustments or deductions.
Adjusted gross income (AGI)
Gross income minus pre-tax contributions and certain adjustments. Many limits are based on it.
Taxable income
AGI minus the standard or itemized deduction. The number the brackets apply to.
Tax bracket
A range of taxable income taxed at one rate.
Marginal tax rate
The rate on your next dollar of income.
Effective tax rate
Total tax divided by total income: what you actually pay on average.
Standard deduction
A flat amount everyone can subtract instead of itemizing.
Itemized deductions
Specific deductible expenses (mortgage interest, SALT, charity) used instead of the standard deduction when larger.
Tax credit
A dollar-for-dollar reduction of tax owed. Refundable credits can be paid out even when no tax is owed.
FICA
Payroll taxes for Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%).
Withholding
Tax taken from each paycheck toward your yearly bill, set by your W-4.
Capital gain
Profit from selling an asset for more than its cost basis.
Cost basis
What you paid for an investment, including reinvested dividends.
Qualified dividend
A dividend taxed at the lower long-term capital-gains rates.
Tax-loss harvesting
Selling at a loss to offset gains or income, then reinvesting in something similar.
Wash-sale rule
Disallows a loss if you buy a substantially identical investment within 30 days before or after the sale.
Step-up in basis
Heirs' cost basis resets to the value at death, erasing prior gains for tax purposes.
Asset location
Placing investments in the account type where they're taxed least.
Provisional income
Other income plus half of Social Security, used to decide how much of your benefits is taxable.
Roth conversion
Moving pre-tax retirement money to a Roth account and paying income tax on it now.
