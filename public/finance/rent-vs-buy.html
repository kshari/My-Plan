Rent vs. Buy & Mortgages Explained

The honest math behind the biggest purchase most people make: what owning really costs each year, a full rent-versus-buy comparison you can run on your own numbers, how a mortgage works, the big choices (fixed or adjustable, 15 or 30 years, how much down, points), and when a refinance pays off.

TL;DR

Rent isn't "throwing money away," and a mortgage isn't automatically "building wealth." Both have unrecoverable costs. For renters it's rent. For owners it's mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance and what the down payment could have earned elsewhere, minus any appreciation. On top of that, buying and later selling costs roughly 8%–10% of the home's price, which is why how long you'll stay matters most. With 30-year rates near 7% (Freddie Mac, September 2026) and a median existing home of about $429,000, renting and investing the difference often comes out ahead over 5–10 years in many markets, while buying tends to win the longer you stay, the cheaper homes are relative to rents, and the lower your rate. Run the calculator with your own numbers. If you buy: keep the payment near 28% of gross income, keep an emergency fund after closing, compare lenders, treat points and refinances as break-even math, and know that PMI can be removed once you reach 20% equity.

What owning really costs each year

The fair comparison isn't rent versus mortgage payment. Part of a mortgage payment is principal, which is really moving your own money into the house, like a forced savings account. The comparison that matters is the unrecoverable costs on each side: money that's gone and doesn't come back when you move.

Owner's unrecoverable cost (first year)Example: $430,000 home, 20% down, 7% rateWhy it counts
Mortgage interest≈ $24,000 (5.6% of price)The price of borrowing. It shrinks slowly as the loan is paid down.
Property tax≈ $4,300 (1.0%)Varies widely by state, from under 0.5% to over 2% of value.
Maintenance and repairs≈ $6,500 (1.5%)Roofs, water heaters and appliances wear out. A common rule of thumb is 1%–2% a year.

On these assumptions, owning's yearly cost is close to renting a similar home for about $2,300–$2,400 a month. What tips the balance is the one-time transaction costs: closing costs of roughly 2%–5% of the loan when you buy, and agent commissions (often 2.5%–3% to each side) plus other fees when you sell. That round trip of 8%–10% of the price has to be earned back, and it takes years.

Home prices over the long run. Homes feel like a great investment because the numbers are large and borrowed money magnifies them. But Robert Shiller's century-long index found U.S. home prices, after inflation, ended the 20th century only about 23% above their 1890 level. A home is mostly a place to live that roughly keeps pace with inflation, not a growth investment. Your mileage varies a lot by city and by decade.

Three things to notice as you change the inputs. First, the buyer starts well behind: closing costs, plus the selling costs they'd pay to cash out, are a hole to climb out of. Second, small assumptions swing the answer. At the U.S. median defaults, buying doesn't catch up for decades, but at a 6% rate the break-even drops to about 10 years, and with 4% yearly price growth to about 15. Third, the comparison assumes the renter really invests the difference. A renter who spends it loses the race, which is the strongest argument for buying: a mortgage is a savings plan you can't skip.

The part no calculator captures

What the math leaves out

Reasons to buy

Stability, control, forced savings

No landlord deciding to sell or raise the rent; freedom to renovate, garden, keep pets; a fixed-rate payment that stays the same while rents rise with inflation; and principal payments that build equity whether or not you're disciplined. Paid off by retirement, a home cuts your living costs sharply.

Reasons to rent

Flexibility, simplicity, diversification

Easy to move for a job or a relationship; no surprise $15,000 roof; money stays liquid and spread across the whole market instead of concentrated in one house on one street, bought with borrowed money. Borrowing works both ways: a 10% price drop on a home bought with 10% down wipes out the entire down payment.

A good rule: buy when you expect to stay at least five to seven years, the payment fits comfortably, and you'll still have an emergency fund after closing. Until then, renting and investing the difference is a perfectly respectable plan, not a failure.

The loan itself

How a mortgage works

A standard mortgage has a fixed monthly payment for principal and interest. Early on, most of each payment is interest; over time the balance falls and more goes to principal. That schedule is called amortization. Your actual monthly bill usually also includes property tax and insurance (held in an escrow account) and, with less than 20% down, mortgage insurance.

On a $344,000 loan at 7%, the 30-year payment is about $2,289 a month and total interest comes to about $480,000, more than the loan itself. Adding just $200 a month pays it off in about 23½ years and saves about $121,000 of interest. With property tax and insurance on the $430,000 home, the monthly bill is closer to $2,850.

Decisions

The big mortgage choices

FIXED

Fixed rate or adjustable (ARM)?

A fixed-rate loan keeps the same rate for the life of the loan, and you can refinance if rates fall. An ARM (for example a 5/6 or 7/6 ARM) is fixed for the first 5 or 7 years, then adjusts every six months with a benchmark rate, within caps on the first adjustment, each later one, and the lifetime total. An ARM can make sense if you're confident you'll sell before it adjusts; otherwise you're taking on rate risk the lender is happy to hand you.

15/30

15 years or 30?

A 15-year loan has a lower rate (6.42% vs. 7.03% in Freddie Mac's September 24, 2026 survey) and costs far less interest, but a much higher payment. On $344,000 at about 6.4%, the 15-year payment is roughly $2,980, with total interest near $192,000 versus $480,000 on the 30-year at 7%. A middle path: take the 30-year for flexibility and prepay when you can, but only after the employer match, high-interest debt and an emergency fund are handled.

DOWN

How much down?

20% down avoids private mortgage insurance (PMI) on a conventional loan. With less down, PMI typically costs somewhere around 0.3%–1.5% of the loan a year depending on credit and down payment. You can ask to cancel it once the balance reaches 80% of the original value, and it must end automatically at 78%. Putting less down to keep an emergency fund is often wiser than draining savings to reach 20%.

LOAN

Loan types

Conventional loans (up to $832,750 in most of the country for 2026; higher in expensive counties) need as little as 3%–5% down. FHA loans allow 3.5% down with lower credit scores but charge a 1.75% upfront premium plus about 0.55% a year, for the life of the loan if you put less than 10% down. VA loans (for eligible veterans and service members) and USDA loans (eligible rural areas) can require nothing down. Above the conforming limit, "jumbo" loans have stricter terms.

SHOP

Shop lenders, and compare the Loan Estimate

Every lender must give you a standardized three-page Loan Estimate within three business days of applying. Get at least three, and compare the rate, the APR, and "origination charges" on page 2. Multiple mortgage credit checks within a short window count as a single inquiry for your score.

Pay now to save later?

Points and refinancing: the break-even test

Buying "points" (paying upfront to lower your rate) and refinancing (replacing your loan with a cheaper one) are the same decision: an upfront cost in exchange for a lower monthly payment. Divide the cost by the monthly savings to get the months to break even. If you'll likely move or refinance again before then, don't do it.

Break-even on points or a refinance
Simple break-even: upfront cost ÷ monthly principal-and-interest savings. A refinance that restarts a 30-year term can lower the payment but add years of interest; compare total cost, not just the payment. One point = 1% of the loan; how much a point lowers the rate varies by lender and market.

At the defaults, one point ($3,440) lowers the rate from 7% to 6.75% and saves about $57 a month, which takes about five years to earn back. That's worth it only if you're confident you'll keep this exact loan longer, which is uncertain when rates may fall and a refinance could replace it.

Affordability

How much house?

Lenders will often approve more than is comfortable. A long-standing guideline keeps housing costs (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, HOA) at or below about 28% of gross income, and all debt payments at or below about 36%.

  • The $430,000 example (about $2,850 a month with tax and insurance) implies a household income of roughly $122,000 under the 28% guideline.
  • Keep an emergency fund after closing, plus a cushion for first-year repairs and furniture. New owners are often surprised by these costs.
  • Don't let the house crowd out retirement saving. The savings order still applies: take the employer match before stretching for a bigger house.
  • Price the total cost of the neighborhood too: commute, property-tax trends, insurance availability, and HOA rules and special assessments.

Right now the market is shifting in buyers' favor in many areas: in August 2026, inventory reached 1.62 million homes (4.9 months of supply, the most in over a decade) while the median price rose just 1.6% from a year earlier. Rents have also softened, falling for 37 straight months in the largest metros, with over 43% of listings offering concessions. Both sides have more room to negotiate than they did a few years ago.

Do this

A home-buying checklist

Run rent vs. buy for your actual market

Use real listings: the price of homes you'd buy and the rent for similar ones nearby.

Check your credit six months ahead

Pull your reports, fix errors, and pay down card balances. Your score sets your rate.

Keep the down payment in cash

High-yield savings, money market funds or T-bills, not stocks, if you'll buy within a few years.

Get pre-approved by at least three lenders

Compare Loan Estimates side by side: rate, APR, points and origination charges.

Set your own budget below the approval

About 28% of gross income for all housing costs, with an emergency fund left after closing.

Get an inspection, and read the HOA documents

A few hundred dollars now can prevent a five-figure surprise.

Negotiate commissions and credits

Agent fees are negotiable, and in a market with more inventory, sellers may cover part of your closing costs.

After you buy: track PMI and rates

Request PMI removal at 80% loan-to-value, and run the break-even test whenever rates drop meaningfully.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Isn't renting just throwing money away?

No more than paying mortgage interest, property tax and maintenance is. Both renters and owners pay for housing; the question is which costs less for your situation and timeline, and whether the renter invests what they save.

Should I wait for mortgage rates to fall?

Nobody can predict rates reliably. Buy when the home, the timing and the payment are right for you at today's rate. If rates fall meaningfully later, you can refinance (see the break-even test). If they rise, your fixed rate is locked.

Should I pay off my mortgage early or invest?

Prepaying earns a guaranteed return equal to your rate, currently about 7% for new loans. That's competitive with expected stock returns, but it's locked in the house. Do it after the employer match, high-interest debt, the emergency fund and tax-advantaged accounts. For a low-rate mortgage from earlier years, investing usually wins.

Is the mortgage interest deduction a reason to buy?

Rarely anymore. It only helps if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction ($32,200 for couples in 2026), which many owners don't reach. The calculator ignores it.

Can I use retirement money for a down payment?

You can take up to $10,000 from an IRA penalty-free as a first-time buyer, withdraw Roth IRA contributions, or borrow from a 401(k). Each shrinks your retirement savings and the 401(k) loan can come due quickly if you leave your job. Treat these as a last resort.

Is a house a good investment?

It's a good place to live that has roughly kept up with inflation over the long run, with borrowed money magnifying both gains and losses. Its biggest financial benefits are the forced savings and a fixed housing cost. Think of it first as housing, second as an asset.

Reference

Glossary

Principal
The amount borrowed, or the part of a payment that reduces it.
Amortization
The schedule by which fixed payments shift from mostly interest to mostly principal.
PITI
Principal, interest, taxes and insurance: the full monthly housing payment.
Escrow
An account your lender uses to pay property tax and insurance from your monthly payment.
Down payment
The share of the price you pay upfront, not borrowed.
Loan-to-value (LTV)
The loan balance divided by the home's value.
PMI
Private mortgage insurance, protecting the lender on conventional loans with under 20% down.
MIP
Mortgage insurance premium on FHA loans, upfront and yearly.
ARM
Adjustable-rate mortgage: fixed for an initial period, then adjusting within caps.
Points
Upfront fees (1 point = 1% of the loan) paid to lower the interest rate.
APR
Annual percentage rate: the interest rate plus certain fees, as a yearly rate.
Loan Estimate
A standardized three-page disclosure of a mortgage's terms and costs.
Closing costs
Fees for the loan, title, appraisal and taxes paid when you buy.
Conforming loan limit
The largest loan Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will buy: $832,750 in most areas in 2026.
Jumbo loan
A mortgage above the conforming limit.
Equity
The home's value minus what you owe on it.
Refinance
Replacing an existing mortgage with a new one, usually to lower the rate or change the term.
Break-even point
How long monthly savings take to repay an upfront cost.
