Annuities Explained

The major components of annuity products, in plain terms and from a personal-finance perspective — not tied to any one carrier or contract. This installment covers what an annuity is, compares the main types against each other, and then digs into why fixed indexed annuities specifically — and how the indexing that credits your interest actually works.

TL;DR

An annuity is an insurance contract, not an investment — you're paying an insurer to take on a risk (usually running out of money) in exchange for guarantees, most often a 0% floor on market losses and/or income for life. Those guarantees are real, but never free: they're funded by caps on your upside, ongoing fees, multi-year surrender charges, and headline features like bonuses that often apply only to a notional number you can never spend directly. For most people still building wealth, the fundamentals — emergency fund, employer match, maxed tax-advantaged accounts, broad low-cost investing — come first; an annuity is a late-stage, narrow-purpose tool, not a growth strategy. This episode walks through the mechanics, the trade-offs, and where — if anywhere — one might actually fit.

Start here

What an annuity actually is

Strip away the marketing and an annuity is a simple trade: you hand an insurance company a sum of money, and in exchange it takes on a risk you'd rather manage yourself — usually the risk of running out of money before you run out of life. Everything past that — riders, indexing, bonuses — is a variation on that one trade.

Annuities split along two independent lines. When income starts: an immediate annuity begins paying within about a year of purchase; a deferred annuity accumulates for years first, and converts to income later — or never; you can also simply take the money back out. How money grows while deferred: a fixed annuity credits a flat interest rate the insurer declares each year; a variable annuity invests directly in mutual-fund-like subaccounts and can lose value along with the market; a fixed indexed annuity (FIA) — the type the rest of this episode focuses on — credits interest linked to a market index like the S&P 500, with a guaranteed floor of 0%, but only up to a cap, participation rate, or spread that limits how much of the index's gain you actually get.

How the carrier actually delivers on that promise

Two different mechanisms are doing the work behind those guarantees — one for growing the money, a separate one for the lifetime income.

GROWS

Pooled, institutional-scale investing

The carrier pools your premium with everyone else's into one large, conservative general-account portfolio — overwhelmingly high-grade bonds — and invests it at a scale and access most individuals can't match on their own. A slice of what that portfolio earns funds the index-linked crediting formula covered next; the rest covers the carrier's own costs, profit, and the reserves regulators require it to hold against every promise it's made.

INSURES

Pooled mortality risk, not investment return

The lifetime-income promise runs on a different principle entirely: actuaries price it by pooling longevity risk across a large group, so the premiums of people who don't live as long help fund the payments of those who do. That's the one piece of this trade no individual can replicate alone — no amount of personal saving guarantees you won't outlive your own money, but a large enough risk pool, priced correctly, can guarantee it for the group. It's the same mechanism behind Social Security and a traditional pension, run here by a private insurer.

That pooling is the genuine value being sold here — a real, if narrow, service. The rest of this episode is about what it costs to buy, and how to tell whether a specific contract is pricing it fairly.

Worth a look, if —

you're within a decade of retirement and want a floor under part of your savings

FIAs are built for money you want protected from a market crash in the years just before or after you stop working — "sequence of returns" risk, where a bad year or two right at retirement does outsized, hard-to-recover-from damage. Used for a slice of a portfolio, alongside stocks, bonds, and cash you can still reach, the trade — capped upside for a real floor — can be a reasonable one.

Usually a mismatch, if —

you're decades from retirement, or need the money to stay liquid

A long time horizon is exactly when uncapped market exposure tends to win — you have the years to ride out a downturn, which makes giving up decades of compounding to a cap rate expensive. Multi-year surrender charges also make FIAs a poor fit for an emergency fund or any money you might need on short notice.

On growth: in a typical year, an FIA buyer should expect a credited return well below what the stock market itself has historically returned. Industry sources put realistic long-run FIA crediting somewhere around 3%–7% a year; the S&P 500 itself has averaged roughly 9%–10% a year in total return (price gains plus dividends) over long stretches of its history — about 9.7%/yr from 2005–2024, and an even higher ~14%/yr over just the last, unusually strong decade. The next section works through exactly why that gap exists, and what it buys you.

Before the deep dive

Comparing the main types

"Annuity" is an umbrella term covering several structurally different products — the right starting question isn't which one credits the best rate, it's when you need the money and how much risk you're willing to hold in the meantime. Six types cover most of what's sold today; the table below lines them up before this episode narrows in on the fifth.

Why this episode goes deep on one type

Fixed indexed annuities, and why they get the rest of this episode

FIAs aren't a niche corner of the market. Insurers sold $127.9 billion of them in 2025 — a fifth consecutive year of growth and a new sales record for the product line — making FIA the second-largest annuity category by dollars sold, behind only plain fixed-rate deferred annuities (MYGA-type, $165.3B) and well ahead of variable annuities ($63.1B) and income annuities (SPIA + DIA combined, $19.2B), per LIMRA's final 2025 sales report. Registered index-linked annuities (RILAs) are newer and growing fastest — up 20% in 2025 to $79.5B — but still sell at roughly two-thirds FIA's volume.

The reason FIA draws that much of the market — and that much of this episode — is the seat it occupies in the comparison above: it's the only major type that lets someone still years from needing the money keep a share of market-linked growth while guaranteeing the account can never post a losing year. That combination is exactly what makes it attractive to pre-retirees rattled by market swings, and exactly why it's the type most commonly paired with the income riders and bonus features examined elsewhere in this project — the 50% upfront bonus contract, the Athene Agility 10. Riders and bonuses are almost always layered onto an FIA chassis, not a fixed or variable one.

It's also the type whose mechanics are hardest to judge at a glance. A MYGA's rate is one number, full stop. An FIA's actual return depends on which index it tracks, which of the three crediting levers it uses, how that lever is set today versus five years from now, and how a specific historical stretch would have played out under those rules — the kind of math a sales illustration can make look better than it will likely perform, precisely because it's hard to see through. That's the gap the rest of this episode closes, starting with how the crediting itself actually works.

The mechanism behind the number on your statement

Indexing, participation, and the price of the floor

An indexed annuity never actually invests in the index. The insurer puts your premium into its own conservative portfolio — mostly bonds — and spends a slice of the interest that generates on index-linked options. What lands on your statement is whatever those options pay out, run through three possible levers.

Two concepts anchor everything below — a floor and a cap — and they exist as a pair, because one is quite literally the price of the other.

What you get: the floor

A guaranteed minimum credit, almost always 0%

Whatever the index does, your credited return for the period can't fall below a stated minimum. That's the entire purpose of an FIA: protecting a slice of savings from a market crash landing at exactly the wrong moment — the sequence-of-returns risk raised earlier in this episode, where a bad year or two right before or after retirement can do damage a portfolio never fully recovers from.

What it costs: the cap

A ceiling on how much of a good year you keep

A guarantee that never credits a loss has to be funded somehow, and the cap is how: a ceiling on how much of the index's gain you're allowed to keep in a good year. The insurer keeps the rest — often most of it — to pay for the years it has to credit 0% instead of a loss. The floor isn't free; it's paid for out of the tail of your best years.

The three levers below are simply the different ways a contract can set that cap — and, on some contracts, layer on a second cost alongside it.

CAP

A ceiling on the gain

The most the contract will credit in one period, however far the index rises. Current cap rates on common annual point-to-point strategies run roughly 5%–11% depending on carrier and contract term — so a year the index returns 20% might still credit only 8%.

PAR

A fraction of the gain

A participation rate credits only a percentage of the index's move, instead of or alongside a cap. At 75% participation, a 10% index gain becomes a 7.5% credit; paired with a 5% cap, that same 10% gain would be trimmed further, down to the 5% ceiling. Some contracts advertise participation rates well above 100% — even 150%–190% — which sounds like more than the market's own gain. The quick catch: a rate that high is almost never attached to the plain S&P 500. It's paired with a proprietary "engineered" index instead, one already carrying its own embedded costs and volatility controls that tend to move less than the raw market — so 190% of a muted, cost-laden index can still credit less, over time, than 100% of the real thing would have.

SPREAD

A fee taken off the top

A spread (or "margin") is subtracted from the index's move before crediting — a 2-point spread on a 10% gain credits 8%. Whichever lever a contract uses, the direction is identical: you receive a bounded fraction of the index's upside, never all of it.

Whichever lever a contract uses, the floor described above still applies underneath it: a down year for the index credits exactly 0%, never negative. The chart below makes the full trade concrete, using one real ten-year stretch of the actual S&P 500.

Between the cap, the forgone dividends, and the rider fee, the realistic 3%–7% range cited above is the sum of everything this section just walked through — not a separate, unexplained number.

Beyond the crediting rate

Bonuses, vesting schedules, and income riders

Once the crediting mechanics above are clear, most of what actually differs from one FIA contract to the next comes down to three features stacked on top: a premium bonus, the vesting schedule that governs when it's really yours, and an income rider that turns the account into a paycheck. Each runs on its own logic.

The premium bonus

A bonus adds a stated percentage — commonly 5%–55% depending on the contract — to your account on day one. The detail worth reading closely: does the bonus land on the real account value (money you could actually withdraw or annuitize as cash) or only on the income base (a notional figure used solely to calculate future guaranteed lifetime withdrawals)? Carriers disclose which, but it's easy to skim past — and a 10% bonus on real cash is a genuinely different offer from a 55% bonus on a number you can only ever convert into income payments.

The vesting schedule

A bonus is rarely fully yours the day it's credited. Most contracts vest it gradually, in step with the surrender-charge schedule — surrender or withdraw beyond the free amount before it's fully vested, and you forfeit whatever portion hasn't vested yet.

The income rider

A guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit (GLWB) rider is the mechanism that turns an income base into guaranteed lifetime income, and it runs in two phases. During the growth phase, the benefit base grows by a stated rollup — either a flat guaranteed percentage, or, on some newer products, an indexed rollup tied to actual index performance. During the income phase, once you elect to start withdrawals — usually after a minimum deferral period and minimum age — the carrier applies an age-banded payout percentage to the benefit base to set your guaranteed annual withdrawal amount, for as long as you live, even if the real account value ever reaches zero.

The rollup is not compounding growth of spendable money — it's an actuarial input the carrier uses to price a lifetime-income promise, applied to a number that only ever becomes income payments, never a lump sum. It's entirely possible, and common, for a contract to guarantee an 8% rollup on the income base while the real account value grows at the 3%–7% realistic range covered above — both numbers are accurate, they're just describing two different books.

What else comes out of the account

Fees beyond the crediting drag

The cap, forgone dividends, and rider-fee drag already covered explain why FIA growth runs lower than the index — but that's about what you don't get credited. A separate question is what can actively come out of the account in dollar terms. A few fee types work that way.

RIDER

The income rider fee

Deducted as a percentage of the benefit base — not the account value — typically 0.95%–1.50% a year, taken whether or not the index credited anything that year. This is the one that matters most: the 0% floor protects the index-linked credit, not the account from this deduction. A contract's real account value can go down in dollar terms in a year the index-linked side floored at exactly 0%, because the fee still comes out.

ADMIN

Contract or administrative fee

A smaller flat annual charge — commonly in the $30–$50 range — that some contracts still bill for administration, separate from any rider, sometimes waived above a premium threshold (e.g., $100k).

OPTIONAL

Optional-rider fees

An enhanced death benefit, a return-of-premium guarantee, or other optional endorsements each typically carry their own percentage charge, stacking on top of a lifetime-income rider fee if more than one is elected.

GROWS

Why the fee gets bigger even when the rate doesn't

Rider fees are charged on the benefit base, and the benefit base itself grows every year via the bonus and rollup — so the dollar size of the fee tends to climb even when the stated percentage never changes. A fee that looks modest at issue can be a meaningfully larger deduction a decade in.

What it costs to change your mind

Surrender charges and liquidity

Almost every FIA carries a surrender-charge period — commonly 6 to 10 years — during which withdrawing beyond a set allowance triggers a declining penalty. A few features soften that penalty. Most contracts allow a free withdrawal — commonly up to 10% of account value per year — with no surrender charge at all, even during the surrender period. Withdrawals beyond that free amount, while still in the surrender period, can also trigger a Market Value Adjustment (MVA): an adjustment, up or down, that reflects how interest rates have moved since the contract was purchased — it can either add to or subtract from what you'd otherwise receive, on top of the stated surrender charge. And some contracts build in hardship-specific relief valves — a terminal-illness waiver, a confinement or nursing-home waiver, or a "bailout" provision that lifts the surrender charge entirely if a specific crediting rate falls below a stated threshold — that grant full or partial access without the usual penalty in defined situations.

None of this replaces real liquidity. Money earmarked for an emergency fund, or that might be needed on short notice for any reason, is generally a poor fit for the surrender-charge years of any FIA, waivers and free-withdrawal allowances included.

Before you sign anything

How to read a contract's own illustration against these mechanics

An illustration is the carrier's own numeric projection for your specific contract, built from exactly the mechanics covered above. A handful of checks turn it from a glossy printout into a real evaluation tool.

01

Guaranteed vs. illustrated

Every illustration shows at least two columns: guaranteed minimum values, which assume the contractual floor — sometimes as low as a 0%–1% minimum guaranteed rate — every year, and non-guaranteed illustrated values, which assume today's current cap, participation, or rollup holds for the life of the contract. Only the guaranteed column is actually promised.

02

The cap can move

Ask for the contractually guaranteed minimum cap or participation rate — the true floor the carrier can reset down to, usually annually after year one, within the contract's own limits — not just today's current, marketed rate.

03

Whose number is growing

For any rider, confirm on the page whether a given growth figure applies to the real account value or the benefit base. The two numbers can, and routinely do, diverge completely — see the two books above.

04

The one number that's really yours

Find the surrender value or cash value column for each policy year — account value minus any applicable surrender charge and MVA. That's the figure that answers "what would I actually walk away with if I needed this money on this date."

05

The crediting method, not just the cap

Two contracts advertising the same 8% cap can perform very differently depending on whether crediting is annual point-to-point, a monthly sum, or a monthly average — ask which method applies, since it changes how a volatile year translates into a credited return.

Before you sit down with an agent

What the sales pitch highlights — and what it actually costs

An insurance company prices risk for a living; it doesn't hand out free upside. Every headline feature in an annuity pitch is paid for somewhere in the same contract, whether or not that somewhere comes up in the same conversation. None of the claims below are false — a 50% bonus really is credited, a 190% participation rate really does multiply whatever the index does. What's rarely offered upfront is where the cost of each one shows up elsewhere in the fine print. The gold line is the pitch; underneath it, the mechanism that pays for it.

"Up to a 50% premium bonus, credited on day one."
The trade-off

The bonus almost always lands on a notional income base used only to calculate future guaranteed withdrawals — not on the real, spendable account value you could take as a lump sum. The real account typically starts at or near 100% of premium, and the bonus is usually funded by a lower cap or participation rate than the same carrier's no-bonus contract offers, plus a longer surrender-charge schedule. Two numbers grow on the same statement; only one of them is money you can actually walk away with.

"Participation rates up to 190% of the index."
The trade-off

Triple-digit participation is almost always attached to a proprietary or "engineered" index — often volatility-controlled, or a decrement index with a servicing cost (commonly 0.50%–0.85% a year) built directly into how the index itself is calculated — rather than a plain S&P 500 strategy. Those indices are frequently designed to move less than the raw market. 190% of a muted, cost-laden index can still credit less over time than 100% of the real S&P 500 would under an ordinary cap.

"Uncapped growth potential."
The trade-off

"No cap" doesn't mean "no limit" — it usually means the limit moved somewhere less visible: a spread deducted from the index's move before anything is credited, a monthly-sum design where down months count in full but up months are individually capped, or, again, a proprietary index with its cost already baked in. The question worth asking isn't whether a strategy is capped, it's what replaced the cap.

"A lifetime income rider, included at no additional cost."
The trade-off

"No additional charge" describes how the fee is billed, not whether one exists. Riders marketed this way typically have their cost folded into lower caps and participation rates on the crediting side, rather than itemized as a separate line — you're still paying for it, just not anywhere you'd see it as a deduction.

"Your principal can never lose money."
The trade-off

True, against a down index year — the 0% floor is real. It says nothing about the other two ways the number you can actually walk away with falls short of expectations: a multi-year surrender charge, routinely 8–10% in the early years, if you need the money out before the schedule ends; and the fact that "can't lose" isn't "keeps up with inflation" — flat and capped years both quietly erode what the balance can buy.

"A guaranteed 7%–8% annual rollup."
The trade-off

Like the bonus above, a guaranteed rollup almost always compounds the same notional income base — a number you can only ever convert into income payments, never withdraw as a lump sum. It's entirely possible for a contract to be honestly advertising an 8% guaranteed rate and a realistic 3–7% real-money return in the very same sentence, because the two figures are describing different books.

None of this means walk away from every annuity with these features — a bonus, a high participation rate, or an income rider can still be the right tool for the right situation. It means the feature list isn't the whole picture. Before signing anything, it's worth asking, for every headline number: what's the real account value doing while this notional number grows, and what's paying for it?

The bottom line, from the policyholder's chair

The broker and carrier rows aren't paid out of thin air. The full $100,000 premium is credited to the policyholder's account — the commission comes out of the carrier's own funds up front, and the carrier recoups it, with a healthy margin, out of exactly the mechanics covered earlier in this episode: the gap between what its own investments earn and what it credits the index-linked side (the cap), plus the ongoing rider fee. The bonus, the cap, and the rider fee aren't incidental costs sitting next to the product — they're what pays the carrier back for the commission it already spent.

The asymmetry is structural, not a quirk of this one illustration. The broker is paid first and with certainty. The carrier's return — roughly 30%/yr on the capital it actually risks — is high, arrives early, and is pooled across the whole block, so it barely depends on any single policyholder's mortality. The policyholder is the only party whose return is genuinely uncertain, resolved only years later by how long they live and whether they ever need the money back early — ranging from a real loss in nominal dollars to a real win, on the very same contract.

As with the rest of this episode, these are generic, illustrative figures, not any specific carrier's actual commission, margin, or claims experience — real contracts disclose their own. Zooming out

Where annuities fit in the bigger picture

Everything above has stayed inside the annuity itself — how it works, what it costs, who profits. The question a savvy investor actually needs answered is different: where does this sit relative to everything else the same dollar could do?

The ordering question

An annuity is almost always a late-stage, narrow-purpose tool — not a place to start.

01

Emergency fund first

Three to six months of expenses, fully liquid — before anything carrying a multi-year surrender charge.

02

High-interest debt

Paying down credit-card-rate debt is a guaranteed after-tax return that few products, annuities included, can match.

03

The employer match

Free money in a 401(k)/403(b) match beats any commission-funded bonus a salesperson can offer.

04

Tax-advantaged room

Max Roth and traditional IRA/401(k) space before reaching for an insurance product for its "tax-deferred growth."

05

Broad, low-cost investing

A diversified index portfolio, sized to your actual time horizon and risk tolerance.

06

Only then, a narrow purpose

A guaranteed floor under one slice of near-retirement savings, or lifetime-income insurance against outliving your money — a specific job, not a growth strategy.

An annuity competing for step 1–5 money is almost always the wrong tool for the job. It's priced, and sold, as a step-6 product.

Before buying the guarantee, price the alternative

A savvy investor can often replicate — or beat — what an annuity is selling, more cheaply and with more control.

Instead of an FIA's floor

A bond or TIPS ladder, timed to your spending

More liquid, no surrender charge, no cap on the underlying instrument's own yield, and you keep control of the money. The trade: you manage it yourself, and there's no pooled lifetime-income guarantee unless you add one deliberately.

Instead of a DIA or QLAC

Delaying Social Security

Between full retirement age and 70, Social Security benefits grow by a guaranteed 8% a year — two-thirds of one percent a month — inflation-adjusted for life, backed by the federal government. For most people who can bridge the gap with savings, that's a better-priced version of exactly what a commercial longevity annuity is selling, and worth pricing first.

Where it can still make sense for a savvy investor

MIND

Behavioral protection

Some investors know, honestly, that a real market crash will make them sell everything at the bottom. A guaranteed floor that's structurally hard to touch can be worth its cost purely as a commitment device — a rational response to real, if uncomfortable, self-knowledge.

GAP

Thin guaranteed income otherwise

Someone with no pension, modest Social Security, and real longevity in the family has genuinely more to gain from mortality pooling than someone whose guaranteed-income floor is already large.

RMD

QLAC-specific RMD management

A Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract lets you exclude up to $210,000 (2026 limit) of IRA/401(k) money from required-minimum-distribution calculations, deferring both the distributions and the tax on that slice until income starts — as late as age 85.

LEGAL

Creditor protection

In many states, annuity cash value carries some protection from creditors and lawsuits, similar to — and sometimes stronger than — a taxable brokerage account. A real, if narrow, consideration for people in litigation-exposed professions.

None of this is a verdict on annuities in general — it's the actual comparison set the decision needs. The product can be the right tool for a specific job; it's very rarely the right first tool, or the right tool for money that hasn't already cleared everything above it.

If you're going to buy one anyway

A buyer's checklist

None of the above rules annuities out — it means going in with a plan. A few concrete checks before signing anything.

01

Use the free-look period

Every state guarantees a window — commonly 10 to 30 days, longer in some states for buyers 65 and older (Arizona extends to 30 days, for example) — to cancel and get your premium back after receiving the contract. Know the exact number of days for your state, and use the window to read the actual contract, not just the illustration you were shown.

02

Check the carrier's financial strength

This is a promise to pay you money for decades. Check the carrier's rating from AM Best, S&P, or Moody's before buying — a strong sales pitch doesn't tell you whether the company backing it will still be strong in year 25.

03

Get the Certificate of Disclosure, not just the illustration

Ask for the actual contract disclosure document, which spells out guarantees, formulas, and fine print an illustration only summarizes — sometimes omits.

04

Get a fee-only second opinion

A fee-only fiduciary advisor, paid a flat or hourly rate rather than a commission on the sale, has no financial stake in whether you buy. Worth the cost of an hour of their time before a decision measured in decades.

05

Know which standard you're getting

FIAs are regulated as insurance under state law, not as securities under the SEC — no prospectus, and in most states a "suitability" standard rather than a fiduciary one. The seller has to believe it's reasonably suitable, not that it's in your single best interest.

06

Understand state guaranty coverage, not FDIC

Annuities aren't bank deposits and carry no FDIC insurance. If a carrier fails, state guaranty associations typically cover up to $250,000 per owner per insurer — higher, $500,000, in New York, Washington, and New Jersey. A real backstop, but a capped one, worth knowing before concentrating a large sum with a single carrier.

Common questions

Frequently asked, in plain language

Can I lose all my money in one of these?

No — the 0% floor means the index-linked side of the contract can't credit a loss. But fees, especially a rider fee, are deducted regardless of index performance, and withdrawing early triggers a surrender charge — so "can't lose to the market" isn't the same as "guaranteed to never go down."

Is this the same as my 401(k)?

No. A 401(k) is your own account, holding investments you largely choose and own outright. An annuity is an insurance contract — you're transferring a risk to a carrier in exchange for a formula-based promise, not owning a portfolio.

What happens to my money when I die?

Depends on the contract, and on whether income had started. Before income starts, most contracts pay a death benefit — commonly the greater of account value or a minimum guaranteed contract value — to your named beneficiary. Once lifetime income has started, it depends on the payout option chosen: a life-only option stops at death; a period-certain or joint option keeps paying for a set time, or to a surviving spouse.

Can I get my money out early if I need it?

Partially. Most contracts allow a free withdrawal — commonly up to 10% a year — with no penalty. Beyond that, during the surrender-charge years, you'll pay a declining penalty and possibly a market value adjustment — see the surrender charges section above for the mechanics.

Is this some kind of scam?

No — it's a real, regulated insurance product, and the guarantees it makes are generally honored. "Complex and commission-driven" is a real concern worth taking seriously; it isn't the same thing as fraudulent.

Do I need one if I already have a pension or a solid 401(k)?

Not necessarily — see "where annuities fit" above. Someone with substantial guaranteed income already has less to gain from paying for more of it.

What if the insurance company goes bankrupt?

State guaranty associations back annuities up to a state-set limit — commonly $250,000, higher in a few states — see the buyer's checklist above.

Reference

Glossary

The terms this episode leans on most, in one place.

Premium
The amount you pay the insurer to buy the contract.
Cap rate
The most an FIA will credit in one period, however far the index rises.
Participation rate
The fraction of the index's gain that's credited.
Spread
A fee subtracted from the index's move before crediting.
Floor
The guaranteed minimum credit — 0% on almost every FIA.
Crediting method
How a period's index move is measured — annual point-to-point, monthly sum, monthly average.
Bonus
A stated percentage added at issue, usually to the benefit base rather than real account value.
Vesting schedule
The timetable that governs how much of a bonus you actually keep if you exit early.
Benefit base / income base
The notional figure an income rider uses to calculate guaranteed withdrawals — not spendable as a lump sum.
Rollup rate
The guaranteed rate the benefit base grows by during the deferral period.
GLWB (income rider)
Guaranteed Lifetime Withdrawal Benefit — the rider that turns a benefit base into income for life.
Payout rate
The age-banded percentage applied to the benefit base to set your guaranteed annual income.
Surrender charge
The declining penalty for withdrawing beyond the free amount during the surrender period.
Free withdrawal
The amount, commonly 10% a year, you can take out with no surrender charge.
MVA (Market Value Adjustment)
An up-or-down adjustment on excess withdrawals during the surrender period, reflecting rate moves since purchase.
Rider fee
The ongoing charge, typically 0.95%–1.50%/yr of the benefit base, for an income or other rider.
Free-look period
A state-guaranteed window, commonly 10–30 days, to cancel for a full refund after receiving the contract.
SPIA
Single Premium Immediate Annuity — converts a lump sum straight into income starting within about a year.
DIA / QLAC
Deferred Income Annuity / Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract — income that starts years or decades later.
MYGA
Multi-Year Guaranteed Annuity — a flat guaranteed rate for a set term, like a CD.
RILA
Registered Index-Linked Annuity — index-linked with a buffer instead of a 0% floor, and higher caps.
FIA
Fixed Indexed Annuity — this episode's main subject.
IRR
Internal rate of return — the annualized return that makes a stream of dated cash flows net to zero.
Illustration
The carrier's own numeric projection for a specific contract — guaranteed and non-guaranteed columns both.
