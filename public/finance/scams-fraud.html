Scams & Fraud Protection: Spot It, Lock It Down, Recover

How today's scams work and why smart people fall for them, a checker for the call or message in front of you, a security setup you can finish in an afternoon, what protection you actually have when money leaves your account, and a step-by-step plan if it happens to you or someone you love.

TL;DR

Americans reported losing a record $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025 (FTC), almost half of it to investment scams, and the real figure is far higher because most losses go unreported. Nearly every scam follows the same four steps: someone pretends to be from a trusted organization, says there's a problem or prize, pressures you to act now, and tells you to pay in a specific way, often a bank transfer, cryptocurrency or gift cards. The single best defense: hang up and call back on a number you already trust. Lock down the basics (credit freezes, a password manager, two-factor login, a carrier PIN, alerts), and know the rules: credit cards protect you best; money you send yourself by wire, payment app or crypto is usually gone. If it happens, act within hours, report it, and watch out for "recovery" scammers who come next.

Why smart people fall for it

The scammer's playbook

Scams don't work because victims are foolish. They work because they're designed to trigger fear, hope or urgency and stop careful thinking. The FTC boils almost every scam down to four moves; spot any one of them and slow down.

1

Pretend

They claim to be your bank, the IRS, Social Security, the police, Amazon, Microsoft, a grandchild, or a new romantic interest. Caller ID and email names are easy to fake.

2

Problem or prize

Your account is hacked, there's a warrant, a relative is in jail, your computer has a virus, or you've won, qualified, been selected.

3

Pressure

Act now, stay on the line, don't tell anyone, not even your bank. Secrecy and urgency are the biggest red flags of all.

4

Pay a specific way

Wire or bank transfer, payment app, cryptocurrency or a crypto ATM, gift cards, cash or gold handed to a courier. These are hard to reverse.

The one rule that beats almost every scam: if someone contacts you, never act inside that conversation. Hang up or close the message, then contact the organization yourself using a number or website you already know, such as the one on the back of your card.

Where the law helps, and where it doesn't

What protection you actually have

The key question is whether a payment was unauthorized (someone else made it) or authorized (you made it, even if you were tricked). The law protects the first far better than the second.

How the money leftProtectionWhat to do
Credit cardBest. Federal law caps your liability for unauthorized charges at $50 (most issuers charge $0), and you can dispute charges for things you didn't receiveCall the issuer and dispute in writing within 60 days of the statement
Debit card or unauthorized bank transferGood if you're fast: up to $50 if reported within 2 business days, up to $500 within 60 days of the statement, possibly unlimited after that. If a scammer used a code you gave them, it's generally still unauthorizedReport to the bank immediately and in writing
Payment app (Zelle, Venmo, Cash App) you sent yourselfWeak. Usually treated like cash. Zelle's network asks banks to reimburse some impostor scams (fake bank, government or company you already use), case by caseCall your bank anyway and say "impostor scam"; ask what the network's policy covers
Wire transferVery weak once sent; sometimes recalled if caught within hoursCall the bank's wire department immediately and ask for a recall
Cryptocurrency or crypto ATMAlmost none; transfers are effectively permanentReport to IC3.gov and the exchange or ATM operator; never pay a "recovery" service
Gift cardsNone, unless the card company freezes the balance before it's spentCall the number on the back of the card right away, with the receipt
Cash, gold or checks handed overNoneCall the police, especially if a courier is coming back

Speed matters

If it happens: the first 24 hours

1 · STOP

Cut off contact

Hang up, stop replying, and don't send anything more, however urgent they make it sound. If they had remote access to your computer, disconnect it from the internet.

2 · CALL

Call your bank and card companies

Use the number on your card or statement. Report the fraud, ask them to stop or recall payments, and change account passwords and PINs. Ask the bank to note the account.

3 · SECURE

Secure your accounts and identity

Change passwords, starting with email (it can reset everything else) and anything that shares that password; turn on two-factor login; freeze your credit at all three bureaus if any personal information was shared.

4 · REPORT

Report it

To the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, to the FBI at IC3.gov for online scams, and to local police if you need a report for your bank or insurer. For identity theft, IdentityTheft.gov builds a personal recovery plan and pre-filled letters.

5 · WATCH

Beware the second scam

Victims are often targeted again by "recovery" services, fake lawyers or fake government agents promising to get the money back for a fee. Real agencies never charge to recover money.

Be kind to yourself, or whoever was scammed. Shame keeps people from reporting and from asking for help, which is exactly what scammers count on. Professionals, executives and financial experts fall for these too; the scripts are built by full-time criminals.

When someone becomes you

Identity theft

The FTC received 1.36 million identity theft reports in 2025. Credit card fraud led (about 598,000 reports, mostly new accounts opened in someone's name), followed by loan and lease fraud, bank account fraud, tax and employment fraud, and phone and utility accounts.

FREEZE

Credit freezes

Free at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, and the strongest protection against new accounts in your name. You can lift a freeze in minutes when you apply for credit. See Credit & Debt.

TAXES

An IRS Identity Protection PIN

Anyone can request one at IRS.gov; it stops someone else from filing a tax return (and taking a refund) using your Social Security number.

BENEFITS

Claim your Social Security account

Create your my Social Security account at ssa.gov before a thief does, and check your earnings record each year.

MONITOR

Watch the reports, skip the paid service

Free weekly credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and alerts from your bank and card apps cover most of what paid "identity protection" services sell.

The people around you

Protecting your family

Parents and grandparents

Settings plus conversation

Trusted contacts at their banks, alerts, a credit freeze, and regular, friendly check-ins. More in Caring for Aging Parents.

Everyone

A family code word

Agree on a word or question only family knows, to check any "emergency" call, especially now that voices can be cloned.

Kids

Freeze their credit too

Children's Social Security numbers are attractive because nobody checks them for years. Parents can freeze a child's credit at all three bureaus for free.

Do this

A fraud protection checklist

Adopt the call-back rule

Never act inside a call, text or email you didn't start. Contact the organization yourself.

Freeze your credit at all three bureaus

And your children's. It's free and takes minutes each.

Use a password manager and two-factor login

Start with email and banking. Prefer an authenticator app or passkey over text-message codes.

Add a PIN and port-out lock with your phone carrier

Stops "SIM swap" thieves from taking over your number and your text codes.

Turn on transaction alerts

For every bank and card account, so you see fraud within minutes.

Get an IRS IP PIN and a my Social Security account

Before someone else uses your number.

Pick a family code word

For any "emergency" call asking for money.

Save the reporting sites

ReportFraud.ftc.gov, IC3.gov and IdentityTheft.gov, so you aren't searching for them in a panic.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

The caller ID showed my bank's real number. Isn't that proof?

No. Caller ID is easy to fake ("spoofing"). So are email sender names and text message sender names. Hang up and call the number on your card.

Is it safe to click "unsubscribe" or reply "STOP" to a scam text?

Better not to: it confirms your number is active. Report it instead by forwarding the text to 7726 (SPAM), then delete it.

Should I pay for an identity theft protection service?

Usually not necessary. Free credit freezes, free weekly credit reports, bank alerts and the IRS IP PIN cover most of it. Some services add insurance and help with recovery, which some people value; compare the price with what you'd get free.

My information was in a data breach. What should I do?

Freeze your credit, change the password for that account and anywhere you reused it, turn on two-factor login, and watch your statements. If your Social Security number was exposed, get an IRS IP PIN.

Can I get money back from a crypto scam?

Rarely. Report it to IC3.gov and the exchange quickly, because exchanges sometimes freeze funds that reach them. Anyone who contacts you offering to recover crypto for a fee is almost certainly running a second scam.

Is it a scam if they want me to install an app or share my screen?

Very often. Real banks and agencies don't need remote access to your computer or phone. Remote-access apps let a scammer see your accounts and one-time codes.

Reference

Glossary

Phishing / smishing / vishing
Fake emails, texts or phone calls designed to steal information or money.
Spoofing
Faking a phone number, email address or website so it looks legitimate.
Impostor scam
A scammer pretending to be a business, agency, relative or romantic partner.
Pig butchering
A long con that builds trust, then steers the victim into a fake investment.
Authorized push payment
A payment you send yourself, even if tricked; generally harder to recover.
Unauthorized transfer
A payment someone else made from your account; protected by federal law if reported promptly.
Chargeback
A card dispute that reverses a charge.
Credit freeze
A free block on new credit being opened in your name.
Fraud alert
A note on your credit file asking lenders to verify your identity; free for one year, seven for identity theft victims.
Two-factor authentication
A second check, such as an app code or passkey, in addition to a password.
Passkey
A phishing-resistant login stored on your device instead of a password.
SIM swap
A thief moving your phone number to their device to receive your codes.
IRS IP PIN
A six-digit number that blocks others from filing taxes with your Social Security number.
Recovery scam
A follow-up scam targeting victims with promises to get lost money back.
Data breach
An incident that exposes personal information held by a company.
