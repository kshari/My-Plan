Which Account? Where Each Dollar Goes

401(k), IRA, Roth, HSA, 529, a plain brokerage account: what each one actually is, what it does with your taxes, and the order to fill them in. Includes a planner that takes the amount you can save each year and shows where each dollar should go, using 2026 limits.

TL;DR

An account isn't an investment. It's a tax wrapper around your investments, and the wrapper decides when the IRS takes its share: now (Roth), later (traditional), for medical costs never (HSA), or a little every year (a regular brokerage account). The order that works for most people follows the Money Roadmap: a small emergency fund, then enough in your 401(k) to get the full employer match, then pay off high-interest debt, finish the emergency fund, fill an HSA if you can, then an IRA, then the rest of the 401(k), then goals like a 529, and only then a taxable account. For 2026 the limits are $24,500 for a 401(k) (more from age 50), $7,500 for an IRA, and $4,400 / $8,750 for an HSA. Roth versus traditional comes down to one question: is your tax rate higher now or in retirement? If you can't tell, split between them. Inside every account, the investments are the same honest baseline from Investing Basics.

Start here

Accounts are wrappers, not investments

People say "I have a Roth" or "I'm in my 401(k)" as if those were investments. They aren't. Each is a container with its own tax rules, and inside it you choose the actual investments, ideally the low-cost index funds from Investing Basics. The same fund can sit in any of them. What changes is when, and whether, you pay tax.

Money in an account can be taxed at three points: when you put it in, while it grows, and when you take it out. Every account type is just a different combination of those three.

Traditional 401(k) / IRATax break nowContributions lower this year's taxable incomeNo taxTaxed as incomeEvery dollar withdrawn, at your rate then

Taxable brokerageTaxed nowPartly taxedDividends and any gains you realize, each yearGains taxedOnly the growth, usually at lower capital-gains rates

The trade for all those breaks is rules: yearly contribution limits, and penalties for taking money out early or for the wrong purpose. The brokerage account has no limits and no restrictions, which is exactly why it comes last. Every other account gives you a tax deal in exchange for patience.

Why the steps come in this order

01 · 04

Emergency cash brackets everything

A small cushion comes first so a car repair doesn't land on a credit card, and the full three to six months comes once the match and expensive debt are handled. Where to hold it is its own topic (Where to Keep Your Cash, coming in Tier 2).

02

The match is an instant 50%–100% return

If your employer puts in 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, that's a 50% gain before the money is invested at all. Nothing else on this list is guaranteed to come close. The average employer contribution in Vanguard's plans hit a record 4.7% of pay in 2025. Not taking it is the costliest mistake on this page.

03

Credit card debt beats any investment

Paying off a card charging 20%+ is a guaranteed 20%+ return. No account on this list reliably earns that.

05

The HSA is the only triple tax break

Money goes in untaxed, grows untaxed, and comes out untaxed for medical costs, which everyone eventually has. That's why it jumps ahead of the IRA, if your health plan qualifies.

06 · 07

IRA, then back to the 401(k)

After the match, an IRA usually offers a wider, cheaper fund menu than a workplace plan, and a Roth IRA's contributions stay reachable in a pinch. Once the IRA is full, go back and raise the 401(k) toward its $24,500 limit. If your 401(k) has excellent low-cost index funds, the order of these two matters less.

08 · 09

Other goals, then taxable investing

A 529 for a child's education, a house down payment, extra payments on low-rate debt: these are weighed against each other by timeline, not in a fixed order. The Roadmap puts retirement room first because students can borrow for college, but nobody lends you money to retire. Anything left goes into a plain brokerage account with the same index funds.

Reference

The accounts, one at a time

Each card covers what the account is for, the 2026 limit, the tax deal, when you can get the money, and the thing people most often get wrong.

Workplace

401(k), 403(b), 457(b), TSP

Retirement plans run by your employer, funded straight from your paycheck. Most now offer both a traditional (pre-tax) and a Roth version: 98% of Vanguard-administered plans did at the end of 2025, though only 18% of workers used it.

2026 limit
$24,500 from you; +$8,000 catch-up at 50+, or +$11,250 at ages 60–63. Up to $72,000 total with employer money.
Tax
Traditional: deduct now, taxed later. Roth: taxed now, tax-free later.
Access
Generally from age 59½. Earlier usually means income tax plus a 10% penalty, with exceptions (leaving your job in or after the year you turn 55, for example). Many plans allow loans.
Watch for
High-fee fund menus (choose the index or target-date options), and a vesting schedule on the match. New for 2026: if your 2025 wages were over $150,000, catch-up contributions must go in as Roth.
Individual · after-tax

Roth IRA

An account you open yourself at any brokerage. You contribute money you've already paid tax on, and everything, including decades of growth, comes out tax-free in retirement.

2026 limit
$7,500 (+$1,100 at 50+), shared with any traditional IRA. You need earned income at least equal to what you put in.
Income cap
Phases out between $153,000–$168,000 (single) and $242,000–$252,000 (married, joint).
Access
Your contributions can come out any time, tax- and penalty-free. Earnings are tax-free after 59½ once the account is 5 years old.
Watch for
Over the income cap? The "backdoor" route (a nondeductible traditional IRA converted to Roth) works, but get tax help if you have other pre-tax IRA money. The pro-rata rule can make part of the conversion taxable.
Individual · pre-tax

Traditional IRA

The pre-tax version of the IRA. Most useful as the landing spot when you roll over an old 401(k), or for a deduction when you don't have a workplace plan.

2026 limit
$7,500 (+$1,100 at 50+), shared with any Roth IRA.
Deduction
Full if neither spouse has a workplace plan. If you do, it phases out at $81,000–$91,000 (single) or $129,000–$149,000 (joint). If only your spouse does, it phases out at $242,000–$252,000.
Access
Like a 401(k): taxed as income, 10% penalty before 59½ with some exceptions. Required withdrawals start at 73 (75 if born 1960 or later).
Watch for
Leaving a rollover in cash. Money moved into an IRA isn't invested until you choose investments.
Health

HSA (health savings account)

A savings and investment account paired with a high-deductible health plan. It's the only account that's tax-free going in, while growing, and coming out (for medical costs), and the money is yours for life, even if you change jobs or plans.

2026 limit
$4,400 self-only, $8,750 family; +$1,000 from age 55.
Eligible if
Your plan's deductible is at least $1,700 (self) or $3,400 (family). New for 2026: bronze and catastrophic marketplace plans also qualify. No contributions once you're on Medicare.
Access
Tax-free for qualified medical costs any time. Other uses before 65: tax plus a 20% penalty. After 65: taxed like a traditional IRA, no penalty.
Watch for
Leaving it all in cash. If you can pay today's bills out of pocket, invest the HSA and keep receipts. You can reimburse yourself years later.
Education

529 plan

State-sponsored education savings. You don't have to use your own state's plan, but many states give a tax deduction only for theirs.

Limit
No federal yearly cap. Contributions count as gifts ($19,000 per person per child in 2026 before any gift-tax paperwork).
Tax
Grows tax-free; withdrawals are tax-free for college, up to $20,000 a year of K–12 tuition (up from $10,000), and, since 2025, some K–12 costs and workforce credentials.
Leftovers
Switch to another family member, or roll up to $35,000 over the child's lifetime into their Roth IRA (account open 15+ years, within the yearly IRA limit).
Watch for
Non-qualified withdrawals pay tax plus a 10% penalty on the earnings. Some states haven't adopted the newer uses for state-tax purposes.
No strings

Taxable brokerage account

A regular investment account. No limits, no penalties, no age rules. You pay tax on dividends and on gains when you sell.

Limit
None.
Tax
Long-term gains (held over a year) are taxed at 0% up to $49,450 of taxable income (single) or $98,900 (joint) in 2026, 15% above that, and 20% at the top. Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income.
Best for
Goals before 59½, and money beyond the other accounts' limits. Broad index funds and ETFs keep the yearly tax bill low.
Watch for
Frequent selling, which turns long-term growth into this year's tax bill.

Also on the menu

  • Self-employed? A Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA lets you save as both employee and employer, up to $72,000 in 2026 depending on your earnings.
  • Trump accounts (new in 2026). A retirement-style account for children under 18. Children born 2025–2028 can get a one-time $1,000 government deposit, and families can add up to $5,000 a year (employers up to $2,500 of that) since July 4, 2026. The money must sit in low-cost U.S. stock index funds, and from 18 the account follows traditional-IRA rules. For college savings a 529 is usually better. Kids & Money (Tier 2) compares them.
  • Flexible spending accounts (FSAs). Not investment accounts, but real tax breaks: up to $3,400 for health costs and, new for 2026, up to $7,500 per household for dependent care (up from $5,000). Mostly use-it-or-lose-it within the year.

The big choice inside most accounts

Roth or traditional? Pay the tax now or later

This decision sounds complicated, but it rests on one comparison: your tax rate on this money today, versus your tax rate when you take it out. If the rates are equal, the two come out exactly the same. Try it.

The same paycheck money, two ways, after all taxes
Assumes a steady 7% yearly return and that the paycheck cost is the same either way: traditional invests the full amount pre-tax; Roth invests what's left after tax. Try setting both rates equal. The defaults are a 22% federal bracket plus a 5% state tax now, and 12% plus 5% in retirement.

For reference, 2026 federal brackets on taxable income (after the $16,100 single / $32,200 joint standard deduction): 12% up to $50,400 single or $100,800 joint, 22% up to $105,700 / $211,400, 24% up to $201,775 / $403,550, then 32%, 35% and 37%. Taxes 101 (the next episode) explains why your bracket and your average rate are different numbers.

Lean Roth if —

your tax rate is low now, or likely to rise

Early career in the 10%–12% brackets, a big income jump ahead, or already holding plenty of pre-tax money. Roth money also has no required withdrawals (Roth 401(k)s included, since 2024), and heirs inherit it tax-free. A maxed Roth also holds more after-tax money than a maxed traditional account, because the limit is the same dollar amount.

Lean traditional if —

you're in your peak earning years

In the 22%–24% brackets or higher now and expecting less income in retirement. The deduction also lowers this year's adjusted gross income, which can help you qualify for credits and subsidies. Withdrawals in retirement fill the low brackets first, so your average rate then is often lower than your top rate today.

Can't tell? Split it. Nobody knows future tax rates. Putting some money in each gives you tax diversification: in retirement you can pull from whichever bucket costs less in tax that year. Also note that employer matches usually go in as pre-tax money even if you choose Roth, so most people end up with some of both anyway.

Avoid these

Common, costly mistakes

01

Leaving the match on the table

Contributing 3% when your employer matches up to 6% gives away free pay every year. Automatic enrollment often starts you below the match, so check your rate.

02

Cashing out an old 401(k) when you change jobs

That triggers income tax plus a 10% penalty, and ends decades of growth. Leave it, roll it into the new plan, or do a direct rollover to an IRA.

03

Money sitting uninvested

Contributions and rollovers into an IRA or HSA often land in cash until you pick investments. Opening the account is only step one.

04

Contributing to a Roth IRA over the income limit

Excess contributions are charged a 6% penalty every year until fixed. If a raise pushes you into the phase-out range, check before contributing, or use the backdoor route.

05

Forgetting beneficiaries

Retirement accounts pass by the beneficiary form, not your will. Update it after marriage, divorce, and births.

06

Picking an account for its product, not its tax deal

An annuity or insurance policy sold "inside" a retirement account adds cost without adding any tax benefit, because the account is already tax-sheltered. Compare against the baseline in Investing Basics.

Do this

A setup checklist

Find your match formula

Look in your benefits portal or plan summary for how much the employer matches and the vesting schedule. Set your contribution to at least the full-match rate.

Choose the funds inside the 401(k)

A target-date index fund or a broad index fund. Check each option's expense ratio.

Check whether your health plan is HSA-eligible

If it is, fund the HSA and switch its cash to investments beyond what you'll spend this year.

Open an IRA at a low-cost brokerage

Roth if your income allows and your tax rate is modest now. Set up an automatic monthly transfer, and make sure it's invested, not sitting in cash.

Decide Roth vs. traditional for the 401(k)

Use the comparison above. Unsure? Split it.

Round up old accounts

List every old 401(k) and IRA. Consolidate where it simplifies things, by direct rollover only.

Name beneficiaries everywhere

Every 401(k), IRA, HSA and 529 has its own form.

Raise your rate every year

Add 1% each time you get a raise until you reach your target. Many plans can do this automatically.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

How much should I be saving in total?

A common target is around 15% of pay for retirement, counting any employer match, started early. Vanguard's plan participants averaged 12.1% including employer money in 2025, a record. If that's out of reach, start with the match and raise your rate 1% a year.

401(k) or IRA first?

401(k) first, up to the match. After that, the IRA usually wins on fund choice and cost, then return to the 401(k). If your 401(k) offers very cheap index funds, simply raising the 401(k) is fine too.

My 401(k) funds are expensive. Should I skip it?

Never skip the match. It outweighs even high fees. Beyond the match, use the plan's cheapest broad index or target-date option, fill the IRA first, and when you leave the job, roll the money into a low-cost IRA.

I earn too much for a Roth IRA. Am I out of luck?

No. A Roth 401(k) has no income limit, and the backdoor Roth IRA (contribute to a nondeductible traditional IRA, then convert) is allowed. If you hold other pre-tax IRA money, get tax help first because of the pro-rata rule.

Can my Roth IRA double as my emergency fund?

It can serve as a backstop, since contributions come out penalty-free. But money you take out can't be put back beyond that year's limit, so you lose tax-free room for good. Keep the real emergency fund in cash.

529, Trump account, or custodial Roth IRA for my kids?

For college, the 529 is the purpose-built tool. A custodial Roth IRA needs the child to have earned income. Trump accounts are new, retirement-style, and restricted until 18. Kids & Money (Tier 2) covers all three side by side.

Should I pay down my mortgage instead?

Usually not before the match, the HSA and IRA, since most mortgage rates are below long-run stock returns and have no tax shelter. It's a "step 08" goal weighed on rate and peace of mind. Credit & Debt and Rent vs. Buy & Mortgages (Tier 2) go deeper.

Reference

Glossary

Tax-advantaged account
An account with a tax break (now, later, or both) in exchange for limits and withdrawal rules.
Traditional (pre-tax)
Contributions lower this year's taxable income; withdrawals are taxed as income.
Roth
Contributions are made with taxed money; qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
Employer match
Money your employer adds to your 401(k) based on what you contribute.
Vesting
The schedule by which employer contributions become fully yours.
Catch-up contribution
Extra contribution room from age 50 (401(k), IRA) or 55 (HSA).
Phase-out range
The income band over which a tax benefit (like Roth IRA eligibility) shrinks to zero.
MAGI
Modified adjusted gross income, the income figure used for Roth IRA and deduction limits.
Backdoor Roth
Contributing to a nondeductible traditional IRA, then converting it to a Roth.
Pro-rata rule
When converting, the IRS treats all your pre-tax IRA money together, which can make part of a conversion taxable.
Rollover
Moving retirement money between accounts without tax. Direct rollovers avoid withholding and mistakes.
RMD
Required minimum distribution: yearly withdrawals from pre-tax accounts starting at 73 or 75.
HDHP
High-deductible health plan: the kind of health insurance that makes you HSA-eligible.
Qualified expense
A use the account's tax break covers: medical for an HSA, education for a 529.
Taxable brokerage
A regular investment account with no special tax treatment and no restrictions.
Capital gain
Profit from selling an investment for more than you paid. Long-term (held over a year) gains get lower rates.
Marginal tax rate
The rate on your next dollar of income: your bracket.
Tax diversification
Holding both pre-tax and Roth money so you can choose which to draw from in retirement.
