The Money Roadmap

The starting point for this whole series: the basic vocabulary of personal finance, the order to tackle financial priorities in, and the specific decisions that matter most on the way into retirement. Three levels, roughly matched to where you are in life — pick one, or read straight through.

TL;DR

Personal finance reduces to four moves — earn, spend less than you earn, save and invest the difference, and protect it — and almost everything else is a variation on those four. Level 1 covers that vocabulary and the mechanics of compounding, debt, and insurance. Level 2 lays out the order to tackle financial priorities in, from a first paycheck through the peak earning years, and which accounts and products typically show up at each stage. Level 3 is for the years right around retirement, where the job flips from growing a balance to turning it into income that has to last — Social Security and Medicare timing, withdrawal strategy, healthcare and long-term-care costs, and estate basics. Other episodes in this series, like Annuities Explained, go deep on individual products this page only introduces.

Level 1 · Money 101

The four moves, and the two numbers worth tracking

Strip away every product, account, and acronym and personal finance comes down to four moves. Everything else in this page — every account type, every insurance product, every rule of thumb — is a variation on one of these four.

EARN

Income

Money coming in — a paycheck, a business, investment income. The foundation everything else is built on; no amount of budgeting substitutes for it.

SPEND

Expenses, and the gap below income

Money going out. The gap between what comes in and what goes out — not the size of the paycheck — is what actually determines whether there's anything left to build with.

SAVE

Saving and investing the difference

Putting that gap to work — first as a cash cushion, then invested for growth — so money earns more money instead of sitting idle or, worse, not existing at all because it was spent.

PROTECT

Protecting what the first three built

Insurance, an emergency fund, and legal documents that keep one bad event — a job loss, a diagnosis, an early death — from undoing years of the first three moves.

The two numbers worth actually tracking

Of everything a budgeting app or spreadsheet can show, two numbers matter far more than the rest.

Cash flow

Income minus expenses, this month

Positive cash flow is the only source of money for every goal below — an emergency fund, retirement, a house. A "budget" is really just naming, on purpose, where that gap goes instead of finding out by accident at the end of the month.

Net worth

Assets minus liabilities, right now

An asset is anything with value — cash, a retirement account, a home. A liability is anything owed — a credit card balance, a student loan, a mortgage. Net worth is the single number that answers "how am I doing," independent of any one month's cash flow.

Income can be irregular, spending can spike in any given month, and neither one alone tells the whole story. Cash flow and net worth, tracked over time rather than judged in any single month, are what everything else on this page is ultimately trying to move in the right direction.

Level 1 · Money 101

The power of time — compounding

Compounding is interest earning interest — money grows, and then the growth itself starts growing too. It's the single biggest reason personal finance rewards starting far more than it rewards the amount of any one contribution. The chart below makes that concrete with one deliberately unfair comparison.

Both investors below assume $300/month invested at a 7%/yr average return, a common long-run planning assumption for a diversified stock portfolio — illustrative, not a guarantee of any specific outcome.

The investor who contributed for only 10 years — and put in a third as much money — ends up with more. The other twenty years of compounding did more work than another twenty years of contributions could make up for. This isn't an argument against saving in your 30s, 40s, and beyond — it's the single strongest argument for starting now, whatever "now" is, rather than waiting for a more convenient year that never quite arrives.

Level 1 · Money 101

Debt and credit, in plain terms

Not all debt behaves the same way, and a credit score is really just a lender's shorthand for "how risky is it to lend this person money." Both matter enough to get right early.

Debt that can work for you

Low, fixed rates on things that build value

A mortgage, a reasonable-rate student loan, or a low-rate auto loan can be a rational way to finance something that builds equity or earning power, especially when the rate is well below what a diversified investment could plausibly earn over the same years.

Debt to get rid of fast

High, often double-digit rates on things that don't

Credit cards, payday loans, and many personal loans routinely charge 20%+ annually. Paying that off is a guaranteed, risk-free return few investments can match — which is exactly why it sits so early in the priority order in Level 2.

What actually moves a credit score

Five factors, roughly in order of how much they matter.

35%

Payment history

Whether you've paid on time. The single biggest factor — one 30-day-late payment can do real, lasting damage.

30%

Credit utilization

How much of your available credit you're using. Keeping balances well below the limit — even if you pay in full every month — tends to help.

15%

Length of credit history

Older accounts help. It's one reason to think twice before closing your oldest credit card, even one you rarely use.

10%

Credit mix

A track record across a couple of different types of credit (a card, a loan) counts slightly in your favor over just one type.

10%

New inquiries

Applying for several new accounts in a short window is a small, temporary ding — worth spacing out where you can.

A credit score isn't just about qualifying for a loan — it routinely affects the interest rate on a mortgage or car loan, and in many states even auto and homeowners insurance premiums. Paying the statement balance in full every month avoids interest entirely while still building the payment history that drives most of the score.

Level 1 · Money 101

Protecting the downside

Insurance and an emergency fund do the same job through different mechanisms: both convert a rare, potentially catastrophic loss into a small, predictable, plannable cost. An emergency fund is really just self-insurance — money you've set aside instead of paying a premium to someone else — for the smaller, more frequent losses not worth filing a claim over.

RiskWhat typically protects against it
Small, frequentA flat tire, a broken appliance, a medical copay An emergency fund — the cost of a claim or a loan, without either.
Large medical eventIllness, injury, hospitalization Health insurance, backed by an emergency fund for the deductible and out-of-pocket portion.
Loss of incomeIllness or injury that stops you from working Disability insurance — statistically more likely during working years than an early death, and far less commonly carried.
Premature deathDependents rely on your income Term life insurance, sized to replace that income for as long as it's needed.
Property lossA house fire, a car accident, theft Homeowners/renters and auto insurance.
A large lawsuitLiability beyond a standard policy's limit An umbrella policy — inexpensive, and worth it once net worth is meaningful.
Outliving your savingsA long retirement Social Security, retirement savings sized to your time horizon, and — for a narrow slice of savings, late in the sequence — an annuity; see Annuities Explained for that trade-off in full.

A common starting target: a small "starter" emergency fund of $1,000–$2,000, then, once higher-interest debt is under control, a fuller cushion of three to six months of essential expenses, held somewhere fully liquid — not invested in the market, and not earning much of anything is the point. Level 2 places both of these precisely in the order they're worth tackling.

Level 2 · The Sequence

The order to do things in

Almost every personal-finance question — "should I pay off debt or invest," "should I save or contribute to my 401(k)" — has the same answer: it depends where you are in this order. Later steps are rarely wrong to want; they're just usually wrong to prioritize before the steps ahead of them.

01

A starter emergency fund

$1,000–$2,000, fully liquid, before anything else — so a flat tire or a broken laptop doesn't become new debt.

02

The employer match

Contribute enough to a 401(k)/403(b) to capture the full employer match. It's an immediate, guaranteed return no later step can beat.

03

High-interest debt

Credit cards and other double-digit-rate debt. Paying it off is a guaranteed, risk-free return most investments can't match.

04

The full emergency fund

Three to six months of essential expenses, fully liquid — more if income is variable or a single earner supports the household.

05

An HSA, if you're eligible

Triple tax-advantaged — deductible going in, tax-free growth, tax-free for qualified medical expenses — available only alongside a high-deductible health plan.

06

Max out tax-advantaged retirement room

A Roth or traditional IRA, then increasing the 401(k) contribution rate toward its annual limit — see the reference table below for current numbers.

07

Other named goals

A house down payment, a child's education (a 529 plan), or extra payments on lower-rate debt — priced against each other by rate and timeline, not a fixed order.

08

Taxable investing

Broad, low-cost index investing, once tax-advantaged space is maxed and there's still a gap between income and spending.

09

Protect what's been built

Term life and disability insurance once others depend on your income, an umbrella policy once net worth is meaningful, a will once there's anything — or anyone — to protect.

Being "behind" on a later step is normal and rarely permanent — catch-up contributions exist for exactly that reason (see the reference table below). The order matters more than any single step's size.

Level 2 · The Sequence

Starting out — first job through mid-20s

The habits formed here matter more than the dollar amounts, which are usually small at this stage regardless.

PAY

Understand your paycheck

What's withheld for taxes, and, if offered, what a 401(k) contribution and employer match actually mean — free money that only shows up if you opt in.

TRACK

Track spending, loosely

Not a strict budget necessarily — just enough visibility to know the cash-flow number from Level 1 isn't a surprise at month's end.

CREDIT

Start building credit, carefully

A first credit card, used for small recurring purchases and paid in full every month, starts the payment-history clock without carrying a balance.

LOANS

Know your student loan terms

Interest rate, whether it's federal or private, and what repayment or forgiveness programs might apply — this determines where it falls in the priority order above.

START

Contribute something, even if small

Even 3–5% into a 401(k) beats waiting for a "someday" that keeps moving — the compounding math from Level 1 rewards the early years disproportionately.

Level 2 · The Sequence

Building — late 20s through 30s

This is typically when income rises, and when other people — a spouse, a child — start depending on the financial decisions being made.

LIFE

Term life insurance, once someone depends on your income

Sized to replace that income for as long as it's needed — commonly until a mortgage is paid off or kids are grown. Cheapest when bought young and healthy.

DISABLE

Disability insurance

Often overlooked next to life insurance, despite being the more statistically likely risk during working years. Many employers offer a base policy; supplemental coverage is often worth pricing.

LEGAL

A basic will and beneficiary designations

Especially with a child — a will is what names a guardian. Beneficiary forms on retirement accounts and life insurance override the will, so both need to exist and stay current.

HOME

The home-buying math, if buying

A 20% down payment avoids private mortgage insurance; a common guideline caps total housing cost around 28–30% of gross income, though local markets vary widely.

KIDS

Education savings, if applicable

A 529 plan grows tax-free for qualified education expenses — worth starting small and early rather than waiting to fund it fully.

RAISE

Raise the contribution rate with every raise

Increasing a retirement contribution percentage alongside income increases, rather than only increasing spending, is one of the highest-leverage habits in this whole page.

Level 2 · The Sequence

Peak earning — 40s and 50s

Income is often at its highest here, and so is the highest-leverage window for closing gaps from earlier decades.

MAX

Push toward the contribution limits

This is typically the highest-income, highest-savings-rate stretch of a career — the reference table below has current 401(k), IRA, and HSA limits.

CATCH-UP

Use catch-up contributions once eligible

Extra room opens at 50, and an even larger "super" catch-up window opens from 60–63 — see the reference table below for the current numbers.

TAXABLE

Diversify beyond retirement accounts

Once tax-advantaged space is maxed, a taxable brokerage account adds flexibility retirement accounts don't have — money reachable before 59½ without a penalty.

REVIEW

Reassess insurance needs

As a mortgage shrinks and kids near financial independence, the case for a large term life policy often shrinks with it — worth revisiting rather than auto-renewing.

LTC

Start thinking about long-term care

Costs can be large and aren't covered by Medicare beyond limited stays — Level 3 covers this in more depth, but the planning window is wider than most people assume.

Level 3 · Nearing Retirement

Turning savings into income

Everything up to this point has been about growing a balance. In the years right around retirement, the job flips: the same balance now has to generate income that lasts an unknown number of years, without running out too early or leaving an unnecessarily large amount unspent.

RATE

A starting withdrawal rate

A long-standing rule of thumb is withdrawing around 4% of a portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjusting that dollar amount for inflation each year after. It's a starting heuristic, not a law — many planners today discuss a range depending on market valuations and how flexible your spending can be.

BUCKETS

A bucket strategy

One common structure: cash for 1–2 years of expenses, high-quality bonds for the next several years, and stocks for the portion not needed for a decade or more — so a market downturn doesn't force selling stocks at a low point to cover this month's bills.

RISK

Sequence-of-returns risk

A market downturn in the first few years of retirement can do outsized, hard-to-recover-from damage — withdrawing from a shrinking balance locks in losses in a way the same downturn wouldn't if it happened mid-career. This is precisely the risk a guaranteed floor is built to address for a slice of savings; see the floor/cap trade-off in Annuities Explained for the mechanics and the cost of that guarantee.

Level 3 · Nearing Retirement

Social Security and Medicare timing

Two of the highest-leverage, hardest-to-reverse decisions in this entire page are simply when to start each of these — and they run on separate clocks.

Claim Social Security at6267 (full retirement age)*70
% of full benefit70%100%124%

*Full retirement age (FRA) is 67 for anyone born 1960 or later. Between FRA and 70, benefits grow by a guaranteed 8% a year via delayed retirement credits — inflation-adjusted, for life, with no market risk. There's no benefit to delaying past 70.

Medicare runs on a completely separate, age-based clock — and it doesn't wait for a Social Security claiming decision. The Initial Enrollment Period is a seven-month window: three months before the month you turn 65, that birthday month itself, and three months after. Missing it — without qualifying employer coverage — triggers a 10% Part B premium penalty for every 12-month period enrollment was delayed, added to the premium for as long as it's paid, typically for life.

A common trap

Delaying Social Security to 70 does not delay Medicare

Someone planning to claim Social Security at 70 still generally needs to enroll in Medicare at 65 on its own separate timeline, unless still covered by a qualifying employer plan — otherwise the late-enrollment penalty applies regardless of the Social Security decision.

Worth deciding deliberately

Which to claim first isn't automatic

The two decisions are independent and both effectively permanent once made — worth mapping out together, ideally with a specific plan for the years between retiring and each date, rather than defaulting to whichever paperwork arrives first.

Level 3 · Nearing Retirement

Healthcare, long-term care, and taxes in retirement

Three costs are easy to underplan for because none of them show up as a single obvious bill during the working years that precede them.

Long-term care

Medicare covers only limited nursing-home or home-health stays, not extended long-term care. Costs for extended care can be substantial and are rarely small enough to casually self-fund. The main choices are self-funding deliberately (setting money aside specifically for this), a dedicated long-term-care insurance policy, or a hybrid life-insurance/long-term-care policy — each with real trade-offs worth pricing well before the need is urgent, since LTC insurance becomes harder and more expensive to buy the closer you get to needing it.

Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

Traditional (pre-tax) retirement accounts require you to start withdrawing a minimum amount each year, whether or not the money is needed.

BornRMDs must start at age
1951–195973
1960 or later75

Per SECURE 2.0. Missing an RMD carries a real penalty, so this date is worth marking well in advance rather than discovering it after the fact. Roth IRAs are not subject to RMDs during the original owner's lifetime.

The years before RMDs start

The stretch between retiring and RMDs beginning is often the lowest-income, lowest-tax-bracket window of a person's adult life — which makes it a common time to consider converting some traditional retirement savings to a Roth account, paying tax now at a known, often lower rate instead of later at a rate that isn't yet known. Whether that trade makes sense depends heavily on individual circumstances and is worth running by a tax professional rather than assuming it as a default.

Level 3 · Nearing Retirement

Estate basics

"Estate planning" sounds like it's only for large estates. In practice, it's mostly a small set of documents that keep decisions in the hands of the people you'd actually want making them.

WILL

A will

Names who receives what, and — critically, if there are minor children — who would raise them. Dying without one lets state law decide both.

FORMS

Beneficiary designations

The forms on file with a 401(k), IRA, or life insurance policy override whatever the will says for that specific account. Worth checking after every major life event — marriage, divorce, a new child — since these are easy to forget to update.

POA

Power of attorney

Names someone to make financial decisions on your behalf if you're unable to — without it, even a spouse may need a court's permission to act.

HEALTH

A healthcare proxy and advance directive

Names someone to make medical decisions if you can't, and records your own wishes about the kind of care you'd want — a conversation worth having before it's urgently needed.

TRUST

A trust, if the situation calls for it

Not needed by everyone, but worth a conversation with an estate attorney for a blended family, a minor or special-needs beneficiary, or a desire to avoid probate or keep the details private.

DIGITAL

A list of accounts and access

A modern, easy-to-skip addition — where accounts and important documents actually live, and how a trusted person could get to them, is often the single most useful thing left behind.

Common questions

Frequently asked, in plain language

Where do I actually start?

If you're not sure, start at Level 1 — the vocabulary and mechanics there make the rest of the page easier to use. If you already know the basics, jump straight to the level that matches your age and situation.

I have credit card debt — should I still contribute to my 401(k)?

At least enough to capture the full employer match (step 02 in the priority order) — that's usually a better return than the interest rate on the debt. Beyond the match, the debt typically comes first.

How much should I actually have in savings?

A starter fund of $1,000–$2,000 first, then three to six months of essential expenses once higher-priority debt is handled — more if your income is variable or you're the sole earner in your household.

Is a will only for wealthy people?

No — it matters most for naming a guardian for minor children and directing specific belongings, regardless of the size of the estate.

Do I need life insurance if I don't have kids?

Usually only if someone depends on your income or would be left with debt you co-signed — a spouse, a business partner, or a co-signer on a loan. Without dependents, the case is much weaker.

When should I start paying attention to Level 3?

Roughly five to ten years before your planned retirement date — earlier for Social Security and Medicare timing decisions, which benefit from being mapped out well in advance rather than decided at the last minute.

What if I feel behind on all of this?

The order in Level 2 still applies regardless of age — and catch-up contributions exist specifically because starting later is common, not a disqualifying mistake.

Glossary

Terms used across all three levels, in one place.

Net worth
Assets minus liabilities — a single snapshot of financial position.
Cash flow
Income minus expenses over a period, usually a month.
Emergency fund
Liquid cash set aside for unplanned expenses, separate from investments.
Compounding
Growth earning further growth on top of itself over time.
Credit score
A lender's numeric estimate of repayment risk, driven mostly by payment history and credit utilization.
Employer match
Money an employer contributes to your retirement account, tied to your own contribution.
Roth vs. traditional
Roth accounts are funded with after-tax money and withdraw tax-free; traditional accounts are funded pre-tax and withdrawals are taxed as income.
HSA
Health Savings Account — triple tax-advantaged, paired with a high-deductible health plan.
529 plan
A tax-advantaged account for education expenses.
Term life insurance
Life insurance covering a fixed period, paying a death benefit only if death occurs during that term.
Disability insurance
Replaces a portion of income if illness or injury prevents working.
Umbrella policy
Extra liability coverage above the limits of home and auto insurance.
Full retirement age (FRA)
The age — 67 for anyone born 1960 or later — at which Social Security pays 100% of the calculated benefit.
Delayed retirement credit
The 8%/yr increase in Social Security benefits for each year claiming is delayed past FRA, up to age 70.
Medicare Initial Enrollment Period
The seven-month window around age 65 to enroll in Medicare without a late penalty.
RMD
Required Minimum Distribution — the mandatory annual withdrawal from traditional retirement accounts starting at 73 or 75, depending on birth year.
Roth conversion
Voluntarily moving money from a traditional account to a Roth account, paying tax on the converted amount now.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The outsized damage a market downturn can do when it occurs in the first few years of retirement withdrawals.
Bucket strategy
Dividing retirement savings into cash, bonds, and stocks by how soon each portion will be spent.
Power of attorney
A legal document naming someone to make financial decisions on your behalf if you're unable to.
Beneficiary designation
The named recipient on file for a retirement account or life insurance policy — overrides a will for that account.
