Life Insurance Explained

The major components of life insurance, in plain terms and from a personal-finance perspective — not tied to any one carrier or policy. This installment covers what life insurance is, compares the main policy types, and then digs into the two permanent types getting the most sales dollars right now — whole life and indexed universal life (IUL) — including how each actually credits growth.

TL;DR

Life insurance is fundamentally insurance against dying too soon, not an investment — you pay a premium, and if you die while covered, your beneficiaries get a death benefit. Term life buys pure protection for a fixed period at the lowest cost. Permanent policies — whole life and IUL among them — bundle that same protection with a savings component called cash value, at a much higher premium, and that bundling is where almost all of the complexity (and almost all of the commission) lives. Whole life credits cash value on a slow, contractually guaranteed schedule plus a non-guaranteed dividend; IUL credits cash value linked to a market index, bounded by a cap and a 0% floor, with fees that grow as you age. Both can take a decade or more before cash value even equals the premiums paid in. For most people, buying term and investing the difference outperforms either permanent option on pure accumulation — permanent insurance earns its premium in a narrower set of cases: estate liquidity, a business buy-sell agreement, a special-needs dependent, or a high earner who has already maxed out every other tax-advantaged account. This episode walks through the mechanics, the trade-offs, and where each type actually fits.

What life insurance actually is

Strip away the marketing and life insurance is a simple trade: you pay an insurer a premium, and in exchange it promises to pay a lump sum — the death benefit — to whoever you name, if you die while the policy is in force. Everything past that — cash value, dividends, indexing — is a variation bolted onto that one core promise.

Policies split along one central line. Term life covers you for a fixed period — 10, 20, or 30 years — and pays nothing if you outlive the term; it's pure, temporary protection, priced accordingly. Permanent life is designed to stay in force for your entire life (or to a stated age, commonly 100 or 121) and bundles that lifetime death benefit with a savings component called cash value — money that accumulates inside the policy, which you can borrow against or withdraw while alive. Whole life and indexed universal life (IUL) — the two types this episode focuses on — are both permanent, but credit that cash value in structurally different ways covered in detail below.

How the insurer actually prices that promise

Two different mechanisms sit behind every policy — one prices the death benefit itself, a separate one funds whatever cash value comes with it.

INSURES

Pooled mortality risk

The insurer pools premiums across a large group of policyholders and prices each policy using actuarial mortality tables — the statistical odds that someone your age, sex, and health status dies in a given year. Premiums from the (large majority) who don't die in any given year fund the death benefits paid to the (small minority) who do. That pooling is the entire value being sold — no individual saver can replicate "if I die this year, my family instantly gets $500,000" on their own; a large enough risk pool, priced correctly, can guarantee it.

GROWS

A separate savings bucket, only on permanent policies

On a permanent policy, part of each premium beyond the pure cost of the insurance itself is set aside and invested by the carrier, building the cash value covered in detail in the next two sections. Term life has none of this — every dollar of a term premium pays for the mortality pooling above, which is exactly why term is so much cheaper per dollar of death benefit.

The mortality pooling is the genuine insurance value here — a real, useful service most people need for at least part of their life. The rest of this episode is about what it costs to bundle a savings account on top of it, and how to tell whether a specific policy is pricing that bundle fairly.

Worth a look, if —

you have people who depend on your income, for a defined stretch of years

Someone with a mortgage, young kids, or a spouse relying on their paycheck has a genuine, time-limited need: if they die, the people left behind need money now, not decades from now. Term life prices that need directly and cheaply — this is the case almost every financial planner agrees on.

Worth real scrutiny, if —

you're being sold cash value as a retirement or investment strategy

Permanent insurance can make sense for narrow, specific reasons covered later in this episode — but "it builds cash value" and "it's better than a 401(k)" are sales pitches, not neutral facts. The math in this episode's later sections is what lets you tell the difference for your own situation.

On cost: a healthy 40-year-old can typically buy $500,000 of 20-year level term for somewhere in the neighborhood of $25–$40 a month. The same person buying $500,000 of permanent coverage — whole life or IUL — should expect a premium several times higher, commonly $300–$600+ a month, because a large share of that premium is funding the cash-value bucket, not just the mortality cost. The next sections work through exactly what that extra premium is buying, and on what terms.

Before the deep dive

Comparing the main types

"Life insurance" is an umbrella term covering several structurally different products — the right starting question isn't which one credits the best rate, it's whether you need coverage for a defined period or for life, and how much control you want over how the cash value grows. Five types cover most of what's sold today; the table below lines them up before this episode narrows in on two.

Why this episode goes deep on two types

Whole life and IUL, and why they get the rest of this episode

Whole life and IUL aren't a niche corner of the market — together they're the majority of it. Insurers sold $6.4 billion of whole life and $4.5 billion of IUL in new annualized premium in 2025, a combined 62% of the entire $17.5 billion U.S. individual life insurance market, per LIMRA's final 2025 sales report — both new sales records, and IUL's fifth consecutive year of double-digit growth. Term, despite being the type most planners recommend first, was a distant third at 17% ($3.1B); variable universal life held 15% ($2.6B); fixed universal life trailed at 6% ($985M).

The reason these two draw that much of the market — and that much of this episode — is the same reason FIAs dominate the annuity side of this series: they're the two permanent types built to answer "what if the market crashes right when I need this money," while still offering more upside potential than a plain guaranteed rate. Whole life answers that with contractual guarantees; IUL answers it with a market-linked floor. Both get sold hard on that reassurance, and both hide real complexity behind it.

They're also the two types whose actual return is hardest to judge from a sales illustration. A term quote is one number — a monthly premium, full stop. A whole life or IUL illustration runs 20+ pages, mixes a guaranteed column with a non-guaranteed projected column, and depends on dividend scales or cap rates that can — and do — change after you buy. That's the gap the rest of this episode closes, starting with how each one's cash value actually accumulates.

The older, more contractual of the two

How whole life actually credits cash value

A whole life policy runs on two layers stacked together: a contractually guaranteed cash-value schedule, published in your policy at issue, and — on a participating policy sold by a mutual insurer — a non-guaranteed annual dividend layered on top if the company's actual mortality, expense, and investment experience beats what it assumed when it priced the policy.

What's guaranteed: the base cash value

A schedule fixed at issue, never reduced

Every whole life contract publishes a year-by-year minimum guaranteed cash value table at issue. It typically starts near $0 in year one or two — most of an early premium pays for underwriting cost, commission, and reserves — then climbs steadily. This number can't be cut, even if the insurer's results are poor.

What's not: the dividend

Declared annually, and can rise, fall, or stop

The dividend is the difference between the insurer's actual results and its guaranteed pricing assumptions, distributed only on participating policies from mutual insurers. It has no contractual floor at all — a company can declare $0 in a bad year — but the major mutuals have long, if imperfect, track records: MassMutual, New York Life, Guardian, Penn Mutual, and Northwestern Mutual have each paid a dividend every year for over a century.

2026 declared dividend interest rates — the input the insurer uses to calculate that year's dividend, not your policy's actual return — run from Northwestern Mutual's 5.75% up to MassMutual's 6.60%, with New York Life (6.40%), Guardian (6.25%), and Penn Mutual (6.00%) in between; all five raised their rate for a second consecutive year in 2026 as bond yields worked into their long-duration portfolios. That headline rate is not what your cash value actually earns — after mortality and expense charges are netted out of the formula, independent long-run analyses put the real internal rate of return on whole life cash value closer to 4%–5% a year, and that's before accounting for how little cash value exists in the first several years relative to premiums paid.

PUA

Paid-up additions — where most of the real growth comes from

Most whole life owners take their dividend not as cash, but to buy paid-up additions (PUAs) — small increments of fully-paid-up additional whole life, layered onto the base policy. Each PUA has its own (smaller) cash value and generates its own dividend the following year, compounding the effect over decades. This is the mechanism behind "the earlier dividends buy more insurance, which buys more dividends" — the single biggest lever a policyholder actually controls, often via an explicit PUA rider that lets extra premium flow straight into PUAs rather than the base policy.

PAR

Participating vs. non-participating

Only participating policies — almost always from mutual insurers, owned by their policyholders rather than shareholders — pay dividends at all. Non-participating whole life, sold by stock-owned carriers, credits only the guaranteed schedule: lower premiums, but no dividend upside and a lower long-term cash-value ceiling.

RECOGNITION

Direct vs. non-direct recognition

If you take a policy loan against your cash value (mechanics in the surrender-and-loans section below), a direct recognition insurer credits the borrowed portion at a different (often lower) dividend rate while the loan is outstanding. A non-direct recognition insurer keeps crediting the full dividend on the entire cash value regardless of any loan balance — a meaningful difference for anyone planning to borrow against the policy heavily, and worth asking about explicitly before buying.

The mechanism behind the number on your statement

Indexing, participation, and the price of the floor

An IUL never actually invests in the index it tracks. The insurer allocates the cash-value portion of your premium (net of cost-of-insurance and expense charges, covered next) toward call options on an index like the S&P 500, using the interest that a conservative bond portfolio would otherwise have earned to pay for those options. What lands on your statement is whatever that options strategy pays out, run through a cap or a participation rate.

The same pair of concepts from the annuity side of this series governs IUL too — a floor and a cap — existing for the identical reason: one is the price of the other.

What you get: the floor

A guaranteed minimum credit, almost always 0%

Whatever the index does in a given period, your indexed credit can't fall below the stated floor. A down year for the S&P 500 — 2022's −18.11%, for instance — credits exactly 0% on the indexed portion, never a loss. This is the headline pitch behind IUL, and it's real: it protects the credited return, though not the account from the separate charges covered in the next section.

What it costs: the cap

A ceiling on how much of a good year you keep

Current S&P 500 point-to-point cap rates on most new-issue IUL policies run roughly 8%–12% in 2026 — down from the 12%–13% range common a decade ago, as carriers' options-hedging costs have risen. A year the index gains 20% might still credit only 9%. Carriers can also lower the cap on an in-force policy going forward — it's a "current," not guaranteed, rate on almost every product.

CAP

A ceiling on the gain

The most the policy will credit in one period, however far the index rises — the most common lever, typically applied to annual point-to-point S&P 500 crediting.

PAR

A fraction of the gain, sometimes uncapped

A participation rate credits a percentage of the index's move instead of, or alongside, a cap. Some "high-par" strategies advertise 150%–200% participation with no cap at all — the same catch as on the annuity side applies here: an uncapped, high-participation strategy is almost never run on the plain S&P 500. It typically tracks a proprietary, volatility-controlled index engineered to move less than the raw market, so 200% of a muted index can still credit less than 100% of the real S&P 500 would have.

FLOOR

Almost always 0%, on the indexed portion only

The floor protects the index-linked credit from a negative index year — it does not protect the account from the cost-of-insurance and expense charges covered next, which are deducted whether the index moved up, down, or flat. A policy can floor at exactly 0% on indexing and still lose real cash value in dollar terms that same year.

Whichever lever a specific IUL uses, the floor still applies underneath it, and the cap or participation rate is still how the insurer funds that floor — same trade as the FIA side of this series, applied here to a policy that also has to fund a death benefit and its own insurance charges out of the same premium dollar.

What comes out of the account before anything gets credited

Cost of insurance, and the fee that grows every year

Every permanent policy — whole life and IUL alike — has to pay for the underlying mortality risk somehow. On whole life, that cost is baked into the guaranteed premium and the guaranteed cash-value schedule, invisible to the owner as a separate line item. On IUL, it's usually an explicit, disclosed monthly deduction called the cost of insurance (COI) — and it's the single most important number on an IUL illustration that most buyers never look at closely.

COI

Priced to your current age, every single month

Unlike level term (which locks one rate for the whole term), IUL's cost of insurance is typically annually renewable — repriced to your attained age each year, following the same shape as any mortality table: low in your 30s and 40s, rising steadily, and accelerating sharply past 60 and 70. The dollar amount deducted from your cash value for pure insurance cost can be many times higher at 70 than it was at 40, for the identical death benefit.

SQUEEZE

Why underfunded IUL policies can implode late in life

Early on, a healthy premium easily covers a small COI charge with plenty left over to build cash value. Decades later, a rising COI can consume most or all of an unchanged premium — and if cash value is thin, the insurer starts drawing down cash value itself to cover the shortfall, right at the point in life the policy was supposed to be most secure. This dynamic — not a market crash — is the leading cause of IUL policies lapsing (running out of cash value and coverage) in a policyholder's 70s or 80s, often after decades of premium payments.

ADMIN

Policy and rider fees, on both types

Both whole life and IUL layer on a flat monthly administrative charge (commonly in the $5–$15/month range) and, if elected, optional riders — a long-term-care or chronic-illness accelerated benefit, a waiver of premium on disability, a guaranteed insurability option — each carrying its own additional cost.

LOAD

Premium expense loads, heaviest in the early years

A percentage of each premium dollar — often front-loaded, higher in years one and two than afterward — is deducted before anything is credited to cash value, covering underwriting cost and the large first-year commission covered in the "who pays whom" section below. This is the single biggest reason cash value starts near $0 regardless of policy type.

What it costs to change your mind, or borrow against it

Surrender charges, policy loans, and lapse risk

Both whole life and IUL carry a surrender-charge period — commonly 10 to 15 years, sometimes longer on IUL — during which fully cashing out the policy triggers a declining penalty on top of however little cash value has accumulated. Most owners never surrender, though; the more common way people actually use permanent cash value while alive is a policy loan.

LOAN

Borrowing from the insurer, collateralized by your own cash value

A policy loan isn't a withdrawal — it's a loan from the insurer, secured by your cash value, typically available with no underwriting and no fixed repayment schedule. The insurer charges loan interest (commonly 5%–8%, fixed or variable by policy), and on a non-direct-recognition whole life policy or most IUL contracts, your full cash value keeps earning its credited rate even while a portion is borrowed against — the spread between what you're credited and what you're charged is the real cost of the loan.

UNPAID

What happens if the loan is never repaid

Unpaid loan interest capitalizes — it's added to the loan balance and starts accruing interest itself. If the total loan balance ever grows to exceed the cash value, the policy lapses, and any outstanding loan balance above what you originally paid in (your "basis") becomes taxable income in the year it lapses — a genuinely unpleasant surprise, since it can arrive as a tax bill with no cash left in the policy to pay it.

GUARDS

Why IUL is more exposed to lapse than whole life

Whole life's guaranteed cash-value schedule and level, guaranteed-forever premium make an unexpected lapse from underfunding unusual as long as premiums are paid on time. IUL's combination of a flexible, often "target" premium, an index credit that can floor at 0% in consecutive weak years, and a cost of insurance that rises with age makes it structurally more vulnerable to the squeeze described in the previous section — a policy funded to the illustrated, non-guaranteed column, in a below-average market stretch, can require a much larger premium increase later just to stay in force.

Most contracts also allow limited partial withdrawals from cash value (rather than a loan) up to the amount of premiums paid in, generally income-tax-free as a return of basis — beyond that, withdrawn gain is taxable. One more constraint governs how much can be paid into either type without changing its tax treatment: the federal 7-pay test. Overfund a permanent policy too aggressively in its first seven years and it becomes a Modified Endowment Contract (MEC) — the death benefit stays income-tax-free, but loans and withdrawals during your lifetime switch to gains-first tax treatment, plus a 10% penalty before age 59½, permanently, even if funding is later reduced.

Before you sign anything

Reading a policy's own illustration

Every whole life and IUL sale comes with a lengthy numeric illustration — the insurer's own projection of how the policy performs. It's a genuinely useful document, and also the easiest place for an optimistic sale to hide. Five things worth checking, every time.

1. Are you looking at the guaranteed column, or only the projected one?

Every illustration must show a guaranteed column — using the policy's contractual worst case (minimum dividend on whole life, current cap held at the guaranteed floor rate on IUL) — alongside a non-guaranteed "current" or "illustrated" column using today's dividend scale or cap rate held flat for decades. A policy that looks fine on the guaranteed column and merely good on the current column is a much safer bet than one that only looks solvent on the optimistic column.

2. Does it stay in force to age 100, or age 121?

Look for the "age insurance lapses" line under the guaranteed column specifically. A policy projected to run out of cash value and lapse coverage at, say, age 85 under guaranteed assumptions is a very different product than one solvent to 121 even in the worst case.

3. Is this a "vanishing premium" pitch?

Some whole life illustrations show premiums "vanishing" after a stated number of years — dividends alone covering the cost from then on. That projection depends entirely on the non-guaranteed dividend scale holding; if actual dividends come in lower, the premium doesn't vanish on schedule, and the owner is asked to resume paying, often after being told they were done.

4. On an IUL specifically — what cap, participation rate, and COI scale is it illustrated at?

Ask directly whether the illustrated cap or participation rate is today's actual current rate or an assumed average, and whether the cost-of-insurance schedule shown is guaranteed maximum or current. Regulators (NAIC's AG 49-B, effective 2023) have tightened how aggressively IUL illustrations can project historical index performance, but a compliant illustration can still be optimistic within the rules.

5. What happens on a 1035 exchange or replacement?

If an agent is proposing you replace an existing permanent policy with a new one, ask directly what surrender charges or lost cash value you'd forfeit on the old policy, and whether the new policy restarts a fresh surrender-charge and commission clock. Replacement can occasionally make sense — it can also mean paying two policies' worth of front-loaded costs for one policy's worth of coverage.

Common pitches, and the mechanics underneath them

What the sales pitch highlights — and what it costs

Permanent life insurance is sold with some genuinely appealing phrases. Each one is built on a real mechanic covered above — the reality is rarely the outright falsehood a skeptic might expect, but it's almost always a narrower, more conditional truth than the pitch implies.

"Tax-free retirement income, with no market risk."
Reality

Policy loans are genuinely not taxed as income when structured and repaid correctly — that part is true. But it's a loan, not income: it accrues interest, reduces the death benefit if unpaid, and can trigger a large taxable event on lapse if the loan balance ever outgrows the cash value, as covered in the surrender-and-loans section above. It's tax-advantaged access to your own money, collateralized against your own policy — not free income from nowhere.

"The market can't crash your policy — you'll never lose money."
Reality

True for the indexed credit itself, on IUL's 0% floor. False for the account overall: the cost of insurance and administrative fees are deducted from cash value every month regardless of what the index does, so a policy can post a real dollar loss in a year the index floored at exactly 0% — the same asymmetry covered in the cost-of-insurance section above.

"Infinite banking" — become your own bank by borrowing against your policy.
Reality

The mechanics are real — a whole life policy loan is genuinely available with no underwriting. What the pitch tends to skip: you're paying loan interest to the insurer the whole time, cash value takes years to build to a meaningful borrowable amount in the first place, and "infinite" leverage still runs on a finite balance that can be exhausted by unpaid interest, as covered above.

"Your cash value is guaranteed to grow every single year."
Reality

True for whole life's guaranteed base schedule and IUL's 0% index floor — both can't go backward from index or dividend movement alone. Not true net of fees: cost of insurance, admin charges, and (on whole life) a dividend that can be reduced or suspended can all shrink net cash value in a given year, even while the guaranteed or floored piece technically held.

"This will outperform your 401(k) or Roth IRA."
Reality

Over long horizons, neither whole life's ~4%–5% net return nor IUL's capped, fee-drag index return has historically kept pace with a diversified stock portfolio inside a 401(k) or Roth — the "where it fits" section below works through why the ordering usually runs the other direction, tax-advantaged retirement accounts first.

"Premiums vanish after a few years — this eventually pays for itself."
Reality

Possible, but entirely dependent on the non-guaranteed dividend or cap-rate column continuing to perform at the illustrated rate for decades, as covered in the illustration-reading section above. It is a projection, not a contractual promise — and it's routinely off in practice.

The worked example

What this is really worth, over 20 years

Take a hypothetical healthy 45-year-old with a $6,000/year budget for life insurance, wanting a permanent-style death benefit. Three honest ways to spend that budget, run out to 20 years: buy term and invest the rest, fund a whole life policy fully, or fund an IUL fully. All figures below are illustrative — built from real 2005–2024 S&P 500 total returns and representative cost structures, not any one carrier's actual rates.

This decade-plus stretch of real S&P 500 returns (2005–2024, including the 2008 crash) was strong enough on average that the term-and-invest path pulls well ahead of both permanent options by year 20 — nearly 2.4× the whole life cash value and 2.4× the IUL cash value, on the same total dollars spent. Both permanent paths take roughly 8–10 years for cash value to merely reach the cumulative premiums paid in — years during which a term buyer's invested difference is already compounding freely. None of this accounts for one thing only the permanent policies (and, for a fraction of the cost, the term policy) actually provide: if the policyholder dies at any point along any of these three paths, the death benefit pays out in full — something the invested balance alone does not replicate unless the term coverage is still in force.

That last sentence is the honest crux of the comparison. "Buy term and invest the difference" wins on pure accumulation in the vast majority of historical stretches — but it only wins on the insurance question if the term policy is still active when death occurs. A 20-year term bought at 45 expires at 65; a permanent policy, by design, doesn't. Whether that difference is worth paying for depends entirely on whether you expect to still need a death benefit past the term's expiration — the question the "where it fits" section below is built around.

Following the money

Who's actually paying whom

Permanent life insurance's high early-year costs aren't mysterious once you see where the money actually goes in year one — a large share of it goes to the person who sold it to you, up front, regardless of how the policy performs afterward.

Policy typeTypical first-year commissionTypical renewal commission
Term30%–80% of first-year premiumOften small or none past year 1–2
Whole life40%–100%+ of first-year premium~2%–10% of premium, often for a set number of years or the life of the policy
IUL / universal life50%–120% of first-year target premium~2%–10% of target premium

On IUL specifically, commission is paid on a "target premium" the carrier sets — not necessarily the full premium you pay — but a carrier routinely pays over 100% of that target amount in year one. A $10,000/year whole life or IUL policy can mean a single first-year commission check well into four figures, which is a large part of why permanent policies carry heavier front-loaded expense charges than term does, and why cash value starts so close to $0.

None of this makes commission inherently wrong — a licensed agent doing real underwriting, paperwork, and ongoing service work is providing a real service, and a heaped commission structure (high in year one, lower afterward) is standard across the industry, not a sign of misconduct on any individual sale. It does mean the person recommending a $500/month IUL over a $40/month term policy has a direct, sizable financial stake in that recommendation — worth factoring in exactly the same way you'd factor in a car salesperson's commission when comparing trim levels.

The personal-finance ordering

Where permanent insurance fits in the bigger picture

For the large majority of people with a life-insurance need, that need is temporary — years until kids are grown, the mortgage is paid off, or savings are sufficient to self-insure — and term is the tool built for exactly that, at a fraction of the cost worked through above. Permanent insurance earns its premium in a narrower set of cases.

Permanent insurance can make sense when —

the need for a death benefit is genuinely permanent, not temporary

An estate large enough to owe estate tax, held inside an irrevocable life insurance trust to provide liquidity without forcing an asset sale; a business buy-sell agreement funding a partner's exit at death; a special-needs dependent who will require support for their entire life; someone who wants to lock in insurability now while healthy, for a need that genuinely won't end. In each of these, "for life" is the actual requirement, not a sales frame.

Usually a mismatch when —

you haven't maxed out tax-advantaged retirement accounts, or need liquidity

A 401(k) match, an HSA, and Roth or traditional IRA room all offer better-understood tax treatment and typically lower costs than cash value inside life insurance for pure accumulation — the true-value section above shows why. Multi-year surrender charges also make permanent insurance a poor fit for money you might need on short notice.

One legitimate edge case worth naming on its own: a high earner who has already maxed every tax-advantaged account available to them — 401(k), backdoor Roth, HSA — and wants one more tax-deferred bucket, with a death benefit attached, can reasonably consider a properly designed, heavily-funded (but non-MEC) permanent policy as a supplemental savings vehicle. It's a genuinely late-stage tool for that specific situation, not a starting point for most savers — the same "late-stage, narrow-purpose" framing this series applies to annuities applies here too.

A practical middle path many planners suggest: buy enough term to cover the full temporary need cheaply, and separately fund a small, deliberately-chosen permanent policy only for whichever narrow, genuinely lifelong purpose from the list above actually applies — rather than one large permanent policy trying to do both jobs at once.

Before you sign

A buyer's checklist

A short list of things worth checking on any permanent policy, before the free-look period ends.

FREE-LOOK

The free-look period

A state-guaranteed window — commonly 10–30 days after receiving the contract, longer in some states for older buyers — to cancel for a full premium refund, no questions asked. Use it: read the actual illustration and contract, not just the sales presentation.

RATING

Carrier financial-strength ratings

A permanent policy is a multi-decade promise — check the insurer's rating from AM Best, Moody's, or S&P before committing years of premium to it. A dividend history spanning a century (several mutual carriers above) is a reasonable, if not contractual, indicator of staying power.

TWO COLUMNS

Guaranteed vs. illustrated, always both

Never evaluate a policy on the non-guaranteed column alone — see the illustration-reading section above for what to check specifically.

MEC

The 7-pay test, if overfunding

If the plan is to fund the policy aggressively for cash accumulation, confirm with the carrier or a fee-only advisor that the funding schedule stays under the 7-pay/MEC limit — see the surrender-and-loans section above for what's at stake if it doesn't.

SECOND OPINION

A fee-only review, separate from the person selling it

Given the commission structure covered above, a second opinion from a fee-only fiduciary advisor — paid a flat fee or hourly rate, not a commission on what you buy — carries a meaningfully different incentive than the agent proposing the sale.

GUARANTY

State guaranty association coverage

If an insurer becomes insolvent, state guaranty associations back life insurance up to state-set limits — commonly $300,000 in death benefit and $100,000 in cash surrender value, higher (up to $500,000) in a few states including New York and Washington. There's no FDIC-style federal backstop.

Plain answers to common questions

Frequently asked, in plain language

Do I actually need life insurance?

If anyone depends on your income or labor — a spouse, kids, a business partner — probably yes, for at least the years that dependence lasts. If no one depends on you financially, the case weakens considerably.

Is whole life or IUL ever a scam?

No — both are real, regulated insurance products, and their guarantees are generally honored. "Expensive and commission-driven for most buyers' actual needs" is a real, serious concern; it isn't the same thing as fraud.

Can I convert term life to permanent later?

Many term policies include a conversion privilege, letting you convert some or all of the coverage to a permanent policy before a stated age or date, without new medical underwriting — worth checking for specifically if there's any chance you'll want permanent coverage later, especially if health might decline.

What happens to cash value when I die?

On most policies, the insurer pays the stated death benefit — the cash value itself is typically absorbed by the insurer, not paid out on top of the death benefit, unless the policy specifically includes a "return of cash value" rider.

Can I borrow against a whole life or IUL policy tax-free?

The loan itself isn't taxable income while the policy stays in force — see the surrender-and-loans section above for the real mechanics and the risk if the loan is never repaid.

Why do I keep hearing "buy term and invest the difference"?

Because, historically, it's usually put more money in the buyer's pocket than a comparably-priced permanent policy — the true-value section above works through a specific 20-year example. It's not universally right for everyone; see "where it fits" above for the cases where permanent insurance still makes sense.

What if the insurance company goes bankrupt?

State guaranty associations back life insurance up to a state-set limit — commonly $300,000 in death benefit, $100,000 in cash value — see the buyer's checklist above.

Reference

Glossary

The terms this episode leans on most, in one place.

Premium
The amount you pay the insurer to buy and maintain the policy.
Death benefit
The lump sum paid to beneficiaries when the insured dies while covered.
Term life
Coverage for a fixed period, with no cash value.
Permanent life
Coverage designed to last a lifetime, bundled with a cash-value savings component.
Cash value
The savings component inside a permanent policy, accessible via loan or withdrawal while alive.
Whole life
Permanent insurance with a guaranteed cash-value schedule, plus a non-guaranteed dividend on participating policies.
IUL
Indexed Universal Life — permanent insurance crediting cash value linked to a market index, bounded by a cap or participation rate and a floor.
Cap rate
The most an IUL will credit in one period, however far the index rises.
Participation rate
The fraction of the index's gain that's credited.
Floor
The guaranteed minimum index credit — almost always 0%.
Dividend
A non-guaranteed annual amount paid on participating whole life when the insurer outperforms its own pricing assumptions.
Paid-up addition (PUA)
A small increment of fully-paid additional whole life, typically purchased with dividends.
Participating / non-participating
Whether a policy pays dividends (participating) or credits only its guaranteed schedule (non-participating).
Direct / non-direct recognition
Whether an outstanding policy loan changes the dividend rate credited on the loaned cash value.
Cost of insurance (COI)
The ongoing charge for the underlying mortality risk, typically rising with age on IUL.
Target premium
A carrier-set premium benchmark used to calculate commissions on universal life and IUL policies.
Policy loan
A loan from the insurer secured by cash value, not a withdrawal.
Lapse
When a policy's cash value (and any loan balance) can no longer support its charges, and coverage ends.
7-pay test / MEC
The federal test limiting how fast a policy can be funded before it becomes a Modified Endowment Contract with less favorable tax treatment on loans and withdrawals.
Illustration
The carrier's own numeric projection for a specific policy — guaranteed and non-guaranteed columns both.
1035 exchange
A tax-free exchange of one life insurance (or annuity) contract for another.
VUL
Variable Universal Life — permanent insurance with cash value invested directly in market subaccounts.
Free-look period
A state-guaranteed window, commonly 10–30 days, to cancel for a full refund after receiving the contract.
