Health & Disability Insurance: Protecting the Paycheck

How health insurance actually charges you, how to pick between a traditional plan and a high-deductible plan with an HSA, the protections you have when bills go wrong, and the coverage working people most often skip: insurance on the paycheck itself. Two calculators use your own numbers.

TL;DR

Insurance is for the bills you can't absorb, and for most working people the two biggest are a major medical event and losing the ability to earn. In health insurance, the number that matters most is the out-of-pocket maximum: the most you'll pay in a year for covered in-network care (no more than $10,600 for one person in 2026). Compare plans by total yearly cost (premiums plus what you'd pay for care, minus any HSA money) across a good year and a bad year, not by premium alone. A high-deductible plan with an HSA often wins, especially when the employer adds to the HSA, but not always. Your future paychecks are likely your largest asset, and just over 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before retirement age. Social Security disability averages about $1,630 a month and is hard to get, so long-term disability insurance that replaces about 60% of pay is the protection most worth checking. Skip narrow add-ons like cancer-only or accident-only policies, and use the savings to fund the emergency fund and HSA instead.

Start here

Insure what you can't absorb

Good insurance follows one rule: pay someone else to carry the risks that would wreck your finances, and carry the small ones yourself. A $300 urgent-care visit is annoying. A $150,000 hospital stay, or five years without a paycheck, can undo decades of saving. Those are what this episode protects.

Risk one

A big medical bill

Serious illness or injury can produce bills in the six figures. Health insurance turns that open-ended risk into a known worst case: your premiums plus your out-of-pocket maximum.

Risk two

Losing the paycheck itself

A 30-year-old earning $70,000 has roughly $2.6 million of paychecks ahead of them before 67, before a single raise. That future income is usually the largest asset a working person has, and disability insurance is what insures it.

Both sit on the Money River's Insurance & Protection thread, and both belong to the Roadmap's "protect" move. Life insurance covers the third big risk: dying while others depend on your income.

The vocabulary, in order

How health insurance actually charges you

Every plan charges you in two ways: a premium you pay every month whether or not you see a doctor, and cost sharing you pay when you get care. Cost sharing works in three stages as your bills for the year add up.

ALWAYS

Premium

The monthly price of having the plan. At work it's usually taken from your paycheck before tax.

STAGE 1

Deductible

You pay the full negotiated price for most care until you've spent this much in the year. Preventive care is usually covered in full, even before it.

STAGE 2

Coinsurance / copays

After the deductible, you share costs: a percentage (often 20%) or a flat copay per visit or prescription.

STAGE 3

Out-of-pocket maximum

Once your cost sharing hits this cap, the plan pays 100% of covered in-network care for the rest of the year.

= your worst case

For 2026, the out-of-pocket maximum on almost any plan can't exceed $10,600 for one person or $21,200 for a family. Employer plans are usually lower: in KFF's 2025 survey, the average single deductible at work was $1,886, and 72% of covered workers had an out-of-pocket maximum above $3,000. Premiums are large but mostly hidden: the average family plan cost $26,993 in 2025, of which workers paid $6,850 and employers the rest.

The out-of-pocket maximum applies only to covered, in-network care. Out-of-network bills on a PPO often have a separate, higher cap, and on an HMO or EPO usually aren't covered at all outside emergencies.

The decision most people make once a year

Choosing a plan: the real math

Picking the plan with the lowest premium, or the lowest deductible, is how most people choose. The better comparison is total yearly cost: premiums, plus what you'd pay for care, minus any money your employer puts in your HSA and the taxes you save putting your own money in. Simplified: every dollar of care counts toward the deductible, then coinsurance applies until the out-of-pocket maximum; copays and out-of-network care are ignored. "Medical bills" means the plan's negotiated price of your in-network care for the year. Tax saved on HSA deposits ≈ your deposit × (your tax bracket + 7.65% payroll tax when made through payroll), e.g. $1,500 × 30% = $450.

At the defaults, the high-deductible plan is cheaper by about $1,850 in a healthy year and stays slightly cheaper up to about $16,000 of care. In a very bad year it costs about $1,150 more, because its out-of-pocket maximum is higher. Three things usually decide it:

  • Employer HSA money is a straight discount on Plan B. Some employers contribute $1,000 or more.
  • Can you cover the deductible from savings? If a $3,300 bill would go on a credit card, the lower-deductible plan may be safer until your emergency fund is built.
  • Known big expenses (a planned surgery, a pregnancy, an ongoing condition with expensive prescriptions) favor the plan with the lower out-of-pocket maximum. Check that your doctors and drugs are covered either way.
The HSA side of the choice. An HDHP is the only way to open an HSA, the account that's tax-free going in, growing and coming out for medical costs. Unspent money rolls over forever and can be invested. Which Account? covers the 2026 limits ($4,400 / $8,750) and the new rules that make bronze and catastrophic marketplace plans HSA-eligible.

Getting covered

Where coverage comes from: work, marketplace, COBRA

WORK

Employer plans

Most working-age Americans are covered at work. You choose during open enrollment (usually in the fall) or within 30 days of a life event like marriage, a birth or a new job. Premiums come out of your paycheck before tax.

ACA

The marketplace (HealthCare.gov or your state's site)

For the self-employed, early retirees and anyone without a work plan. Plans come in metal tiers (bronze, silver, gold, platinum) that trade premiums against cost sharing, and no one can be turned down or charged more for a pre-existing condition. For 2027 coverage, open enrollment runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027 in most states (sign up by December 15 for a January 1 start). A job loss, move, marriage or birth opens a 60-day special enrollment window.

2026

Subsidies shrank this year

The extra premium tax credits from 2021–2025 expired at the end of 2025. For 2026, people earning above 400% of the poverty line lost subsidies entirely, and KFF estimates average marketplace payments rose 58% and average deductibles 37%. If you're planning an early retirement, price marketplace coverage carefully. Income from IRA withdrawals and Roth conversions counts toward subsidy eligibility.

COBRA

Keeping a work plan after leaving

COBRA lets you stay on your former employer's plan, usually for up to 18 months, but you pay the full premium plus up to 2%. Compare it with a marketplace plan, especially if your income will be low that year.

65

Medicare

At 65, most people move to Medicare, with its own enrollment clock and penalties. Social Security & Medicare in Depth covers it.

When bills go wrong

Protections when the bill goes wrong

ProtectionWhat it does
No Surprises ActBans most surprise out-of-network bills for emergency care, for out-of-network doctors at in-network hospitals, and for air ambulances. You pay only your in-network cost sharing. Ground ambulances aren't covered. If you're uninsured or paying cash, you're entitled to a good-faith estimate and can dispute a bill that's $400 or more above it.
Preventive careMost plans must cover recommended screenings, vaccines and checkups with no cost sharing, even before the deductible.
AppealsYou can appeal a denied claim with the plan, then ask for an independent external review. Many denials are reversed on appeal.
Itemized bills and errorsAsk for an itemized bill and the plan's explanation of benefits. Compare them, and question duplicate or unfamiliar charges before paying.
Medical debt and creditThe three big credit bureaus don't report paid medical collections or unpaid ones under $500, and they wait a year before reporting the rest. A federal rule to remove all medical debt was struck down in 2025, but 15 states ban medical debt on credit reports.
Financial assistanceNonprofit hospitals must have charity-care policies. Ask for the application, and ask for a payment plan (often interest-free) before using a credit card.

The protection people most often skip

Disability insurance: protecting the paycheck

Social Security estimates that just over 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before reaching retirement age. Most long disabilities come from illness (cancer, heart disease, back and joint problems, mental health), not workplace accidents, so workers' comp usually doesn't apply.

SSDI

Social Security disability is a thin, slow floor

You must be unable to do substantial work of any kind (in 2026, earning under about $1,690 a month), for at least a year. Benefits start after a five-month wait, applications are often denied at first, and the average payment is about $1,630 a month in 2026.

GROUP

Long-term disability through work

Many employers offer it, often cheaply or free. A typical policy replaces about 60% of base salary (bonuses and commissions often excluded), up to a monthly cap, after a 90-day waiting period, and pays to 65 or 67. If your employer pays the premium, the benefits are taxable. If you pay with after-tax dollars, they're tax-free. Benefits are usually reduced by any SSDI you receive.

OWN

Individual policies

Worth considering if you have no group coverage, earn well above the group cap, or have specialized skills. Look for an own-occupation definition (pays if you can't do your job, even if you could do another), a benefit to age 65 or 67, and a cost-of-living rider. A strong policy often costs about 1%–3% of income.

SHORT

Short-term disability

Covers the first weeks or months, including recovery after childbirth. A handful of states run their own programs, and your emergency fund does the same job for shorter gaps.

If you couldn't work for a year: your monthly income gap
Take-home now assumes about 25% of pay goes to income and payroll taxes; taxable disability benefits assume about 15% (no payroll tax). SSDI shown only if you have no coverage, at the 2026 average of $1,630 after a five-month wait, if approved. "Paychecks at stake" is salary × years to 67 with no raises.

Keep it simple

Coverage worth having, and coverage to skip

Usually worth having

Broad coverage for big losses

Comprehensive health insurance; long-term disability while you work; term life if anyone depends on your income; auto and home or renters insurance with solid liability limits; an umbrella policy once you have real assets to protect.

Usually skip

Narrow policies for specific events

Cancer-only, accident-only and hospital-indemnity add-ons, credit-card and loan "protection," extended warranties, and flight insurance. They pay only in narrow cases, often return a small share of premiums as benefits, and duplicate what broad coverage and an emergency fund already do.

A useful test: if you could cover the loss from your emergency fund, don't insure it; if you couldn't, insure it broadly. Raising deductibles on car and home insurance and banking the premium savings is often one of the easiest wins in a budget.

Do this

An open-enrollment checklist

Run both plans through the calculator

Use this year's actual premiums, deductibles, out-of-pocket maximums and employer HSA contribution from your benefits guide.

Check your doctors and prescriptions

Confirm they're in network and on the drug list (formulary) for the plan you pick.

Make sure the deductible fits your cash

Your emergency fund should cover at least the deductible, ideally the out-of-pocket maximum.

Choosing an HDHP? Set up HSA payroll deposits

Payroll deposits skip payroll tax as well as income tax. Invest what you won't need this year.

Look for long-term disability

Enroll if it's offered. Note the percentage, the monthly cap, the waiting period and who pays the premium. Paying it yourself makes future benefits tax-free.

Skip the narrow add-ons

Accident, cancer and hospital-indemnity riders rarely beat putting the same money in an HSA or emergency fund.

Consider FSAs

A health FSA (up to $3,400 in 2026) if you're not in an HSA plan, and a dependent-care FSA (up to $7,500 per household) if you pay for child care.

FAQ

Frequently asked, in plain language

Is a high-deductible plan a bad idea if I have kids?

Not necessarily. Run the numbers with the family deductible and out-of-pocket maximum, plus the family HSA limit ($8,750 in 2026). Families with predictable, modest costs often come out ahead; families expecting big bills may not. Most preventive care for kids is covered either way.

My spouse and I both have work plans. Should we each take our own?

Compare three options: each on your own plan, everyone on plan A, everyone on plan B. Watch for "spousal surcharges" some employers add when a spouse could get coverage elsewhere.

How much disability coverage do I actually need?

Enough that your essential expenses are covered, with no paycheck, for as long as a disability could last. Group coverage at 60% often leaves a gap for higher earners because of the monthly cap and because bonuses aren't covered. That's what individual policies fill.

I'm self-employed. Where do I start?

A marketplace plan (or an HDHP with an HSA), an individual disability policy, and an emergency fund large enough for a gap in income. Self-employed health premiums are generally deductible on your return.

I'm retiring before 65. What bridges the gap?

COBRA for up to 18 months, a spouse's plan, or a marketplace plan. With subsidies based on income, the taxable income you draw from savings in those years matters. See Taxes 101, and Turning Savings into a Paycheck for planning around it.

Is dental and vision insurance worth it?

Often roughly break-even: premiums close to what routine care costs, with low annual limits. Useful if an employer subsidizes it or you expect major dental work. Otherwise, paying cash from an HSA is fine.

Reference

Glossary

Premium
What you pay to have the plan, whether or not you use it.
Deductible
What you pay for covered care each year before the plan starts sharing costs.
Coinsurance
Your percentage share of costs after the deductible.
Copay
A flat fee for a visit or prescription.
Out-of-pocket maximum
The most you'll pay in a year for covered in-network care; the plan pays 100% after that.
Network
The doctors and hospitals that have agreed prices with your plan.
HDHP
High-deductible health plan; required to contribute to an HSA.
HSA
Health savings account: tax-free in, growing and out for medical costs.
FSA
Flexible spending account: pre-tax money for health or dependent care, mostly use-it-or-lose-it.
Formulary
A plan's list of covered prescription drugs.
Explanation of benefits (EOB)
The plan's statement of what a provider billed, what it paid and what you owe. Not a bill.
COBRA
The right to keep a former employer's plan temporarily, at full cost.
Special enrollment period
A window after a life event to change or buy coverage outside open enrollment.
Elimination period
The waiting time after a disability starts before benefits begin.
Own-occupation
A disability definition that pays if you can't do your own job.
Any-occupation
A stricter definition that pays only if you can't do any job suited to your training.
SSDI
Social Security Disability Insurance, for workers unable to do substantial work for at least a year.
Benefit cap
The maximum monthly amount a disability policy will pay, regardless of salary.
